Ten things left my closet in the last six years. The first one was a black turtleneck. I did not give it away because it was worn out. I gave it away because of what it did under my chin.

I am sixty-eight. I live in a wooden house in Kyoto, in the neighborhood where I was born, and I am not thin, and I am not rich. What changed my closet was not a diet and it was not money. It was one rule about what sits next to my face.

Black adds years in a photograph. You were told black is safe, black is elegant, black goes with everything. It does go with everything. It also takes the light out of your face, and from across a room, in a photograph, that is the only thing anybody sees.

There are ten of these. Most cost me nothing to lose. The last one, almost nobody in America knows it exists. By the end you will know what to take out of your closet tonight, with things you already own. Not one thing to buy. Well. Two things. And one of them is two dollars.

My mother was Fumiko Arai. For forty years she dressed brides and geiko in Gion, in kimono. She tied an obi on women who were nervous, on the morning of their wedding, with their mother standing behind her. She knew where the light falls on a face before she knew anything else about a woman.

I also lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002, because my husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. I did it the American way too. I had the big closet, the softener, the black turtleneck, the bag with everything in it. So I am not laughing at anybody. I bought all ten of them myself.

I see videos now that tell you to empty your closet and buy five perfect pieces. My mother never said that. She said a closet is not a collection. It is a conversation between your face and the fabric, and most women are having it with the wrong half of the room.

Number one. The black turtleneck.

I bought mine in Sunnyvale in 1998, for about thirty dollars. It was soft, it was thin, it went under everything. I wore it for twenty years.

In 2019 I went to Hanamikoji, in Gion, to see a friend of my mother's. She is a tiny woman, ninety years then, and she asked for a photograph with me in front of the wooden door. When she sent it, I looked at it for a long time. I looked tired. Not sad. Tired. And I had slept well.

Here is why. Black under the chin does not add a shadow. It removes one. The light comes down, it hits the black, and it stops. Nothing bounces back up, so the jaw goes flat, the neck goes dark, and the skin above it looks grey, because the only thing reflecting into it is black.

My mother would hold a colour under a bride's chin and look at the neck, not the face. She said the neck tells the truth.

[emphatically] So this one left. I gave it to the clothing box at Doshisha University, where my granddaughter Yui studies, in the spring of 2020.

What I wear instead is a navy crew neck. Navy is not black. It has blue in it, and blue is a light colour pretending to be dark. It bounces a little. I paid eighteen dollars on Kawaramachi, and I have three of them now, because I am not a saint.

Tonight, before you sleep. Take your blackest thing and your navy thing to the window. Grey sky is better than sun. Hold the black under your chin and look at the space under your eyes. Then the navy. The shadow changes. Your face did not.

Number two. The leopard blouse.

My friend Carol Whitfield gave it to me in 2002, the year I left California and came home. She is seventy now, she lives in Sacramento, she calls me every Sunday night, my Monday morning. The blouse was bright, it was polyester, it had a little shimmer. Carol said, Michiko, this is you.

I put it on once. It made a sound when I moved. Polyester is plastic, and plastic does not breathe, so under the arms it holds everything the day gives it, and by four in the afternoon it was speaking for me.

I did not give this one to a box. I put it in a paper box with a note, and the box is on the top shelf of the tansu, and it is still there. That is a longer story, and it belongs to another Sunday.

[emphatically] Here is the rule I took from it. A fabric that does not breathe does not stay clean. It only hides. And a thing that hides is working all day, quietly, behind you.

Number three. The fleece jacket.

I bought a pale grey fleece in 2011, near the station, for forty dollars. It was warm, it weighed nothing, everybody had one. In February our house is thirty-five degrees inside, because it is wood and it is thin, and that fleece kept me alive.

But after two wearings the collar had a smell. Not dirty. Warm. Like a closed room. I washed it, and a day later the collar had it again.

I took it to Sachiko-san, who runs the laundry on the corner. She does not read the label. She smells the collar and tells you how many days you wore it. She smelled mine and said, three days. I said, one day. She said, three days.

Fleece is a net of plastic fibres. The oil from your neck sits in that net, and washing it with a softener is like putting perfume on a sponge. My mother never used a softener. She used sun and air, and she hung wool by the window for one hour, not more.

The jacket went into the donation box at Doshisha in 2023. What I wear in that cold house now is a lambswool cardigan, forty dollars, which I air on the veranda in the morning and wash twice a winter.

Tonight, smell the collar of your fleece, not the front. The collar is where the truth is.

Now, before number four, do something with me. It takes one minute, and you need a window, not a mirror.

Hold a black thing under your chin. Look at the skin under your eyes, in the hollow. Now change it for navy, or grey-blue, or a soft green. Look at the same place.

[emphatically] The hollow under the eye is the darkest place on your face. Whatever you wear under the chin either fills it with light or empties it. That is the whole test. That is what my mother did with a hundred brides, and she never once used the word flattering.

Number four. The handbag that weighed two kilos.

I carried a big leather bag for years. Wallet, umbrella, a second pair of glasses, a paperback, a bottle of water, a small towel, because in Kyoto you always have a small towel. I weighed it once at the post office. Two kilos and a little more. On one shoulder, every day, for eleven years.

In 2017 my right shoulder started to sit higher than the left. I saw it in a photograph before I felt it.

Mrs. Kinoshita, across the street, is eighty-one, and she walks ten thousand steps every morning on the Philosopher's Path. She carries a small bag across her body, flat against her, the size of a hand. She walks straight, and she is not a small woman.

I gave my big bag to Naomi, my daughter, in Osaka, in 2018. She now complains about her shoulder. I say nothing, because I am her mother, but I think it loudly.

I bought a small cross-body bag near Sanjo for twenty-five dollars. It holds a wallet, a phone, one pair of glasses, and the small towel. If I need more, I take a tote and leave it in the car.

Tonight, empty your bag on the kitchen table. Everything you have not used in one month goes into a drawer. Then lift the bag with one finger through the strap. If it pulls, it is too heavy.

Number five. The pointed shoes.

I had black pumps with a pointed toe and a three-inch heel. I bought them in 1993, in San Francisco, for a hundred and ten dollars, and I wore them to every wedding for twenty years.

In 2014 I wore them to the wedding of my niece, in Gion, in a hall with a stone floor. By nine in the evening I was in the corridor in my stockings, holding them. My mother's friend saw me and laughed and said, Michiko, you are sixty.

A pointed toe is not a shoe. It is a shape. Your foot is a rectangle and the shoe is a triangle, so the front of your foot lives in the triangle, and the triangle is where you fall.

I gave them away in 2018. What I wear now is a flat leather shoe, round toe, one finger of space at the front. Mrs. Kinoshita has had hers fifteen years and walks ten thousand steps a day in them. She has them resoled at the cobbler in Nishiki market for twenty-two dollars, once every three years.

Tonight, put your shoes on the table and turn them over. If the toe is narrower than your foot, the shoe is not yours.

Number six. The shaping camisole.

I bought a nylon camisole in California in 1999, the kind that pulls everything in. It worked. It also did not let my skin breathe, and Kyoto in August is ninety-five degrees with humidity you can hold in your hand.

In 2016 I was in a restaurant near Kawaramachi with Yui, and I stood up in the middle of the meal, went to the bathroom, took it off, and came back with it in my bag. Yui was twelve. She said, Obaachan, what is that. I said, it is a mistake, and I am fixing it.

Nylon against the skin in the heat is a plastic bag with a knot in it. Your body does what it does, the fabric holds it, and by evening the fabric has a smell that no wash removes, because the smell is in the fibre, not on it.

What I wear instead is a cotton camisole from a shop in Nishiki market, twelve dollars, and in summer a linen blouse over it that does not touch my back.

Tonight, read the label of the thing you wear under everything. If it says nylon and polyester together, it goes in the bag by the door.

Number seven. The pull-on trousers.

For a few years I wore those trousers with the elastic waist and the thin fabric. They looked correct when I was standing at the mirror in the morning. That is the trick of them. They look correct standing.

In 2021 I was waiting for Yui outside the library at Doshisha, and I sat on a bench for twenty minutes, and then I stood up and saw myself in the glass door. The trousers were shining at the knees, and they were holding the shape of the bench.

Thin polyester does not crease. It shines. And it remembers every chair you sat in.

I gave away four pairs. What I wear instead is linen, wide at the leg, with a proper waistband and a zip. Mr. Okada, at the fabric shop in Nishiki, saves me the ends of his linen rolls, and the woman who does alterations above the market makes them for twenty dollars. Linen creases. That is the point. A crease is cloth behaving like cloth.

Tonight, sit down in a chair for two minutes in the trousers you wear most, then stand in front of a mirror and look at your knees. In the light, not in the shadow.

Number eight. The bottle of fabric softener.

In 2002 our things came home from California in a container on a ship, and inside that container was a large bottle of fabric softener. I had used it for nineteen years. I used it here for seventeen more.

Then one winter I opened the oshiire where the towels live, and the whole cupboard smelled like a shop that sells perfume to people who do not like perfume. The towels were clean. They smelled of nothing. They smelled of the softener.

Softener is a coat of wax with a smell in it. It makes a towel feel soft because it fills the loops that are supposed to take the water. So the towel stops drying you.

In 2019 I gave the bottle to the recycling, and I have not bought one since. What I use is white vinegar, two dollars at any supermarket, a quarter of a cup in the rinse. And the sun. My mother dried everything in the morning sun and brought it in by eleven.

Tonight, open your closet and smell the shelf, not the clothes. If the shelf has a smell, the smell is coming from the cloth, and the cloth is holding it.

Number nine. The white shoes with the thick sole.

In 2015 I bought white sneakers with a thick sole, for a trip to Arashiyama with Yui. The shop had only one pair left and it was one size too big, and I bought it anyway, because I am sometimes not a patient woman.

We walked the bamboo path, and I caught the front of the shoe on a stone and went down on one knee, in front of a bus of tourists. Nobody was hurt except my pride, which is the largest organ I have.

A thick sole looks like comfort. It is a platform, and a platform on a stone path is a thing you have to think about with every step. Mrs. Kinoshita feels the ground through a thin leather sole, and that is why she does not fall.

I gave the sneakers away that month. What I wear now is a canvas shoe with a thin sole, thirty dollars, or the leather shoes I told you about, and in the rain, rubber boots that come to the ankle, and I walk slowly, like a woman who knows something.

Tonight, turn your shoe over. If the sole is thicker than your thumb, you are not walking. You are balancing.

Before the last one, I want to ask you something. If this is the kind of thing you want more of, subscribe. Not for me. Yui reads the comments to me with my tea, and when there are many, the tea gets cold, and that is a good afternoon.

Number ten. The nylon half-slip. Nobody in America knows this one.

For years I wore a nylon half-slip under skirts. It is thin, it is beige, it makes a small crackle when you walk, and in winter, when I crossed the tatami, it made a spark at my ankle.

My mother never wore one. She wore a susoyoke, a cotton wrap that ties at the waist, one layer, nothing tight, nothing plastic. I thought it was something from the war. It was not. It was a woman who knew that the layer closest to the body should be cotton, in every season.

In 2022 I bought a cotton susoyoke in a small shop near Kitano Tenmangu, for twelve dollars, and the woman there wrapped it in paper and said, your mother wore these. I said, yes, she did, and I was late.

The difference is not the look. Nobody sees this layer. The difference is that cotton takes the moisture and lets it go, and nylon keeps it, so the skirt sticks to you behind the knee, and you spend the day pulling at it. Pulling at your clothes is what makes a woman look tired, more than any colour.

Tonight, open the drawer where the under-layers live and read the labels. Anything nylon goes. If you want the cotton one, look for a shop that sells kimono things. Or sew one. It is two rectangles and two ties.

Now let me tell you what my mother said, because it is the only rule you need.

She said: if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you. Not the colour, not the price, not the label. The body knows before the mirror does.

I am sixty-eight. I have ten things fewer than I had six years ago, and I have not replaced one of them with the same thing. My closet is quieter now. When I open it in the morning, I do not have to decide anything. I only have to look at what is next to my face.

So tell me in the comments: what colour is closest to your face right now, this minute? Not your favourite colour. The one that is on you. Yui reads them to me, and I read them with my tea.

And next Sunday I will tell you what happened when I took Hiroshi's beige fleece to Sachiko-san, and she smelled the collar and said three days, and he said one. She was right. The rules my mother used so that wool and towels never smell sour. That is the next one.

Thank you for sitting with me. Stand straight.
