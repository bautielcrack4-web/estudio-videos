[emphatically] Seven thousand funerals. Forty years at a desk in San Antonio, Texas. And when I try to remember them, what comes back to me is not the caskets. It is the bills.

I keep one in the drawer of this desk. March of nineteen ninety-nine. The Herrera family, on Zarzamora Street, three blocks from the funeral home where I worked. The mother died on a Tuesday. By Thursday the father owed eleven thousand two hundred dollars. He paid with a cashier's check and did not say a word to me until the parking lot was empty. Then he said, Ernie, did she need all of that?

I have been asked that question maybe four hundred times in forty years. Tonight I am going to answer it the way I should have answered him in that parking lot.

My name is Ernie Navarro. I am sixty-eight. My father, Don Refugio Navarro, opened Funerales Navarro in Analco, in Guadalajara, in nineteen fifty-eight, two blocks from San Sebastián. I started there at thirteen, sweeping the chapel. In two thousand I came to San Antonio and spent twenty-five years on the West Side — Guadalupe Street, Zarzamora, Commerce — burying Mexican families inside a system that was not built for them. Close to seven thousand people. My wife, Rosa, has been a hospice chaplain for thirty-four years, so between us we have been in the room for most of it.

Tonight I am going to give you the nine things families regret paying for. Not the nine most expensive. The nine that, years later, in that parking lot, somebody turns around and asks me about. With the price of each one, and with the paper that says you do not have to pay it, because the Federal Trade Commission makes every funeral home hand you a General Price List in writing before you tell them what you want. Most families never ask. That is the whole business, mijo.

[excited] And there is one more thing, at the end, which is not in any price list. It is in a yellow envelope, written in pencil, by my father, in nineteen ninety-nine. I will show it to you before we finish.

If you are going to be the person making these decisions for somebody you love, subscribe now, while nobody is dying. That is the only good time to learn this. Not at two in the morning in a hospital hallway, not in a front office with a carpet and a folder.

Before the nine, a test. It takes two minutes and you can do it in any funeral home in this country.

Walk in with your coat still on. Before you sit down, before you say the name of your mother, say exactly this sentence. Before we talk about anything, may I have your General Price List?

Then watch what happens. And I mean watch, not listen to.

If she reaches under the counter and hands it to you unasked, you are in a decent place. If she says of course, and then asks whether you would like the casket price list too, remember the name of that funeral home, because you found one.

But if she says, let's first sit down and talk about what kind of service you're thinking about, that is not kindness, that is a script. And if she says the price list is something we go over together at the end, you are being sold to, and the number you agree to was designed for you before you walked in.

[emphatically] The rule is federal. It has been required since nineteen eighty-four. You do not have to buy anything to see it, and you do not have to be next of kin. You can walk in off the street, ask for it, read it, and leave. And if a funeral home refuses, you walk out and you do not go back, and you write one word in the comments so the next family in your city knows which door not to open.

So tell me: yes or no. Did they hand it to you in the first two minutes? One word. I read all of them.

Number one. The airtight casket.

In the Herrera bill there was one line: protective casket, brushed bronze, sealed, gasketed. Two thousand nine hundred and forty dollars. In the same book, on the next page, the plain metal casket that does exactly the same job was nine hundred and eighty. Both of them hold a person. Both of them go into the ground. One of them had the word protective printed on the tag.

Let me be fair to the rubber, because I sold those caskets and I will not pretend I didn't. The gasket does what it says for a while: it keeps water out of the box. What it cannot do is keep air out of what is inside, and that is the part nobody at that table explains, because the word protective was never about your mother. It was about the casket.

The Federal Trade Commission wrote this into the Funeral Rule: a funeral home may not tell you that a casket will preserve a body indefinitely, or that a vault will. It is in the rule. They can sell you the gasket. They cannot promise you the result. And in forty years I never once heard a salesman say indefinitely. They say protective, and they let you finish the sentence in your own head.

Why does it happen? Because the casket is the only object in that room that grief can touch. The family puts a hand on it. And the difference between nine hundred and three thousand dollars is invisible from three feet away, under a light, with flowers on top. That is not an accident.

Here is what changes. The Rule also requires a separate casket price list, and you may take it home. Not look at it there — take it home. And you may buy a casket somewhere else, and the funeral home has to accept it and may not charge a handling fee. That has been the rule since nineteen ninety-four.

Tonight's check. Take out the bill and look for four words: protective, sealed, gasketed, monoseal. If one of those is on the line and nobody can tell you why, that is the two thousand dollars. And if you have not buried anybody yet, write this down: ask for the casket price list, and ask one question. Which of these are you required by law to sell me? The answer is none.

Number two. The vault.

This is the one that makes people angry, because it does not happen at the funeral home. It happens at the cemetery, on a different piece of paper, usually the same afternoon.

In Texas, and in most of this country, the cemetery requires something around the casket before they close the grave, so the ground does not sink in a year and the mowers do not fall into a hole. That is the real reason. It is a maintenance rule, not a law.

Two things can go down there. A grave liner, concrete, open at the bottom, four hundred to eight hundred dollars, and it does what the rule asks. Or a vault, sealed, with a lid, fifteen hundred to four thousand, depending on how many walls they tell you it has.

The Salas family, in two thousand one, at San Fernando Cemetery. The father died in October. They paid two thousand eight hundred and fifty dollars for a double-reinforced sealed vault, because the man at the cemetery said the word required, and nobody asked him to show them where it was written. When I asked for the cemetery's rules in writing, the sentence said a rigid container. A six-hundred-dollar liner is a rigid container.

Why does it happen? Because the funeral home earns on the vault and the cemetery earns on the vault, and the person who should say the cheapest one that meets the rule is the same person selling you the most expensive one. And because it comes at the end of a long day, when the family has already signed nine pieces of paper and would sign a tenth to go home.

Here is what changes. Ask the cemetery, not the funeral home, for the rule in writing. Not what they recommend. The rule. Then ask: what is the least expensive container that meets this rule? They have to tell you, and if they don't, you have it in writing and you can take it to the state board that licenses cemeteries.

Tonight's check. If you own a burial plot, call the cemetery office this week. One question: is a grave liner acceptable for this plot, or does your rule require a vault? Write down the name of the person who answers. Four minutes.

Number three. Embalming.

I want to say this one slowly, because people repeat it wrong in both directions.

Embalming is not required by federal law. It is not required by state law for a funeral with a closed casket. It is not required for cremation. It is not required for burial in the ground. Some states require it in specific situations — commonly a public viewing with an open casket, or transporting a body across state lines on a common carrier. But those are specific situations, and the Funeral Rule makes a funeral home disclose in writing that embalming is not required by law except in those cases.

The Alvarez family, in two thousand and four, on Guadalupe Street. The mother died in a hospital on a Friday. The family chose cremation, no viewing, because the grandchildren were small and the daughter did not want them to remember her that way. On the bill there was nine hundred and seventy-five dollars for embalming, and two hundred and fifty more for preparation for identification. And do you know how the family identified her? From a photograph the hospital had already taken.

Nobody lied to them. That is the part that still bothers me. Nobody said the law requires it. Somebody said, this is our standard preparation, and the family heard, this is what has to happen, and they signed.

Why does it happen? Because embalming is the highest-margin hour in the building. The fluid costs almost nothing, the room is already there, the person doing it is already on the payroll. And it is the one line a family will never question, because questioning it feels like saying you do not want to take care of her.

Here is what changes. One sentence, and it is not rude, not cheap, not disrespectful. We are choosing direct cremation with no viewing. Please take embalming and preparation off the estimate and give me the new total in writing. If they tell you the body cannot be kept without it, ask how many days and what refrigeration costs, because refrigeration is a line item too, usually twenty-five to seventy-five dollars a day.

Tonight's check. If you are planning ahead for yourself, write one line and put it with your will: no embalming unless there is an open casket. One line. It saves your children between seven hundred and twelve hundred dollars and one very bad conversation.

Number four. The package.

This is the big one, and it is the one that sounds like a kindness.

A traditional service, complete, eight thousand nine hundred dollars. That is a real number from a real price list I read in two thousand and eight, and it is not far from the median today. It includes the basic services of the funeral director and staff, the transfer of the remains, the embalming, the other preparation, the use of the facility for the viewing, the use of the facility for the ceremony, the hearse, one limousine, a register book, fifty acknowledgement cards, and a casket I would not put my own mother in.

The Villarreal family, in two thousand and eight, on the north side of San Antonio. A big funeral home, the kind with a fountain in front and a chandelier in the lobby. They were quoted twelve thousand four hundred dollars for a package, and they came to me because a cousin told them to. I did not sell them anything. I took their own price list and itemized the same service with a pen on the back of an envelope. The same casket, three hundred dollars cheaper from a casket company across town. The same hearse. No limousine, because they had four cars in the family. No programs from the funeral home, because Lalo printed them. Seven thousand eight hundred and fifty dollars. Same funeral. Same priest. Same flowers.

Why does it happen? Because a package hides the margin in the three lines nobody questions — the register book, the cards, the preparation. And because a family in the first hour of a death cannot do arithmetic, and everybody in that office knows it.

Here is what changes. You have the right to buy only the items you want. Say it plainly: please price this as separate items from your General Price List, and remove anything we did not ask for. If they tell you the package is cheaper, ask them to show you the arithmetic on paper. Twice in forty years, the package was actually cheaper. Twice, out of hundreds.

Tonight's check. Ask for the printed price list and circle the four things you know you want: transfer, shelter, the service, and the grave. Add them up yourself with a calculator, in the car, before you go back inside.

Number five. The cremation casket and the urn.

Two objects, one line each, and together they are the easiest thousand dollars in the building.

The Funeral Rule requires a funeral home to offer an alternative container for cremation. A rigid box, usually cardboard or plywood, that the crematory can put into the retort. About a hundred and fifty to four hundred dollars. That is what is required. Nothing else is.

But on the estimate it says cremation casket, one thousand two hundred dollars, and it is a beautiful piece of furniture, and it is going to burn. That is the entire transaction. A family buys a wooden casket for a body that will be in it for one hour and then be ash.

And then the urn. Fifty dollars to twelve hundred, and the twelve-hundred-dollar one is not better at being an urn than the fifty-dollar one. It holds the same weight. It is the same size. One of them has a hand-engraved dove.

The Ortiz family, in two thousand and fifteen. The grandmother had told her daughter, years before, that she wanted to be cremated and put in the tin she kept her sewing in. So the daughter brought the tin to the funeral home. And the man at the counter told her they could not use it, that it had to be a cremation urn purchased from the funeral home for reasons of dignity.

That was not true, and it has not been true since nineteen ninety-four. The Funeral Rule says you may provide your own casket or urn, the funeral home must accept it, and they may not charge a fee for accepting it. The daughter called me from the parking lot. I walked in with her. We put the sewing tin on the counter, and I asked him to show me the sentence in the rule that said no. He could not, because there isn't one.

Why does it happen? Because the urn is the last object, and the last object is the one the family takes home. It sits on the mantel for twenty years. So it is sold like jewellery, not like a container.

Here is what changes. Two sentences. We are providing our own container. And: we are providing our own urn. If they push back, ask them to put the refusal in writing on the estimate, with the rule they are citing. I have never seen anybody write that sentence down.

Tonight's check. If cremation is what you want, find your container now. A tin, a box, a shelf in the closet. Decide it while you are calm, because your children will not be calm, and the person behind that counter will be very calm.

Number six. The limousines.

A limousine from a funeral home in San Antonio runs about a hundred and fifty dollars an hour, with a two-hour minimum, and a lot of packages include two of them. That is six hundred dollars before the family has bought a single flower.

The Cano family, in nineteen ninety-two, back in Guadalajara. There were seven of them and they had a station wagon and a pickup truck, and they followed the hearse in those two vehicles, and I promise you not one person at that cemetery noticed. In forty years I have never heard a family say, we should have rented the second car.

Why does it happen? Because it is on the package, because a family in grief does not want to be seen counting cars, and because there is a real and decent instinct underneath it: we should all arrive together. That instinct is good. It does not cost six hundred dollars. It costs a cousin with a minivan and a text message the night before.

Here is what changes. Ask for transportation as a separate line, and ask one question: what are we charged if the family drives itself? Then decide in the kitchen, not in the office.

Tonight's check. Count the cars in your family. Count the seats. If the number is bigger than the number of people who live more than an hour away, you do not need a limousine. You need a group text.

Number seven. The stationery kit.

Register book, seventy-five dollars. Acknowledgement cards, sixty. Two hundred printed programs, two hundred and seventy-five. A memorial video with photographs and music, two hundred and ninety-five. A webcast of the service for relatives who cannot travel, three hundred and fifty.

Individually, none of those numbers looks like anything. Together they are a thousand and fifty-five dollars, and on the estimate they arrive as one line called the memorial package.

Lalo, my apprentice, printed the programs for the Sosa family in two thousand and eleven on the machine in the back office. Two hundred of them. Thirty-eight dollars in paper and toner, and he stayed late to fold them, and he did it wrong the first time and had to start again, and the family kept one of the wrong ones because the fold was crooked in a way that made them laugh two days later.

Why does it happen? Because the family is exhausted and the funeral home is the only place that has all of it in one cabinet. Convenience is worth something. It is not worth a thousand dollars.

Here is what changes. We will handle the printing ourselves. That is the sentence. Then go to any copy shop with a photograph and a paragraph, and you will have programs for eighty dollars by the next morning. The register book you can buy online for fifteen. The acknowledgement cards, most families never send them, and the ones who do can write on plain cards from a drugstore.

Tonight's check. If you have a printer at home, print ten copies of the obituary tonight and put them in a folder. That is the beginning of the program, and doing it while you are not in crisis is the whole point.

Number eight. The newspaper obituary.

In San Antonio, a ten-line obituary in the daily paper ran between four hundred and eight hundred dollars the last time I helped a family place one, depending on the day and whether there was a photograph. Sunday costs more. A photograph costs more. A flag costs more.

The Fuentes family, in two thousand and nineteen, paid six hundred and forty dollars for an obituary that ran on a Tuesday. Eleven people called the funeral home about it. Eleven, in a city of a million and a half. The grandchildren found the death notice on the funeral home's own website, for free, because the funeral home posted it there anyway, and shared it on their phones, and that is how the family actually found out who was coming.

Why does it happen? Because the newspaper was how you told a city that somebody died, and that was true in nineteen seventy. It is not true now, and the price did not go down when the readership did.

Here is what changes. Ask the funeral home to post the obituary on their website, which they will do for nothing, and ask the church to put it in the bulletin, which is also free. If you want the newspaper, buy it for the two aunts who still read the paper, and buy the shortest version they will sell you.

Tonight's check. Open the funeral home's website on your phone right now, and find the page where the obituaries go. If you can find it in under a minute, that is where your family will look. If you cannot find it, that tells you something about that funeral home.

Number nine. The grave and the headstone.

This is the one that comes after the funeral, when everybody thinks the bill is finished.

Opening and closing the grave: a thousand to fifteen hundred dollars, and in some cemeteries it is charged by the funeral home and then again by the cemetery, and the family pays it twice because the two invoices arrive a week apart and nobody compares them. I have seen it. I have called about it. It is not always dishonesty. Sometimes it is just two computers.

The headstone. Twenty-five hundred dollars at the funeral home, for a flat granite marker with a name and two dates. Fourteen hundred at a monument company three miles away, for the same stone, and the cemetery has to accept it, because you are allowed to buy a marker anywhere and the cemetery may not require you to buy it from them.

The Ramirez family, in two thousand and seventeen. They had paid everything, they thought. Then in March the cemetery sent a bill for opening and closing, eleven hundred dollars, and the funeral home had already charged them for it in November. They came to me with both papers. It took one phone call and nine days, and they got the eleven hundred back.

Why does it happen? Because grief has an end date in the family's mind, and the paperwork does not. The funeral is over. The bill is not.

Here is what changes. Keep every invoice in one folder, and when the second one arrives, put them side by side. And before you buy a marker, call two monument companies and ask for a price on the same size and the same stone, in writing.

Tonight's check. If a funeral in your family happened in the last two years, open that folder tonight and count the invoices. If there are two that say opening and closing, you have a phone call to make on Monday morning.

[matter-of-factly] Now the thing that is not on any list. The yellow envelope.

In nineteen ninety-nine, my father was in his seventies and he walked with a cane. That was the last Day of the Dead he spent with us. He sat in the chapel in Analco all night with a candle for his own mother, and he would not let anybody else carry it, and at two in the morning he turned to me and asked the question I have never been able to put down. Mijo, do you know where the paper for this grave is?

Not the grave of his mother. His own. He had bought it, he had paid for it, and the paper that said where it was and what it had cost was somewhere in that building, and at two in the morning he could not remember where.

We found it at dawn, in a yellow envelope in the drawer of the desk I am sitting at right now, and by then the mariachi from the corner had started, and the whole street smelled of cempasúchil and wet earth. On the outside, in pencil, in his handwriting: entering on the right, next to the big pirul tree, the third grave.

Inside, on a sheet of paper folded in four, there were two lists. What he had paid for. And what he had refused to pay for.

He had refused four things. No vault. No protective casket. No limousine. No printed programs. He wrote, next to the last one, in pencil: the family will write the name with a pen, and that is better.

And he had paid for one thing that I would pay for too, and it is the only line on that paper that is not about an object. He paid the two men who dug the grave double. Not for speed. So they would take their time, and not hurry his mother into the ground.

My father died in two thousand and three, and I was in San Antonio, and I could not get there in time. But that envelope was in the drawer, and my brother found it, and everything was decided. There was nothing to argue about in the worst week of our lives, because a man with a cane had sat down on an ordinary afternoon and written it in pencil.

[whispering] So here is the tenth thing, and it is the only one that costs nothing.

The nine things are money. The tenth is a yellow envelope, and a sentence, and somebody in the family knowing where it is.

Tonight, before you go to bed, take a sheet of paper. Write four lines. Where the paper is. Who to call. What you want. And what you refuse to pay for. Then put it in an envelope, write the name of a person on the front, and tell that person where you put it.

That is the whole inheritance, mijo. Not the money. The envelope.
