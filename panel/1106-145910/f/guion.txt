Seven pieces. I counted them last Tuesday, standing in front of my mother's tansu, and I had to sit down on the tatami. Everything else in that closet is noise. Not because I am thinner than you, I am one meter fifty-two and I weigh what I weigh. And not because I am richer, my white shirt cost less than four pizzas. It is the cut, and it is one rule my mother used on every bride in Gion. The piece has to hold your body up, not hang on it. When it hangs, people see the hanger. And the hanger makes a woman look tired before she opens her mouth. The last piece is the one nobody in America knows. Eight inches of silk from 1975, and on Friday it goes into Carol's suitcase.

My mother was Fumiko Arai. She dressed brides and geiko in Gion for forty years, and I grew up between the mirrors of her shop in Nishijin. Then I lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, because my husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. So I know your closet. I know the mall, the sales, the three black jackets, the sweater you keep for a cold restaurant. I did it the American way too.

Here is what I learned, and it took me forty years. A piece you own forever has to pass three questions. One. Does it hold its shape after twenty washes, or does it go soft at the collar and lie down. Two. Does it work with the other six pieces, or does it need a special friend. Three. Can you sit on the floor in it, in a machiya, in February, and get up without holding the table. That is the test nobody asks in a shop.

People tell me the answer is a bigger closet. In Sunnyvale I had a closet the size of this kitchen, and I still stood in front of it every morning with nothing to wear. The clothes were there. The system was not. A closet with forty pieces and no rules is just forty decisions before breakfast, and by the time you make them, your face is tired and you put on the black jacket again. Seven pieces is not a punishment. It is a rest. It is the same reason a good kitchen has one good knife and not eleven bad ones.

And before we start, I want you to do something with me. Go to a window with good daylight, and take two things from your closet. One black, one white, a shirt or a scarf, whatever you have. Hold the black under your chin and look. Then the white. Do not look at the color. Look at the line under your jaw, under your eyes. With the black, that line gets deeper. It is not your face. It is the shadow. This is the whole video in one minute, and it costs nothing. Yui did it on Sunday with my black cardigan, and she said, Grandma, I look like I have not slept. That is the shadow.

Number one. A white cotton shirt with a collar that stands. Not a blouse with a soft collar that folds over and dies at the second wash. A shirt, with a proper stand, the kind a man wears, in your size. I have four. One of them is twenty-two years old and the collar is still standing, because it is cotton with a little weight, not the thin stuff from the big store. In 1975 my mother dressed a bride in Gion whose mother wore a white collar under her kimono, and Fumiko said, look, the collar is doing half the work of the face. A white collar throws light up under the jaw. A black one eats it. In summer I wear Mr. Okada's linen, from his stall in Nishiki; the rest of the year, cotton. Mine cost thirty-eight dollars at a shop near Sanjo, and a good one lasts ten years, which is four dollars a year. Tonight, take your white shirt and your black sweater to the window, both under your chin, one after the other. Then take a photo with your phone. The phone does not lie, and the mirror is very polite.

Number two. A thin cardigan in merino wool, the color of oatmeal or dark navy, no buttons you have to fight. Thin. Two-ply, if the label says it. In February the inside of this house is thirty-five degrees, and in August it is ninety-five with the humidity of a wet towel. A thick sweater is not warm, it is heavy. In 2023 I had two thick knits on and I could not lift my arms to hang the laundry on the second floor. Hiroshi found me standing on the stairs like a scarecrow. My mother said warmth is in the layer, not in the weight. Thin wool under a thin wool, that is warm, and you can still move. The thick fleece one is the trap; it is soft in the shop and it makes your shoulders into two round balls. My merino was sixty dollars, I have had it eight winters, and I wash it at eighty-six degrees in a net bag and dry it flat on a tenugui. Tonight, put on your thickest knit and raise both arms over your head. If your shoulders climb up to your ears, that piece is not doing you a favor. Fold it for your daughter and keep the thin one.

Number three. Navy trousers, straight, sitting at your real waist. Not black. Black looks hard in daylight, it shows every piece of lint from the tatami, and it makes the shadow we talked about. Navy is softer, and it goes with the white shirt and with the oatmeal cardigan, so it works with the other six. In 2016 I bought a pair one size too big because the waistband pinched me in the shop, and all the way home on Imadegawa-dori I was pulling them up in front of Yui. That is not the trouser's fault. That is the waistband's fault. After sixty, the waist is the part that changes, not the hip, so you buy for the waist and let a woman with a machine fix the rest. Mine cost seventy dollars, and the seamstress near Kawaramachi took eighteen dollars to shorten them and take in the waist. Tonight, sit down on a chair in your trousers for one full minute, then stand up. If you have to pull them up when you stand, they are not yours yet. Take them to a tailor, or give them away.

Number four. A square scarf, twenty-seven inches, cotton or silk, in a color that puts light in your face. Not a blanket. I see women in Kyoto with a scarf the size of a tablecloth, and by the third block it is a bag they carry on one shoulder. A small square is enough, folded corner to corner, once around the neck, one knot. In March Carol sent me a photo from Sacramento in a mustard scarf, and she wrote, Michiko, why does my face look gray. The mustard was the same value as her skin, so the two melted into one and her face disappeared. We found a soft coral for her, and in the next photo she looked awake. You do not need a new color; you need the color next to the face to be lighter or darker than the face. Mr. Okada keeps cotton remnants in a box at Nishiki, and a square with a hem costs eight dollars. Tonight, take three scarves to the window, one at a time, and photograph yourself. Choose the one where your eyes come forward. Not the one you love. The one where your eyes come forward.

Number five. Flat leather shoes that a man can resole. Mrs. Kinoshita lives across the street. She is eighty-one, she walks the Philosopher's Path every morning, ten thousand steps, and she has had the same leather shoes for fifteen years. Last spring she bought rubber shoes for the rain, new and light, and she slipped near the Eikan-do temple and sat on the wet stone for a while. The next morning she put the old leather back on. The cobbler near Demachiyanagi resoles them for twenty-five dollars, and they come back like new. Leather takes the shape of your foot, and your foot at sixty-eight is not the foot you had at fifty, it has its own map now. A stiff sole does not learn that map. Mine cost a hundred and twenty dollars, and I have resoled them twice. Tonight, pick up your shoe and bend it where the toes meet the sole. If it does not bend, if it is a board, it will not walk with you. It will make you walk on your heels, and that is what people hear before they see you.

Number six. A small bag that crosses your body and leaves both hands free. Carol came with a bag the size of a small suitcase, and at Kawaramachi station she could not find her glasses because both hands were holding the bag. Mrs. Kinoshita carries a bag the size of a paperback, three hundred grams, with one strap across her chest. Keys, glasses, a tenugui, a little purse. That is all. A heavy bag pulls one shoulder down, and then you walk crooked, and then your whole body looks tired even when it is not. Mine was forty-five dollars at a shop in the station, and the strap is wide enough that it does not cut. Tonight, put your bag on the kitchen scale. If it is over two pounds, take everything out and put back only what you used this week. You will be surprised what you have been carrying since March.

Before the last one, two small things. If you have been with me since the beginning of this closet, press the like button, it helps Yui and me more than you know. And subscribe, because next week Carol comes, and I will open her suitcase on the tatami.

And now the seventh piece. [whispering] In 1975 my mother dressed a bride in Gion in a kimono with an obi the color of a persimmon. When the bride left, Fumiko cut eight inches from the end of the obi and put it in her drawer, because she said a good silk should not disappear with one wedding. She died in 2009. That winter I found the silk, and I sewed it into a small pouch with my own hands, badly, the stitches are not straight. It holds my keys. It has no label and no price, and it is the piece I would save from the house. On Friday it goes into Carol's suitcase, with a note from me, because she is coming in April and she will need somewhere to put her keys. You can do this. There is one piece of fabric in your house that belonged to someone: a scarf, a sleeve, a tablecloth from your mother. Take it to a tailor, ten dollars, and ask for a small pouch with a zip. That is the seventh piece, and it costs ten dollars and a memory. Tonight, open the drawer where you keep the things you do not use, and find it. Put it where you see it every morning, next to your keys, and you will understand why I cried on Tuesday.

So. Seven pieces. A white shirt with a standing collar. A thin cardigan. Navy trousers at your waist. A small square scarf. Flat leather shoes. A small bag. And the piece with a person inside it. You do not need to buy anything tonight. Open your closet, take out the black thing, and hold the white one under your chin at the window. That is the whole beginning.

My mother used to say, your clothes speak before you do, so be careful what they say. She was right for forty years in Gion, and she is right in my kitchen in Nishijin.

Tell me in the comments: if you could keep only seven pieces, which one would be the first one you save? Yui reads them to me with my tea on Sunday. And in the next one, Carol arrives in April with two suitcases, and I show you the four things I take out of them before she unpacks. She will not be happy. She will thank me later.
