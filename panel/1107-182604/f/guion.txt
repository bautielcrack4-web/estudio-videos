Nine things Medicare already pays for when a parent is dying. Nine. And in thirty-four years of sitting in other people's kitchens, I watched families pay for most of them out of their own pocket, because nobody told them the number to call.

The number is on the back of your parent's Medicare card. Hospice. Ask for it by name.

My name is Rosa Navarro. I was a hospice chaplain here in San Antonio for thirty-four years, almost all of it out in the homes, out past Culebra and Zarzamora, out in Bandera and New Braunfels. I am not a physician and I have no medical training at all. I want that clear before we start, honey, because people hear a woman talking about dying and they assume a uniform. There was no uniform. I came with a bag and a rosary in my pocket and I sat down. If your mother has a symptom, you call your hospice team, not me. But the paperwork, the equipment, the money, the people who are supposed to show up at your door — that is what I know. That is what this video is.

And I will tell you the truth of it right now: the benefit is bigger than what most families use. Much bigger. I have seen a daughter sleeping on a recliner for eleven weeks with a bad back, and a hospital bed sitting in a warehouse three miles away, already paid for. I have seen a man driving to the pharmacy at midnight to buy something that was already covered for five dollars. Nobody was lying to those families. They just never asked, because they did not know there was anything to ask.

So here are the nine. I will go slow, and at the end of each one I will give you one thing to do tonight at that kitchen table. Not tomorrow. Tonight, while you still have the energy.

First, let me explain the thing underneath all nine, because once you understand this part, the rest stops feeling like charity and starts feeling like a bill you already paid.

Medicare hospice is Part A. Not a supplement, not an advantage plan, not something you buy. It is the same Part A that paid for your mother's hip in 2019. What makes it different is the door you walk through. Two physicians — your parent's own physician and the hospice medical director — have to certify that, if the illness runs its natural course, the prognosis is six months or less. That is the whole gate. And here is the part families get wrong every single time: the six months is not a deadline. It is a doorway. If your mother is still with us after six months, she is still on hospice, and they recertify her, and Medicare keeps paying. Nobody gets in trouble. Nobody gets cut off for living too long. I have had people on hospice for two years. Two years. [emphatically] Living too long is not the problem.

The periods work like this. Two periods of ninety days, then periods of sixty days with no limit on how many. You can revoke the benefit at any time and go back to regular Medicare. You can also change hospice agencies once in each period. Once. That is a real right and almost nobody uses it, and I will come back to it at the end, because it is the ninth thing and it is the one that saves the most people.

Now, before the nine, my test. It takes thirty seconds and I want you to do it out loud.

Say this sentence: "Who bathes my mother?"

If you know the answer — a name, a day of the week, a phone number — then you are already ahead of nine families out of ten and you can relax for a minute.

If you do not know, that is not a failure. That is your list. That is the exact place to start. Because somebody is going to bathe her. Somebody is going to lift her. Somebody is going to change the sheets at two in the morning when you have been awake for three days. The only question is whether that somebody is a trained aide who is already paid for, or you, at the end of your rope, with a bad back and a husband at home who has not had a hot dinner in a month.

Ask the question. Write the answer down. That is the test.

Number one. The aide.

Medicare hospice pays for an aide to come and help with what they call personal care. Bathing. Shampooing hair. Getting dressed. Getting to the bathroom. Changing the sheets with a person in the bed, which is a skill, honey, I promise you it is a skill.

I am thinking of Mrs. Ophelia Washington. East Side, off of New Braunfels Avenue, the year was 2008. She sang in the choir of a Baptist church all her life and she was still singing, quietly, in that bed, almost to the end. Her daughter Deborah had taken leave from her job and she was doing everything herself. Everything. Baths, laundry, the cooking, the cousins on the phone. By the fourth week Deborah was so tired she was crying in the driveway so her mother would not hear it.

And I said to her — this is the part I want you to hear — I said, "Deborah, who told you that you were supposed to do the bathing?"

She looked at me like I had asked her who told her to breathe. Nobody had told her. Nobody had told her she could ask for help either. So we called the agency that afternoon. The aide came Tuesdays and Thursdays, and she was a woman named Alma who had done this work for twenty years and sang while she worked, in Spanish, and Ophelia Washington did not understand one word of it and loved it anyway.

Deborah got her Tuesday mornings back. She started going to church again. And I want to be careful here, because this matters: she did not stop being a daughter. She stopped being an aide. There is a difference and it is the difference between a family that survives this and a family that breaks.

How much does it cost? For the aide, under the hospice benefit, nothing. Not a copay. Nothing. It is part of the benefit.

Tonight's check: call the agency and ask one sentence — "How many aide visits a week does my mother's plan include, and which days?" Get the days. Put them on the refrigerator. Because if you do not know the days, you will clean the house before they come, and then you will be too tired to sit with her.

Number two. The equipment.

The hospital bed. The alternating pressure mattress, so she does not get sores. The wheelchair. The walker. The shower chair. The bedside commode. The oxygen and the concentrator. The lift, if you need a lift.

All of it from the hospice, on loan, at no cost to the family.

I cannot tell you how many houses I walked into where a family had bought a hospital bed. Bought it. Four hundred, six hundred dollars, off of a listing, from a man in a truck, and then it did not fit through the bedroom door. This happened to me three times. Three times, different families, same hallway.

Mr. Herman Vogel, my very first family, 1991, New Braunfels. German, retired, kept his tools in perfect order in the garage and his opinions in perfect order in his head. His wife Ilse would not let anybody move the furniture. Not one chair. We were going to bring a bed in and she said no, and I learned that day that no is not always about the bed. Sometimes no is about the last room in the house that is still hers.

But here is what I want you to take: the equipment is not a gift you are asking for. It is delivered by a company under contract with the hospice, and the hospice arranges it, and it arrives, usually the next day, sometimes the same day. You call the hospice team and you say the words "we need a bed." That is the whole process. You do not need to be poor. You do not need to qualify separately. It is inside the benefit.

And when it is over — when your mother dies — they come and take it away. You do not have to do that. That is the call you should not have to make, and you will not have to make it.

Tonight's check: walk into the bedroom and look at the path from the door to the bed. Is it wide enough for a wheelchair? Is there a plug near the bed? Is there a lamp she can reach? Say it out loud into your phone as a note. Tomorrow you read the note to your hospice team and it becomes an order.

Number three. The chaplain.

Yes. The chaplain is part of the team. Not a volunteer from a church who means well. A chaplain, assigned to your mother's case, paid for by the benefit, at no cost.

And before you decide you do not need one because you are not religious — hear me. I sat with Baptists and Catholics and Jews and Jehovah's Witnesses and men who had not darkened a church door since 1974 and told me so in the first five minutes. I was not there to convert anybody, honey. I was there so that nobody dies alone at two in the morning.

Mr. Delgado. Veteran of Korea, from over by Kelly Field. His daughter called me because he had started talking to his brother. Ramiro. Dead since 1951. And the daughter was frightened, and she wanted me to tell him to stop.

I did not tell him to stop. I sat down and I asked him about Ramiro, and he told me a story about a bicycle, and he laughed, and then he cried, and his daughter sat in the doorway with her hand over her mouth because she had not heard her father laugh in a year.

That is what the chaplain is for. Not to fix anything. To be the person in the room who is not afraid of the conversation. And every hospice in this country has one, and it costs your family nothing, and most families never ask for it because they think it is only for the very religious, or only for the last hour.

It is not for the last hour. It is for the whole time. And it is for you, too — the daughter, the son, the husband. The chaplain is not only your mother's chaplain.

Tonight's check: when you call the agency, say, "I would like the chaplain to visit." Not "if there's a chaplain available." Say it as a request, because it is one, and they will schedule it.

Number four. The social worker.

This one surprises people, and it is the one that saves families from the paperwork, which is its own kind of dying.

The social worker is part of the hospice team, covered, no cost. And the social worker is not there to take your mother away to a nursing home, which is what everybody assumes. I have heard that assumption a hundred times. "We don't want the social worker, we're keeping her home." Good. Keep her home. The social worker is one of the people who makes that possible.

What do they actually do? They help with the advance directive nobody can find. They help with the power of attorney that was signed in 2011 and now nobody remembers where. They help with the family — the brother in Phoenix who calls every night with opinions and has not once been in the room. They help when the money runs out. They help when you have to tell your boss you are not coming back next week and you do not know how to say it. They know which programs exist in this county. And they know which funeral home will not take advantage of a family at four in the morning, and yes, honey, that is worth knowing.

Señora Inés Garza. Dementia. Out in Bandera, and her daughter Mari had three brothers and all three of them had a plan for their mother and none of the three plans was the same. At one point two of them were not speaking. I sat in that living room and I watched this family split down the middle over a house and a bank account, while their mother was in the back bedroom.

The social worker got them into one room. Not me. Her. She was the one who said, "Let's write down what your mother wanted, not what each of you wants." And they did. It took two hours and one of the brothers cried, and it held.

That is the work. It is not glamorous. It is the difference between a family that can be in the room together at the end, and a family that cannot.

Tonight's check: find the advance directive. Tonight. If you cannot find it in twenty minutes, that is your answer — you do not have one you can use. Write that down and tell the social worker at the first visit.

Number five. Respite.

This is the one that makes people put their hand over their mouth.

If you are the family caregiver, Medicare hospice pays for your mother to go into a hospice facility — or a nursing facility that contracts with the hospice — for up to five days at a time, so that you can rest. So that you can sleep. So that you can go to your own physician, or your daughter's wedding, or just stay in your own house and be quiet for one day.

Five days. And the copay is five percent of the Medicare rate. Five percent. That is, in most cases, a handful of dollars a day.

When I explain this to families, the reaction is always the same. A pause. And then, "You can do that?"

[whispering] You can do that.

I had a wife — I will not use her name, because she is still living and she would know herself — and she had not slept more than three hours in a row in four months. Four months. She was doing the turning, the medications, the bedpan, all of it, and she was sixty-eight years old herself with a heart condition. And she kept saying, "I'm fine, I'm fine," the way we all say it.

I said, "Would you let your husband drive a car with no sleep for four months?" She said no. I said, "Then why are you doing it?"

She took the respite. She went to her sister's house in Kerrville. She slept for two days and then she cried for an hour and then she came back, and she was a different woman, and her husband had five more weeks and she was there for all of them.

That is not selfish. That is how you get to be there at the end without breaking.

Tonight's check: say out loud, "If I needed five days, where would he go?" If you do not know the answer to that, ask the social worker at the first visit, before you need it. Because you will not be able to plan it at three in the morning on the worst night.

Number six. Continuous care.

This is the one nobody knows exists, and it is the one for the crisis.

Normally, hospice comes to your house a few times a week. Skilled nursing visits, aide visits, the chaplain, the social worker. But there are situations — a pain crisis, a symptom that will not settle, a family that is suddenly in over its head — where the hospice can send someone to your home for shifts of hours, around the clock, until the crisis passes.

That is called continuous care, and it is part of the benefit.

I want to be careful here because I have no medical training, so I am going to say only what I saw. I saw a house in the middle of the night where the daughter was alone and frightened and the hospice team came and stayed until the morning and the daughter slept on the couch for the first time in a week. I saw it more than once. It is not automatic — you have to meet the criteria, and the hospice team is the one who decides — but it exists, and you have to know the words to ask for it.

The words are: "Is this a crisis? Do we qualify for continuous care?"

Walt Pruitt. Ranchero out in Bandera. There was a night his daughter called me at one in the morning and she could not get a word out. And I called the on-call line, and they came out that night, and they stayed. That is what the on-call number is for. It is not for business hours.

Tonight's check: find the on-call number. It is on the paperwork they gave you and it is probably in a folder you have not opened. Put it in your phone right now, under "Hospice — night." Do it while I am talking.

Number seven. The medications.

Here is the number that changes people's faces: medications for pain and for the symptoms of the terminal diagnosis are covered, and the copay can be no more than five dollars per prescription.

Five dollars.

Not the blood pressure pill, not the cholesterol pill, not the ones for the other conditions — those are handled differently, and you should ask about them specifically. But the medications that are for comfort, for pain, for the symptoms of what is happening now: those are inside the benefit, at a copay of up to five dollars.

I have watched a man drive to a pharmacy at eleven at night to pay ninety-four dollars for something that was covered. Ninety-four. He had the receipt in his shirt pocket and he showed it to me like it was proof of something. It was proof all right.

And I have watched a wife skip her own heart medication so there would be money for her husband's, which is the kind of arithmetic that happens in this country in houses that look perfectly fine from the street.

Tonight's check: take every bottle on that kitchen counter and put a piece of tape on the ones that are for comfort and pain. Then call your hospice team and read them the list. Ask her which ones go through the hospice and what the copay is. That is a five-minute call and it is worth hundreds of dollars.

Number eight. The grief support.

The benefit does not end when your mother dies. It keeps going — for the family, for up to thirteen months after the death. Counseling, support groups, the chaplain, the social worker. Visits and phone calls. It is called bereavement care and it is part of the hospice benefit.

Thirteen months. And here is why it is thirteen and not twelve: because the first year has a birthday in it, and a Thanksgiving, and a Christmas, and the day she died. And then it has all of them again.

My mother, Doña Refugio — we called her Cuca — died in her own house in March of 2015, with hospice, with me beside her. And I had been a chaplain for twenty-four years by then. Twenty-four years of telling other people what to expect.

And I still could not find the potatoes in the grocery store for six weeks.

That is not a metaphor. I stood in the aisle at the H-E-B on Bandera Road and I could not remember which ones we bought. And the bereavement coordinator called me — me, the chaplain, the professional — and she said, "Rosa, how are you doing," and I said, "I'm fine," and she said, "I'm going to call you again next Tuesday," and she did.

Nobody is too strong for that call. You are not too strong for that call.

Tonight's check: ask the agency, "Who is the bereavement coordinator, and will they call my family after?" Get the name. Write it in the same place you wrote the aide's days.

Number nine.

This is the one I have been saving, and it is not a benefit. It is a right, and it is the one that saves the most families.

You can change hospice agencies. Once in each benefit period. And you can revoke the benefit entirely and go back to regular Medicare, at any time, for any reason.

Why does that matter? Because a hospice is only as good as the people who walk through your door. And I am going to say something that will not make me popular with some agencies: some of them are wonderful and some of them are understaffed and some of them will take your mother's case and then not call you back for four days.

You are allowed to fire them.

I have seen a family sit in a house for three weeks with a hospice that never came, thinking they had no choice, because nobody had told them they did. They thought hospice was like a hospital — you get assigned, you go where they send you. It is not. It is a service you are receiving, and you can change it.

Here is how: you call the new agency, they handle the transfer, and the two agencies coordinate the date. You do not have to have a fight with the first one. You do not have to explain yourself. You say, "We're changing agencies effective such and such a date." That is the whole sentence.

And I will tell you when to use it. If you call the on-call number at night and nobody answers twice — change. If the aide does not come and nobody calls to say why, twice — change. If you cannot get a straight answer about the plan of care — change. You do not owe anybody loyalty at the worst moment of your family's life.

[excited] That is the ninth thing. That is the one nobody tells you, and it is free, and it is yours.
