Last week my friend Carol, in Sacramento, asked me what perfume I wear. I wrote back one word. None. She called me on Sunday night, Saturday for her, and she said, "Michiko, nobody smells that clean by accident."

She is right. It is not an accident. It is nine small habits, and eight of them cost nothing at all.

I own one bottle of perfume. I bought it in 1985, at a department store in San Jose, the first year my husband Hiroshi was sent to California for his work. It is still three-quarters full. Forty-one years, and I have never finished it. My daughter Naomi thinks this is a scandal. Yui, my granddaughter, thinks it is a joke. I think it is simply how we were raised here.

And I want to be careful with one thing, because I have watched a lot of videos about this, and most of them get it backwards. They tell you that a woman over sixty needs a stronger perfume, because her skin has changed, because her nose has changed. That is like putting a bigger hat on a bad haircut. [emphatically] The problem is never the smell you add. The problem is the smell you are covering.

There is a rumour, in America, that Japanese women smell clean because of something in the water, or green tea, or a soap you can only buy in Tokyo. I have heard that from three different women at a wedding in Sunnyvale. I laughed. No secret soap. There is a way of handling cloth, and a way of handling the evening, and after forty years of doing it, the body smells like a clean house instead of a clean bottle.

Here is my credential, so you know why I am the one telling you this. My mother, Fumiko, was a kitsuke-shi in Gion for forty years. She dressed brides. In one morning she would put a woman into eleven layers of silk, and she would tie the obi so tight the bride could not breathe, and she could tell, from the inside of a collar, whether that girl's family dried their clothes in the sun or in a machine. She could tell from a metre away. I learned to smell fabric from her the way other children learn to read.

And I lived nineteen years in California. Sunnyvale, 1983 to 2002. I did it the American way too. I had the big plastic jug of softener. I had the dryer sheets. I had a bottle of perfume for going out. So when I tell you what I stopped doing, I am not guessing. I stopped because I lived inside it.

So. Nine things. Every one of them you can do tonight, with what is already in your apartment.

The first one: I never put perfume on cloth. Never on a blouse, never on a scarf, never on the inside of a collar.

Because cloth does not forget. Skin forgets in a day. Cloth keeps a perfume and then, after two or three days, it changes it. The alcohol goes, and what stays behind is the heavy part, the sweet part, and that part mixes with the body oil that is already in the fibre. That is the smell people describe as stale. Not perfume. Perfume, plus cloth, plus time.

My mother used to say it about kimono: a kimono does not wear perfume, the woman wears the kimono. You do not pour a bottle on your grandmother's silk.

Tonight: take the scarf you wore yesterday and put it near your face, not at the edge, in the middle, where it touched your neck. Breathe. If it smells like a candle, it is your scarf talking, not you.

The second one: what I do instead is the back of the neck and the brush. One place on the body, one place on the hair.

Your neck, at the back, under the hair. That is the warmest place on the body that a person comes near when they hug you. Your hair, that is the place that moves when you turn your head. Those two places do not need a perfume. They need to be clean, and they need to be the last thing you rinse.

Here is the thing nobody tells you. When you take a bath, you wash your face, your arms, your feet. You do not wash the back of your neck properly, because you cannot see it. So you finish the bath and the back of your neck still has the day on it, and then the perfume goes on top of the day. And the day wins.

Three dollars for a small brush at a pharmacy. In the evening, five strokes with one drop of camellia oil on the bristles, not on your hair, on the brush, and your hair smells like a clean pillow.

The third one, and this is the biggest: the sun.

Not a candle, not a spray, not a linen water that costs twenty-two dollars. The sun. When cotton dries in the sun, it takes in a smell that nothing in a bottle can copy. We call it hi no nioi. The smell of the sun. Every woman on this street knows it. You can smell a shirt that dried outside from across a room, and you will not be able to say what it is.

Carol's towels in Sunnyvale smelled of a candle shop. She had a dryer and a box of sheets that smelled like a bakery, and she thought that was clean. Then one morning in 1989 I hung a towel over the fence in her garden, just one, and she came out and said, "What is this?" I said, "That is a towel."

Thirty minutes outside is enough. Thirty. A windowsill, a balcony, a chair by an open window. Thirty minutes, and then bring it in.

And here is the test I want you to do today, because it takes two minutes and it is the only test in this video where you have to leave the room.

Go outside. Close your door. Stand there with your back to it for two minutes, and do nothing, breathe the street air. Then open the door and walk in. The first breath you take inside is what your house really smells like. Not what you think it smells like. What it smells like. Everybody's nose switches off the smell it lives in, yours included, so the only way to find it is to leave and come back.

Write down the first word that comes to you. Very often it is a word we do not like, and that word is the real reason a woman reaches for a bottle.

The fourth one: half a cap.

More detergent does not make clothes cleaner. It makes them hold more. Every grain of powder that does not rinse out stays in the fibre, and in the summer that is what sours. If your clothes smell fine when they are wet and strange when they have sat in the drawer for a week, it is not the wash. It is the rinse.

I use half of what the bottle says, and one extra rinse cycle. The extra rinse costs nothing, it is one button, and it is the single change in this house that made the biggest difference. I have done it since 2002.

And the softener. The softener is a coat of wax. That is the whole story, and I will tell it to you another day, because there is a plastic bucket in this story that deserves its own video.

The fifth one: the evening, not the morning.

In Japan we bathe at night. Ofuro, the deep bath, before bed. Not a shower in the morning on the way out. The reason is not modesty and it is not ritual. The reason is that a body washed at night goes into a clean bed clean, and a body washed in the morning has spent eight hours in its own night first.

If you only change one thing from this video, change this one. Wash at night, and change your pillowcase twice a week. Twice. Not once. The pillowcase is where your face spends a third of its life, and it is the first thing a grandchild smells when she hugs you in the morning.

And the futon, or the duvet, in an American bedroom, gets aired. Ten minutes with the window open, every morning, even in winter. In Kyoto we hang it over the railing. In a New York apartment you fold it back over the foot of the bed and open a window. It is enough.

The sixth one: know which cloth holds and which cloth lets go.

Wool holds. Silk holds. Anything with a plastic in it holds like a closed jar. Linen and cotton let go. You can wash a linen shirt and the smell of last Tuesday is gone, completely, and that is a kind of freedom.

So the cardigan that sits against your neck, the scarf, the collar of the coat. Those should be wool, but they should be aired often, because wool is a sponge. And the shirt under it should be cotton or linen, because that is the layer that takes the day and gives it back at the laundry.

I have a black wool cardigan from 1994. I wear it in November. I never wear it two days in a row, and on the day I do not wear it, it hangs by the window. It does not smell like anything. That is the achievement. A cardigan that smells like nothing is a cardigan that smells clean.

The seventh one: what goes in the drawer.

This is the one almost nobody in America knows about, and it costs about four dollars.

In my tansu, the paulownia chest that was my mother's, every drawer has a small cloth bag. Inside the bag: dried yuzu peel, a few cloves, and a piece of lavender from the garden. A bag the size of your palm, tied with a string, that you made in five minutes.

The clothes come out of that drawer smelling faintly of citrus and wood, and it stays on them for a day, and then it is gone. That is the difference. A perfume announces itself for six hours. A drawer gives your blouse a smell for one day, so faint that only a person standing close to you will find it. And that person will think it is you.

[excited] Make one tonight. A clean sock, a spoon of dried peel, two cloves, tie the top. Put it in the drawer with your sweaters. In June, after the rainy season, you change the peel. Four dollars for a bag of dried yuzu at an Asian grocery, and it lasts a year.

The eighth one: the room, not the woman.

Here is what I think the real difference is, after all of this. In America, a woman perfumes herself. In Kyoto, a house perfumes itself, and the woman lives in it.

My kitchen in the morning smells of rice and dashi. My hallway smells of wood and tatami. The rush mat, igusa, has a green smell, like cut grass in the shade. When I come home from the market, my coat smells of my hallway for an hour, and then it does not. And when someone hugs me at the door, they are not smelling a bottle. They are smelling a house they want to sit down in.

You can do this with a pot. Put a pot of water on the stove with a piece of orange peel and a cinnamon stick, ten minutes in the evening, and let the steam go through the room. Your curtains and your coat will take it. It costs nothing, and it is not a perfume, it is a house.

The ninth one. And before this one, do the small thing, press the button, it helps Yui and me more than you know.

The ninth is the hardest, and it is the only one that is really about you and not about your clothes.

You cannot smell yourself. You never could. The nose switches off the smell it lives in, for everyone. So a woman who has worn the same perfume for twenty years has no idea what her living room thinks of her.

The test is three seconds. Take the sweater you wore yesterday. Turn the cuff inside out, the inside, the part that touched your wrist all day. Breathe in it three times. That is what your family smells when they hug you. Not the outside of the sweater. The inside of the cuff.

If it smells like soap and nothing else, good. If it smells like a warm animal, the layer under it is not clean. If it smells like a flower, you are wearing too much of something, and everybody but you knows it.

The tenth one is not a habit. It is a sentence, and I had to learn it from my mother, and I will give it to you.

[whispering] She said: you do not need to be noticed from across the room. You need to be remembered from close.

That is the whole thing. In a restaurant, in a line at the bank, at church, a perfume is a voice raised so everyone can hear it. But the people who love you do not stand across the room. They stand here, this close, with their face at your neck, and they are not judging a bottle. They are deciding whether they want to stay.

And here is the part that surprised me. When I stopped adding a smell, I started noticing my own. After two weeks I could tell the day I needed to wash my hair, the day the pillowcase was due, the day the cardigan had to go to the window. My nose came back. I had not lost it with age. I had buried it under laundry.

So, tonight. Half a cap in the machine. One more rinse. A sock with orange peel in the drawer. A towel on the windowsill for thirty minutes. And your nose in the cuff of yesterday's sweater.

Nothing to buy. That is the point. Every woman I know on this street spends less on smell than anybody I met in California, and every one of them gets told she smells good.

My mother died in 2009. When we opened her tansu, the kimonos smelled of paulownia wood, and a little of the incense she burned in the room on the anniversary of her own mother's death, and nothing else. No perfume. Forty years of dressing brides, and the last thing her closet said was: clean.

Now I want to hear from you. What is the smell that means home to you? Mine is the inside of that drawer. Yui reads the comments to me with my tea on Sunday, and I listen to all of them twice, because the second time I understand the jokes.

And next time, I will tell you about the bucket. The big yellow jug of fabric softener I carried home from Sunnyvale in 1994, and the day in 2002 when I carried it out to the street and did not feel sorry at all. What I use in the machine now costs two dollars, and you already have it in your kitchen. That is the next one.
