[whispering] Last time I told you about Carol and the subway map. Five pairs of glasses, one small shop on Sanjō-dōri here in Kyoto, and a mirror that does not lie.

I am sixty-eight. I have a small face, a short nose, and grey hair I cut myself. For ten years I wore the wrong glasses, because I bought them the way most of us buy them: in a pharmacy, the pair that looked nice on the rack, four seconds in the mirror.

Four seconds cannot tell you this. A frame is a line you draw on your face, and that line decides where the eye goes first. Draw it low and the eye goes down. Draw it dark and heavy and the eye stops at the frame and never arrives at your face.

This is not about being thinner, and it is not about money. The pair I wear now cost ninety-five dollars, frame and lenses. The pair that made me look tired cost four hundred, and they were beautiful. Beautiful is not right.

By the end you will know the five shapes I tried in that shop, what each does to a face after sixty, and one test you can do tonight with a mirror and your thumb. The last is the shape almost nobody in America asks for. It took the most years off mine.

My mother was Fumiko Arai. She was a kitsuke-shi in Gion for forty years, which means she dressed brides and geiko in kimono, by hand, layer by layer. She was not an optician and neither am I. She looked at faces all day and had to decide, in one second, what a face needed.

And I lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002. So I know the American closet: the pharmacy rack, the buy-one-get-one table, the pair in the drawer for the car.

The shop is Tanaka Megane-ten, on Sanjō-dōri, one door from Teramachi. Narrow, two meters wide, frames in wooden trays to the ceiling, a cat asleep on the cash register. Mr. Tanaka is seventy-one. It was his father's shop and his grandfather's.

He did not ask what I liked. He asked what I do all day. I said: I read, I cook, I walk to the river, I look at a telephone more than I admit. He nodded, took out a wooden caliper, and measured me temple to temple. One hundred and twenty-eight millimeters. Then he measured the glasses I came in with. One hundred and forty-two.

That difference is the whole video.

Before the five shapes, one test. Thirty seconds, and it is what Mr. Tanaka does with every woman before he shows a single frame.

Take the glasses you wear most. Go to a mirror. Hold them at arm's length, level with your eyes. Find the top edge and find your eyebrow. Is the eyebrow inside the frame, above it, or cut in half by it?

If the top edge cuts your eyebrow, or sits above it, that frame is not on your face. It is on your forehead, and everything below it hangs.

Now turn them sideways and find the top of your cheekbone. If the frame sits below it, the frame is longer than your face, and it is pulling the face down. There should be a gap, a finger's width, not a hand's width.

The last part is the thumb. Put it under the bridge, at the little pads, and lift two millimeters. Look. That is your face with the frame where it belongs.

Do this in the room where you drink your tea, not the bathroom. Bathroom light comes from above and flatters everything.

Now, the five shapes.

Shape one. Rimless. No frame around the lens, just the lens, a thin metal arm, and a hinge.

The problem it solves. Past sixty the skin under the eye gets thinner, and a heavy frame throws a shadow there, in the crease under the lower lid. That shadow does not read as shadow. It reads as a bag. I spent two years believing I had new bags. I had a frame.

The nicest pair was a rimless oval with a tiny gold hinge at the temple. It weighed nothing. Mr. Tanaka put them on me, stepped back, and said nothing, which in that shop is a compliment.

Mrs. Kinoshita lives across the street. She is eighty-one, five feet one, and walks the Philosopher's Path every morning. Rimless glasses for twelve years, and she will tell you why: they do not slide. She bends down for a persimmon and they stay put.

Our faces get softer at the nose and the temples. A frame that rested on bone at forty rests on skin at sixty-eight, so a heavy frame creeps. And when your glasses creep, you tilt your chin up, and that tilt is the posture everybody reads as tired.

The lens is where the money goes. The rimless pair was ninety-five dollars with a single-vision lens. A progressive is a hundred and forty more, and Mr. Tanaka will tell you a progressive in a very small rimless oval is a compromise. If you drive at night, use a wider pair in the car.

Your check tonight: take the glasses off and look at the skin under your eye. Put them on. If it looks darker with them, the frame is drawing the bag. Try thinner, or no frame at all.

Shape two. The soft cat-eye. I mean soft. Five degrees, maybe eight. Not the 1960s thing with points, which is a costume.

A straight line across an eye that has begun to drop makes the drop visible. It is a ruler laid next to a slope.

My mother did makeup for a bride's mother in 1975, in a back room in Gion, a woman of about sixty who was crying. Fumiko made one small line at the outer corner of each eye, upward, two millimeters, and said: "The line goes up, so the face goes up." I was sixteen. I thought she meant makeup. She meant every line near a face, including a frame.

After sixty the outer corner of the upper lid relaxes and the space between it and the cheek shortens. The cat-eye puts the space back. Mr. Tanaka's is warm brown acetate, thin at the bottom, thicker at the top corner, and the lift is so small you cannot name it from the front. You only see that the face is brighter. One hundred and twenty dollars with lenses.

Your check tonight: compare the inner and outer corner of your eye inside the frame. If the frame's outer corner sits lower, it is pulling you down. Two millimeters up is a difference you feel in your shoulders first.

Shape three. A rounded rectangle, in tortoise. Not black.

Here I have to be careful. I have a rule in this house and I keep it: no black near my face. Not a scarf, not a collar, not a frame. If I tell you a thing is wrong, I do not get to do it.

The problem with black is not fashion. It is light. Black is a hole: it takes the light that reaches it and gives nothing back. On silver hair it becomes the strongest contrast in the picture, so your eye goes there first and your face comes second. And its shadow under the eye is colder than a brown frame's.

Carol Whitfield is my closest friend. She is seventy, she lives in Sacramento, and in 2019 she sent me a photograph from her son's wedding. Big black frames she had bought that spring, and a note: "Do I look tired? The photographer was terrible." She did not look tired. She looked grey. Her hair had gone silver that year, and the black was eating the light.

Tortoise does the opposite. Brown, warm, some variation in it, so it sits next to the skin instead of in front of it. Olive works. Deep honey works. Grey-brown if your hair is very white. This pair was one hundred and forty dollars, and I almost bought it instead of the rimless.

Your check tonight: hold the frame next to your hair, near your ear, in daylight. Colder than your hair, it is fighting you. Warmer, it is helping.

Shape four. A small oval in thin gold metal. Small is the whole point.

The problem: the frame is too wide for the face. I have a small face, I kept buying frames made for an average head, and then I wondered why I looked like a child in her father's glasses.

Mr. Okada sells linen and tenugui in the Nishiki market, at a stall two meters wide his father built. He cuts cloth to the body, never the body to the cloth, and he told me that once about a blouse. Glasses are the same. The frame is cut to the face.

The caliper said one hundred and twenty-eight. The glasses I brought in were one hundred and forty-two. Fourteen millimeters of frame standing in the air on each side, and the center of the lens outside my pupil, so I looked through the inner edge, where the correction curves.

Any woman in a frame too wide does the same thing: she pushes it up with one finger, ten times an hour. I did it so often that Hiroshi thought it was a habit.

Why after sixty: the bridge of the nose softens, the frame sits lower, and the wider it is, the more it slides. A woman always pushing her glasses looks like she is looking for something.

The gold oval was one hundred and ten dollars, lenses included. Thin metal, no weight, and small enough to sit inside my face instead of on top of it.

Your check tonight: look straight into the mirror. Is your pupil in the middle of the lens, or near the inside edge? Then shake your head gently, like a small no. If the glasses move, they are too wide, and a good optician can tighten that in ten minutes for free.

Now, before the fifth one, two things. I do not sell anything and I never will.

Shape five. The clear frame. Translucent acetate, the color of weak tea, or pale grey-green, so you can see the wall through the rim.

Nobody asks for this one. The clear frames sit in the bottom tray, and in nineteen years Mr. Tanaka has sold maybe one a month. It is the one that took the most years off my face.

Here is why. A frame's job is to draw a line, and a line can be made of light. A translucent rim picks up the light of the room and passes it, so instead of a dark border around the eye you get a bright one. The skin under the eye is lit by the frame itself. Nothing is drawn down.

Mine was made in Sabae, in Fukui, where most Japanese frames are made, in workshops older than my house. Pale amber, soft round corners, a slightly thicker top. One hundred and eighty dollars, and I bought it.

Yui came with me that afternoon. She is twenty-two, she studies at Doshisha, and she picked up the clear pair as a joke, because they looked like safety glasses. She put them on me, took a photograph, looked at it a long time, and said, in English, because she is showing off: "Obaachan. You look like you slept."

That is the whole review. Not younger. Rested. And rested is what we are asking for when we say younger.

I wore them out of the shop and we walked to the river. A woman I have known for twenty years, who I see every week at the market, did not recognize me for two seconds. Then she said: "Did you change your hair?" I had not.

At this age the face needs light near the eye more than a shape. The skin is thinner, the shadow under the brow is deeper, and every bit of light that reaches the eye makes the face read as awake.

Your check tonight: hold the frame against a white wall. If the rim turns grey, it will turn grey on your face. If it stays the color of the light, take it to the window. That is the pair.

So. Five shapes. Rimless for the shadow. A soft cat-eye for the outer corner. Tortoise for the light. A small gold oval for the size of the face. And clear, for the light that comes through.

But I want to end where my mother would end, and she would not end at a shop.

She kept her glasses in a cloth bag in the tansu, the paulownia chest from her mother, and wore them only to read the order book for the brides. Gold, small, round, one arm mended with silk thread. When I was young I asked why she did not wear them always. She said: "The frame is a hairpin, not a hat."

It holds something, and it is almost nothing. If you notice the frame before the face, the frame is too much.

So do not go and buy five pairs. Open the drawer. You have three in there: the pharmacy, the car, the wedding. Take them out tonight, stand where you drink your tea, and do the thumb test on each. One will be right, or almost right, and almost right can be fixed for ten dollars.

And if none is right, you know what to ask for. Not "something stylish." Ask for the width of your face in millimeters, ask for the top of the frame to sit on your brow, and ask to see the clear ones.

Now, Carol. Carol is coming to Kyoto in April and does not know it yet. Last week a box arrived from Sacramento, and inside it was the leopard blouse, the bright one she gave me in 2002, the one I almost kept. She sent it back with a note: "It makes me look tired. You always know why."

I know why. It is not the print and it is not her face. It is the fabric. Polyester holds the shape of the body that wore it before, and it holds the light in a way that flattens everything near it. That is next week's conversation, and also why I gave away seven things from this house and never bought them again.

And if you are standing in a pharmacy tonight, put the frame down for one second, and look at your eyebrow. That is all. The rest follows.
