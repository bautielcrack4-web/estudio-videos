Three days. Hiroshi had worn it three days, and he told me it was still clean. It looked clean. It smelled clean to me, because I live in this house and I smell it every morning. Sachiko-san does not live in this house, so she smelled the truth.

This is not a video about being dirty. You are not dirty. I am not dirty. It is a video about one small thing: the water that stays in a towel, or in a shirt, or in a sweater, and what happens in that water while you are sleeping.

Sour is not the smell of sweat. Sour is the smell of living things that grew after the washing was finished.

By tonight you will know seven rules my mother used, and every one of them can be done with what is already in your kitchen and your laundry room. No machine to buy. One thing costs two dollars, one costs nine, and I will tell you exactly which.

Sour has three ingredients, and only three. Water that stayed too long. Oil that never left the fibre. And soap that dried into a film and held the water there. That is the whole chemistry of it. When you understand those three, every rule in this video makes sense, and you will start to see the problem in your own house before you smell it.

My mother was Fumiko Arai. She was a kitsuke-shi in Gion for forty years. She dressed brides and geiko, the women who work in the teahouses, in kimono, by hand, in a room with one window and a heater she never turned on. Every layer of silk had to sit straight, and every layer had to smell of nothing. Not perfume. Nothing.

I lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002. My husband worked in an office with carpets and air conditioning, and I learned the American way of washing. Everything together, cold water, a big jug of softener, and a machine that does the work while you sleep. I did it the American way too. I am not here to tell you that you are doing it wrong. I am here to tell you what I changed when I came home, and why my towels stopped smelling sour.

Now, before the rules, I want you to do one test with me. It takes one minute, and it will tell you more than any video.

Tonight, take a towel from your cupboard. A clean one, the one you think is fine. Do not smell it in the bathroom, where the air is wet. Take it into the room where you drink your tea. Unfold it, fold it again the other way, and put your nose right into the crease in the middle. Then tell me what you smell. If it is nothing, you are lucky. If it is a little sour, a little like a wet dog, a little like bread that sat too long, that is the smell I am talking about. And it did not come from your body. It came from the last time you washed it.

The test matters: smell the towel when it is dry, not when it is wet. A wet towel always smells of water. A dry towel tells you what is living inside it.

Rule one. Wash towels at one hundred and forty degrees Fahrenheit, not cold.

Here is the problem. Body oil is not water. It is fat, and fat needs heat to let go of a cotton loop. When you wash at sixty-eight degrees, the water is comfortable for your hands, and it is also comfortable for the oil. The oil stays on the fibre. The towel comes out looking white, feeling clean, and holding a thin film of last week.

My mother washed the white under-collars of the geiko's kimono by hand, in 1975, in a copper basin behind the shop on Hanamikoji. She used water hot enough that she wore two pairs of gloves, and she changed the water three times. She told me: "Cold water is for silk. Hot water is for skin." She meant the skin that touches the cloth.

Why does this matter more after sixty? Because we were taught to save. My generation, and yours, we turn the dial to cold because our mothers did, because energy was expensive, and because cold is gentler on the colours. For sheets and towels, that is the wrong place to be gentle.

The change: one load a week, towels only, hot water, one hundred and forty degrees. Everything else can stay at eighty-six. If your machine has a sanitize setting, that is fine too, but read the dial and find the temperature in numbers, not pictures.

Rule two. The towel comes out of the drum within one hour. Not two. One.

This is the rule that fixed my house, and it costs nothing.

In 1994, in Sunnyvale, my friend Carol Whitfield, she is seventy now and lives in Sacramento, had a machine in a little room off the kitchen. She started a load before bed and took it out in the morning. Her towels were always a little sour. She blamed the water. She bought a new machine. The new machine did the same thing, because the machine was never the problem. The towels sat in a warm, wet drum for nine hours, and that drum was a small greenhouse.

After the wash, a towel is clean and wet, and it is also warm, and the door is closed. Bacteria do not need much. They need water, warmth, and one night.

The change: when the machine stops, the towels go out. If you cannot hang them right away, run the rinse and spin again, but do not leave them in a ball. In the rainy season here, in June, I hang them inside, in the room with the fan, and they dry in four hours. The fan costs less than a coffee.

And for us, after sixty, there is one more thing about this rule. We have a favourite towel. We use it after the shower, we hang it on the rail, and at eight in the evening it is still a little damp in the middle, so we use it again in the morning. A towel that never fully dries is never really clean, even if it was washed on Sunday. Two towels, used on alternate days, dry completely, and that costs nothing at all.

Rule three. Use one third of the soap you are using now.

I know how this sounds. More soap should be cleaner. It is not. The machine uses the same amount of water, and the extra soap has nowhere to go. It stays in the folds, in the hem, in the thick part of the towel where the loop is doubled. Then it dries into a film, and the film holds water, and the water holds the smell.

Mr. Okada sells linen and tenugui in the Nishiki market. He has a stall about two meters wide, with a wooden counter his father built. He keeps the ends of the bolts for me, the last half meter of linen, the cotton with the wrong dye. One afternoon I told him my kitchen cloths smelled sour even after washing. He picked one up, rubbed it between his fingers, and said: "Too much soap. It is not dirty, it is sticky."

For us, after sixty, there is a second reason. Our skin is drier, so we use more cream, and the cream goes onto the towel, and the cream is oil. Oil plus too much soap makes a film that never rinses out.

The change: fill the cap one third. If your load is small, half of that. And once a month, run the towels with no soap at all and a cup of white vinegar in the rinse. That is the reset.

Rule four. The machine has a rubber lip, and it is the dirtiest thing in your house.

I did not know this for years. Open the door of a front-loading machine and pull the rubber gasket back, all the way around, especially at the bottom. There is a grey film in there. That film is the same sour smell, concentrated.

My granddaughter Yui moved into a small apartment in Osaka in 2023 and called me because her clothes smelled after washing. She is twenty-two, she is careful, she cleaned everything. She had never looked at the gasket. We cleaned it with a toothbrush nobody uses and a cup of white vinegar, and the black water that came out made her laugh, and then made her quiet.

The change: once a month, a toothbrush and vinegar, two dollars. Leave the door open when the machine is empty. Always. And leave the soap drawer out to dry once a week.

Older machines are worse for this. The rubber hardens, it does not close tight, and a little water stays in the fold all week. You do not need a new machine. You need ten minutes a month and a chair to sit on, because bending down is the hard part, not the cleaning.

Rule five. No softener on towels. Vinegar instead.

Softener is made to coat fibre. That is what soft is, a thin layer. On a towel, that layer does two things. It stops the towel from taking water, and it traps the oil underneath it. Your towel feels lovely and dries nothing, and after a month it smells sour even though it is clean.

I bought a big jug of softener in Sunnyvale, the kind that lasts a year. In 2002, when we came back to Kyoto, I put it in the cupboard under the sink and did not use it. In 2003 I threw it away, and I did not buy another one for twenty years.

After sixty we like soft things. The skin is thinner, the cotton feels rough, and the softener feels kind. I understand this very well. But a towel is a tool before it is a comfort. Keep the softener for a sweater, if you must. Not for the towel that touches your face.

The change: half a cup of plain white vinegar where the softener goes. It costs about two dollars a bottle, and it does not smell of vinegar when it dries. It disappears. It takes the soap film out, and the towel comes out soft because it is actually clean.

And while we are in the kitchen: the dish cloth is the worst offender in the house. It touches everything, it stays wet on the counter, and then it goes into the machine with the bath towels. I wash them separately, in the hottest load, and I change them every day. A pack of ten plain cotton cloths is nine dollars at the market, and that is the nine dollars I told you about at the beginning. It is the best money in this whole video.

Rule six. One hour of morning sun, then shade.

This is the oldest rule in this house, and it is free.

My mother put everything on the balcony before ten in the morning. Not at noon, not in the afternoon. Before ten. She said the morning sun is clean and the afternoon sun is tired. I do not know if that is science. I know that the sun before noon comes in at a low angle, under the roof of the balcony, and it is strong enough to kill what grew in the wash, and it is not strong enough to fade the colours, because the cloth is not in the direct heat for long.

In June, when it rains for three weeks, there is no sun. Then I use the fan, and I turn the towels over after two hours. The rule is the same: moving air, and time.

The change: one hour in the morning sun, then move the towels to the shade. Do not leave wool in the sun. Wool goes into the shade from the first minute. And in winter, when it is thirty-five degrees inside this wooden house, the sun through the window is still warm enough for cotton. I put the towels out at nine and bring them in at eleven, because cold air with sun dries cotton better than warm air in a closed room.

Mrs. Kinoshita, my neighbour across the street, walks the Philosopher's Path every morning. Ten thousand steps, she says, and she says the same thing about her shoes that I say about towels: air them, do not bake them. She is eighty-one, and she has been right about most things for fifty years.

Rule seven. And this is the one nobody in America talks about.

Synthetic fabric keeps oil. Not water. Oil. A cotton towel releases the oil into hot water. A polyester fleece holds it, because polyester is plastic, and oil likes plastic. That is why Hiroshi's beige fleece, washed and clean, still said "three days" to Sachiko-san.

The change, for tonight, is small. After he wears it, it goes on the balcony for one hour, in the air, inside out. That is not the full fix.
