There is a piece of metal the size of a quarter that goes into the fire with every person who is cremated in the state of Texas. It has a number stamped on it. It goes in at the head end, and it comes out with what is left. In forty years, I have never once had a family ask me to look at it.

That little disc is the whole story. Because every fear people carry about the crematory door — that they mix people up, that you get somebody else's ashes, that nobody is watching — comes down to that disc and whether the man in that room is doing his job.

My name is Ernie Navarro. I have been a funeral director for forty years, most of them on the West Side of San Antonio, Texas. I started at thirteen in my father's funeral home in Analco, in Guadalajara, sweeping the chapel floor before school. I have buried close to seven thousand people. I have stood in crematories in two countries, at two in the morning, more times than I can count.

Families cry harder at that door than at the casket. I have seen it a thousand times. And I think I know why. Because at the casket, you can see. At that door, you cannot. The imagination takes over, and the imagination, mijo, is always worse than the truth.

So here is what I am going to do tonight. I am going to walk you through that room in order. From the minute the door closes to the minute somebody hands you a box and says, "Here is your mother." I am going to tell you what the machine is, what the fire does, what comes back, and what it weighs. And I am going to tell you the three things you are allowed to ask for that almost nobody asks for, because nobody ever told them they could.

And I am going to tell you one price you should never pay. Not one family in a hundred knows this, and it cost one lady in my office eleven thousand dollars before she walked in and sat down in front of me. I will get to her.

But first, the room. Let me show you the room the way I saw it the first time.

Nineteen eighty-seven, Guadalajara. I was twenty-nine years old, working with my father, and a family from Mexico City brought their father home to be cremated. My father could not go, so he sent me. Behind a wall off Calzada Independencia there was a little courtyard, a metal door painted green, and a man in a rubber apron smoking a cigarette by the door. He was maybe sixty. He had a coffee can on the windowsill full of cigarette butts.

I expected something. I do not know what. Smoke, maybe. A smell. What I found was a room like a mechanic's garage. Concrete floor. A machine the size of a delivery truck, brick and steel, with a door on the front like a bank vault, and a small window of thick glass, black with soot. A clipboard on a nail. A hose coiled on the wall. A broom in the corner.

That is the crematory. That is what is behind the door. Not a furnace out of a story about hell. A piece of industrial equipment, maintained by a man who has to change the bricks in it every few years, the same way a mechanic changes brakes.

In San Antonio I worked out of a building on Zarzamora for nineteen years. The crematory there was in a separate building in the back, cinder block, painted the color of sand. The man who ran it was named Gilberto. Thirty-five years in that room. He kept a radio on the shelf, tuned low, to a station that played only old songs, and he kept a folding chair by the door for the family, because he believed that if a family was going to come, they should not have to stand.

He told me once, "Ernesto, the machine does not care. That is why I have to."

That is the first thing to understand, and it is the thing that should calm you down. The fire does nothing. The fire is just heat. Everything that matters in that room is done by people, on paper, before the door ever closes.

Which brings me to the paperwork, and to the disc.

Before anybody is cremated in Texas, a person has to sign a cremation authorization. Not a form the funeral home fills out for you. A document that names who is authorized to make that decision, that lists what is going to happen, and that says what is to be done with the remains. And the state makes us wait forty-eight hours after the death. Forty-eight hours is not red tape. Forty-eight hours is the law giving somebody who is out of town a chance to get on a plane, and giving the county a chance to look at anything that does not sit right.

That paperwork follows the body into the room. It is on the clipboard on the nail. And the disc — the metal tag — has a number on it that matches the number on that paperwork. The tag goes in with the person. It goes in because metal does not burn. Not at fifteen hundred degrees. Not at eighteen hundred. That disc comes out the other side exactly the way it went in, only blackened, and it stays with the remains until they are sealed in front of you.

Now, before I go further, I want to try something with you. It takes fifteen seconds, and I promise it pays off later.

Stop the video. Say out loud how much you think a grown man's cremated remains weigh. Not the urn — the remains. Just say a number out loud. Six pounds? Twenty? Forty? Say it. Then start the video again.

I asked that question in my office for twenty years, to families, to apprentices, to my own son. Nobody gets it right. Not one person in ten. And the answer tells you more about what is behind that door than anything I could say.

So hold your number. Put it in your pocket. We are coming back to it.

Now let's talk about what goes in.

This is where families lose money, and it is where I have had the most arguments with people I like. Because there are two ways to go into that machine, and the difference between them is about four thousand dollars.

The first way is a casket. A beautiful one. Mahogany, or the metal ones with the little handles and the lining. Now, that casket is built to be lowered into the ground and to hold a body for a very long time. It is overbuilt for the fire. And when it goes in, the machine has to burn all of it — the wood, the glue, the fabric, the handles, the hardware — before it can get to the person inside. That is more fuel, more time, and for the family, a lot more money. I have seen families pay three and four thousand dollars for a casket that is going to be ash in two hours.

The second way is an alternative container. That is the term on the price list. What it means, in practice, is a rigid box, usually heavy cardboard, sometimes plywood, with a simple liner. It costs between seventy-five and two hundred and fifty dollars. It does the same job. It goes in, it burns, it is gone. The person is the same person.

The federal Funeral Rule says you have the right to buy the container you choose, and the funeral home has to tell you the price of the least expensive one they offer. They have to. It is not a favor. If a funeral home tells you that you must buy a casket for a cremation, they are either breaking the rule or they do not know it, and either way you should take your business somewhere else.

I want to say one more thing about that price list, because it is the most useful piece of paper in this whole business.

By federal law, every funeral home has to give you a General Price List, itemized, at the start of the arrangement, before you sit down and talk about anything. You can keep it. You can take it home. You can call them on the telephone and ask for prices, and they have to give them to you. This has been the law since nineteen eighty-four. Twenty years ago, half the homes in San Antonio did not want to give me that list, and I would stand there in the lobby, an old man in a suit, and say, "I will wait. Go get it."

So that is your test for tonight. Not a test about death. A test about paper. Call one funeral home in your city tomorrow and ask one question: "What is your price for a direct cremation, all in, including your crematory fee?" If they will not give you a number on the phone, you have learned something about them that no review will ever tell you.

Now. The door closes.

The chamber is called a retort. It is lined with firebrick, and it runs between fourteen hundred and eighteen hundred degrees Fahrenheit. For a grown adult, the process takes about two to three hours. Not twenty minutes. Families are always surprised by that. Two to three hours, and then another hour or more for the chamber to cool before anybody can open it, because everything inside is still hot enough to melt the tools.

The person goes in on a tray. The door comes down. There is a sound when it latches that I will not try to describe, because my description would not be fair to it. Then the burner comes on, and for a while the only thing you hear in that room is the roar of the flame and the radio.

Here is what the fire actually does. It is not destroying a person. The person is already gone. That is the part that took me years to understand, and I only understood it because of a woman named Rosa — my wife, thirty-four years a chaplain in hospice. She sat with more dying people than I ever buried. She used to say, "Ernesto, by the time they get to you, the hard part is over. All you are doing is returning the coat."

The fire returns the coat. The soft tissue goes first. Then the rest. What is left at the end is bone — the dense part, the part that held the weight of a whole life — and a small amount of mineral ash. Nothing else. No smoke that carries a person up a chimney. No soul in the flue. Just heat, and time, and bone.

And here is something families do not know they can ask for.

You can be there.

It is called a witness cremation. Not every funeral home offers it. Most do not advertise it. But in Texas, and in most states, you can ask to be present when the door closes, and many homes will let you. You can stand in that room. You can put your hand on the door. Some places let you press the button yourself. Gilberto used to stand at the back of the room with his hands folded and his eyes down, like a man in church.

I have seen a widow sing to a closed steel door. I have seen a son say the prayer his mother taught him in Spanish, the one he had not said since he was a boy. I have seen a family stand in a cinder block room on Zarzamora Street and be more at peace than they were in the chapel, because the chapel is for the living and that room, finally, was for him.

Ask for it. The worst they can say is no.

Do you have your number? The one you said out loud at the beginning?

The remains of a grown man weigh between four and eight pounds. Most of the time, closer to six. A woman, often a little less. A child, sometimes two. That is all. Everything a person ate, everything they carried, everything they were afraid of, all of it, six pounds on a steel tray.

And it is not ash. I want to be careful here, because this is the word that scares people. What comes out of the chamber is bone. Fragile, white, dry bone, in pieces, some of them the size of your hand. The machine does not turn a person into powder. It turns a person into bone.

Then the operator takes those pieces and puts them in a processor — a unit that works like a heavy-duty grinder — and reduces them to particles about the size of a grain of coarse sand. That is what goes in the bag. That is what goes in the urn. The industry invented a word for it, "cremains," because "ashes" is not accurate and "bone fragments" is not something a family wants to hear at the counter.

So now you know why I asked. Nobody guesses six pounds. Everybody guesses twenty, or forty, or one. Because we imagine that a person is a big thing. And the truth behind that door is that a person is a small thing, and a very heavy one, and the two do not weigh the same.

Now, the things that do not burn.

This is the part of the room that no brochure will show you, and it is the part that made me respect crematory operators more than anybody else in this business.

A body can carry things that the fire cannot take. A pacemaker, with a battery in it, will explode in that chamber. Not might. Will. That is why the law in most states requires that it be removed before cremation, and why the operator checks the paperwork and the body before the door closes. Titanium hips, steel pins, dental work, a bullet from a war in nineteen forty-four that a man carried for sixty years — none of that burns. It comes out in the tray with the bone, and it is separated, and it is disposed of according to the rules of that county.

Gilberto kept a coffee can in the back of his room, and in that can he kept the metal. Not for any morbid reason. He said, "Somebody's grandfather is in this can. I am not going to throw him in the trash."

There is another thing in that room that does not burn, and this one is harder to talk about, so I will say it plainly.

Sometimes the family does not come. Sometimes the person has been in a refrigerator for three weeks because nobody claimed them, and the county pays for the cremation, and the only people at the door are the operator and a clerk with a clipboard. I have done those. I have signed those. And I have stood in a parking lot afterward and smoked a cigarette I did not want, because there is something in the air on those days.

If you take one thing from this video, let it be this: put it in writing. Not because you are going to die tomorrow. Because the paperwork is the only thing that stands between your family and a county clerk who has never heard your name.

Now let's talk about money, because that is where the truth about this business lives.

A direct cremation in San Antonio, at an independent funeral home, runs between about twelve hundred and twenty-five hundred dollars. All in. That includes the transfer of the body, the paperwork, the permit, the crematory fee, and the container. The crematory fee by itself is usually three hundred to five hundred dollars, and a lot of homes do not put it on the first page of the price list. Look for it. It is your right to see it.

At one of the big chains, I have seen the same service priced at twenty-eight hundred. Same machine. Same fire. Same six pounds. The difference is a carpet and a coffee machine in the lobby.

And then there is the urn. Here is a number that makes me tired. An urn is a container. It holds six pounds. A simple one costs a hundred dollars, and a nice one costs three or four hundred. I have seen a family pay nine hundred dollars for an urn at a chain funeral home, and then put it in a closet for twenty years, which is what most families do with urns, and I am not criticizing them. I am telling you what it costs to be surprised at the worst moment of your life.

Now, Mrs. Delgado.

Twenty sixteen. She came into my office on a Tuesday with a folder under her arm. Her husband had died on a Sunday. She had gone to one of the big funeral homes first, because it was close to her house, and they had handed her a quote for eleven thousand dollars. Cremation. Eleven thousand dollars. She had the paper with her. She put it on my desk, and she said, "Mr. Navarro, I do not have eleven thousand dollars, and I do not want to borrow it from my children."

Now. The last point. What happens to what is left.

Because the door opens again. That is the thing nobody thinks about. The door opens, and there is a tray, and there is bone, and there is a metal disc with a number on it, and there is a bag.

Most families take the remains home. Some keep them for years. Some scatter them. Some take them to Mexico, and I have done that more times than you would think. If you are taking remains across the border, you need the death certificate and the cremation certificate from the funeral home, and you should put both in the same envelope as the urn, and you should carry the urn with you and not check it. I have seen a family put their father in a suitcase in the cargo hold of a bus, and I have never forgotten it.

If you scatter, do it somewhere you can return to. That is not a rule. That is forty years of listening to people.

And if nobody claims the remains — and this happens more than anybody in this business will admit — they stay on a shelf. Some states have laws about how long. Some funeral homes have a room.
