Last week I told you about the nine things families in San Antonio regret paying for. One of those nine I could not explain in a minute, so I saved it for today. It is the only one where the family pays twice, and the second bill arrives a thousand miles away, in a language they know, in a building they have never seen.

In 2019 the Reyes family, from here in San Antonio, paid nine thousand four hundred dollars to send their father home to Zacatecas. They signed on a Tuesday. They flew to the funeral on a Friday. And when they walked into the funeral home in Zacatecas, the man behind the desk handed them a second bill. Two thousand one hundred dollars. Storage, customs, the last hundred miles. Nobody had told them. Their father was already in the chapel. They paid.

I keep that bill in a folder in my desk. [emphatically] It is the most expensive piece of paper in this office.

My name is Ernie Navarro. I am sixty-eight years old. My father opened our funeral home in Analco, in Guadalajara, in 1958, and I started there at thirteen, sweeping the chapel and carrying the folder of papers for each burial. In 2000 I came to San Antonio and spent twenty-five years on the West Side, on Guadalupe Street, on Zarzamora, on Commerce, working almost entirely with Mexican families. I have buried close to seven thousand people. I have sent maybe four hundred of them home to Mexico. My wife Rosa spent thirty-four years at the bedside of dying people, and she taught me the part that no price list has: a family remembers everything you do in the first hour, and nothing you say after.

Tonight I am going to show you what happens between the signature and the church door in your town. Nine things. The last one is the money, and by then you will understand why it costs what it costs.

First, the word. In this business we call it repatriation. Repatriación. It sounds like a government word. It is not. It is a product, and it is sold like a product.

When a Mexican dies in the United States and the family wants him buried in his town, four things have to be true at the same time. The body has to be prepared. The paperwork has to be accepted by two governments. An airline has to carry it. And a funeral home on the other side has to receive it. Four hands. Four invoices. And here is the thing nobody explains at the counter: you are only talking to one of those four hands.

Number one. The folder.

When you sit down in San Antonio, the contract has one line that says shipping, or transfer to Mexico, or repatriation services. One line. Behind that line there are six documents, and each one is issued by a different office, on a different day, and if one is missing, the plane leaves without him.

I learned this in 2002, with my first one. Isidro was thirty-four years old, from a ranch outside Tepatitlán, in Jalisco. He was killed on I-35 on a Thursday morning, and by Friday his mother was on the phone from Mexico, and she did not ask me about the price. She asked me one question: when is he coming home. I told her I did not know. I had been in this country two years and I had never sent anyone back.

I drove the folder myself to the consulate on Navarro Street. I stood in line with a woman who was renewing her passport and a man who was registering his son. When I got to the window I was missing the certified death certificate, and the certificate takes ten days in this county, and nobody at my funeral home had told me that either. That is how I learned that the folder is the real work. The casket is the easy part.

Here is why it happens. The death certificate comes from the county. The embalming certificate comes from the funeral home. The transit permit comes from the state. The sanitary permit comes from the Mexican consulate. The container certificate comes from whoever sold you the casket. And the airline acceptance comes from the airline. Six offices. Six calendars. Nobody in that chain works for you. They all work for the chain.

So here is the change, and it costs nothing. Before you sign anything, ask for the checklist in writing, on one page, with the name of the office that issues each paper and the day it is expected. If the person across the desk cannot produce that page, you are not talking to a repatriation department. You are talking to a salesman with a phone.

And here is the test for tonight. While you watch this, go and find the folder. Any folder. The contract from a funeral home, the papers from a hospital, the envelope from the last time. Open it and look for one thing: the name of the funeral home in Mexico. Not the city. The name. If it is not written there, nobody has been named to receive him, and the second bill I showed you at the beginning of this video is already on its way.

Number two. The box inside the box.

[emphatically] This is the part that families never see, and it is the reason I wanted to make this video.

The casket you choose in San Antonio is not the box that crosses the border. Inside it goes a second container, a metal liner, and that liner is sealed. Hermetically sealed is the phrase. In the old days we soldered it shut in the back room with a small torch, and the smell of the solder stayed on my hands for the rest of the day.

In 2016 the Salas family came to me from a house on Zarzamora. Their mother had died at seventy-nine, and the whole family wanted her buried in Arandas, where she was born. We picked a casket together. Wood, light color, a simple cross on the lid. They paid for it. They saw it. They touched it.

Two weeks later, in Arandas, the family asked the funeral home to open the lid one more time, just for the grandchildren, just for a minute. And the man from the funeral home said no. He explained why: the metal liner inside had been sealed at the airport, and to open it you have to break the seal, and a broken seal means the body cannot be moved again, and the permit is void. The daughter called me that night. She was not angry about the money. She said, we paid for a casket, not for a box. And I did not have an answer for her, because in a way she was right.

Why does it happen. Because Mexico requires a hermetically sealed container for a body entering the country. It is not the funeral home being difficult. It is the sanitary regulation at the border, and it exists for a reason, and I am not going to pretend it does not. A body that travels for a week, through two climates, in the hold of a plane, has to arrive sealed.

But the regulation is not the problem. The problem is that nobody tells you at the counter what it means for your goodbye. You are buying a casket for a viewing that will happen in Texas. You are not buying it for the viewing in Mexico, because in Mexico there will not be one, not with the lid open.

So the change is one question, asked before you pay. Ask: is the sealed liner included in this price, and will the casket be sealed before or after the viewing here. If the answer is after, then you have your viewing in San Antonio, and you say your goodbye there, and you plan the rest around that. The liner itself runs between nine hundred and twenty-five hundred dollars depending on the casket. It is one of the lines most often buried inside the word shipping.

Number three. The consulate.

There is a document called the permiso sanitario de traslado de cadáver. The sanitary permit for the transfer of a body. Without it, the airline will not take him, and without the airline there is no repatriation. It is one sheet of paper, and it is the door.

In 2021 I had nine families at the same time. The consulate was working by appointment only, and the appointments were three weeks out. I had a man from a town near Ocotlán who waited twenty-two days. Every morning his widow called me at eight. She did not cry on the phone. She asked the same question every day: did they give you the date. And every morning I told her the same thing: not yet.

Why does it happen. The consulate is not slow to be cruel. It is checking a chain. They want the certified death certificate, the embalming certificate, the identification, and the certificate of the container. If the name on the death certificate has one letter different from the name on the passport, the chain stops. I have seen it stop for an accent mark. I have seen it stop because a man was registered as José with an accent in one paper and Jose without it in another, and it took eleven days and a notary to fix.

So the change is this. Find out who is taking the folder to the consulate, and on what day, and get that in writing before you pay the shipping line. It should be the funeral home. If they tell you it is your job, then you are the one standing in line, and you need to know that on the first day, not on the fifth. The consulate fee itself is small. The delay is what costs, because every day the body is in a cooler, somebody is charging for the cooler.

Number four. The embalming.

Now we get to the sentence I have heard more than any other in forty years. The law requires it.

In 2013 a woman came to my office, Mrs. Treviño, seventy-one years old, from a house near Woodlawn. Her husband had died at home, in his own bed, and she had called a funeral home before she called me. The man who came to her house told her that Texas law required embalming, and that it had to be done that same night, and that it cost fourteen hundred dollars. She paid it. She paid it with a check from a joint account that was about to be closed.

Texas law does not require embalming. Most states do not, with a few exceptions and time limits. What requires embalming is the airline, for an international flight, and the country that receives the body. That is a different law, in a different book, for a different reason.

Why does the sentence happen. Because it works. Because a widow at eleven at night is not going to ask which law. Because the price list is a piece of paper she has never seen, and the man at the door is the only authority in the room.

So here is the change. The Federal Trade Commission, the FTC, has a rule about this. The Funeral Rule. It says the funeral home has to give you an itemized price list, in person, and it says you can buy only the items you want, with one exception, the basic services fee. It also says they cannot tell you that embalming is required by law when it is not. If you are shipping to Mexico, embalming will be required, by the airline and by the receiving country, and that is fine. But ask the question out loud: required by whom. And ask to see the line on the price list.

Embalming for an international shipment in San Antonio runs from five hundred to twelve hundred dollars. If somebody quotes you fourteen hundred at your kitchen table at midnight, you are not paying for embalming. You are paying for the hour.

Number five. The flight.

Here is something that surprises almost every family I have worked with. He does not fly with you.

The body travels as cargo. Not in the cabin, not in the hold with the luggage, but as freight, on an air tray, which is a wooden base with a cardboard cover, strapped and labeled. It is loaded with the freight, and it is weighed like freight, and it costs like freight. And it does not get priority. If the plane is full, the freight waits.

In 2008 I had a casket scheduled from San Antonio to Guadalajara with a connection in Houston. The first leg was canceled for weather. The casket sat in the cargo facility in Houston for thirty hours. The family was already in Guadalajara. They had flown on Tuesday for a wake on Wednesday. They spent Wednesday at the cargo terminal at the airport, not at the funeral home, because nobody had told them that the body arrives at cargo and not at arrivals. Thirty hours. The mother sat in a plastic chair with her purse on her lap and did not eat.

Why does it happen. Because a casket weighs between two hundred fifty and four hundred pounds with the tray and the liner, and freight moves on a schedule that is not built for grief. The airline will tell you the flight is confirmed. Confirmed is not the same as loaded.

So the change is three things. First, ask for a direct flight if there is one, even if it costs more, because every connection is a chance to wait. Second, ask for the air waybill number before you pay the shipping line, and write it down. That number is how you find him. Third, ask what time he has to be at the airport, because it is usually three to four hours before the flight, and somebody has to drive him there, and that somebody is not the airline.

The air cargo itself runs from four hundred to fifteen hundred dollars, depending on the route and the weight. The air tray is separate, two hundred fifty to seven hundred. If the quote you have does not separate those two numbers, ask why.

Number six. The second funeral home.

This is the one that produced the bill I keep in my desk.

When the casket lands in Mexico, it does not go to the church. It goes to a funeral home in the city where the airport is, and that funeral home is a different business, in a different country, with its own prices, and it has never heard of the price list you signed in San Antonio.

The FTC Funeral Rule stops at the border. It protects you in Texas. It does not protect you in Jalisco, and the man behind the desk in Zacatecas is not breaking any law when he hands you a bill.

In 2019, the Reyes family, the one from the beginning of this video. Nine thousand four hundred dollars in San Antonio, paid in full, with a line that said all services included. And then two thousand one hundred dollars in Zacatecas. The funeral home there had stored the body for two days, had done the customs paperwork at the airport, and had driven the casket from the airport to the town, four hours. All of that was real work. All of it was legitimate. None of it had been named in the contract.

Why does it happen. Because the receiving home is usually a handshake. The funeral home in San Antonio knows a man in Guadalajara who knows a man in Zacatecas, and the price is agreed between them on the phone, and nobody writes it down, and the family finds out at the worst possible moment, in the room where they are supposed to be saying goodbye.

So the change is one page. Ask for the name, the address, and the phone number of the funeral home in Mexico that will receive him. Ask for their quote, in writing, itemized, before you pay the shipping line. And if the funeral home in San Antonio cannot give you that, ask them to give you the number of the one they use, and call it yourself. You are allowed to call. Nobody will stop you. In my experience, the receiving home will quote you a better price directly, because they are not splitting it with anybody.

Number seven. The last hundred miles.

The airport is not the town.

I have seen families pay for the whole trip and then discover that the town is four hours from the airport, and the last leg is a road, at night, in a hearse, and it is charged by the kilometer, and it is charged in cash.

In 2017 a family from a village outside Arandas, four hours from Guadalajara, told me the receiving home wanted six hundred dollars for the road. It was two in the morning when they called me. The casket was at the airport. The funeral home in the city wanted to be paid before it moved. I called the man I knew in Guadalajara, and he called a man he knew in Arandas, and by four in the morning it was arranged, and the price was four hundred and fifty. The difference was one phone call. The family had been awake for two days.

Why does it happen. Because the last leg is where the chain is thinnest. The receiving home in the city does not want to drive four hours at night on a road with no lights, so it subcontracts, and the subcontractor sets the price, and nobody negotiates for a family that is crying at an airport.

So the change is to plan the road before the flight. Ask, in writing, how many kilometers from the airport to the town, who drives, at what hour, and how much per kilometer. Ask if the price changes at night. It does. A night transfer can cost forty percent more than the same distance at noon. If the family can wait until morning, they should know that option exists, and nobody will offer it to them.

Number eight. The week in between.

Nobody tells you how long it takes. Five days. Seven. Sometimes ten. And nobody tells you where he is during those days.

He is in a cooler. In San Antonio, in a funeral home, waiting for the papers. Or in a cargo facility at the airport, waiting for a flight. Or in Mexico City, waiting for a connection to Guadalajara. He is not lost. He is in a system, and the system does not move faster because a family is suffering.

In 2023 Mrs. Campos called me every morning for six days. Her husband had died on a Sunday, and the flight was the following Saturday, and she called me at eight every morning to ask where he was. On the fourth day I did something I had never done. I gave her the air waybill number and the phone number of the cargo office, and I told her to call them herself. She called. They told her the casket was in Houston, and that it would fly the next morning. She called me after and said thank you. She said it was the first time in four days that somebody had told her something true.

Why does it happen. Because the chain has slack, and the slack is invisible, and the family fills it with the worst ideas. A family with no information imagines everything.

So the change is a calendar. Ask for the dates: the day of the embalming, the day the folder goes to the consulate, the day of the flight, the day of arrival, the day of the wake. Write them on a piece of paper and put it on the refrigerator. And ask one more thing: how many nights of wake will there be. Because in Mexico a wake is usually two nights, and after a repatriation it is often one, and the family does not find that out until they arrive.

Number nine. The total.

Now let me add it up, the way I add it up for a family in my office.

Embalming, five hundred to twelve hundred. The sealed liner, nine hundred to twenty-five hundred. The air tray, two hundred fifty to seven hundred. Air cargo, four hundred to fifteen hundred. The consulate and the documents, a few hundred. The receiving home in Mexico, eight hundred to twenty-five hundred. The road, two hundred to eight hundred. The casket itself, which is separate from all of this, from eight hundred to five thousand. And the wake in Mexico, if the family wants flowers and a chapel, another few hundred.

The real range for a family in San Antonio sending a parent to central Mexico is three thousand five hundred to nine thousand dollars, and at the top of that range you are paying for the casket. Not for the flight. The flight is almost never the expensive part. The box is.

In 2024 the Ramirez brothers came to see me. Three brothers, all in their fifties, all with good jobs. They had a quote of eleven thousand dollars from a big funeral home, and they had already put down a deposit. I asked to see the quote. It had one line: repatriation package, Mexico, eleven thousand. No breakdown. No name of the receiving home. No air waybill. Nothing.

We rebuilt it from zero, line by line, on my desk, with a pencil. It came to six thousand two hundred. And the difference was not a discount. The difference was that for the first time, somebody had written down what each part actually was.

Here is why it happens. Because a package is a price with the work hidden inside it. And when the work is hidden, you cannot check it, and when you cannot check it, you cannot refuse the part you do not want. That is not a Mexican problem or an American problem. It is how a product is sold when the buyer is in the worst week of her life.

So here is the change, and it is the most important thing in this video. Ask for the total on one page, every line separate, with the name of the person or the business that receives each payment. If a line says package, ask them to break it. If they will not break it, that is your answer.
