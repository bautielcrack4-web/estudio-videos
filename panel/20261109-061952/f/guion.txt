Hello, please come in. Sit near the window, where the light is soft. Let us take our time.

Before I say anything else, I want you to look at one small thing. Not at me. At your own collar. If you are wearing a shirt today, reach up with two fingers and feel the band that touches your neck. Is it soft and flat, like a ribbon that has given up? Or does it stand up with a small, gentle curve, as if it knows where it belongs?

This whole video is about that narrow band of cloth. One press of an iron, made in the right place, can make a plain shirt from the market look as though it was cut for you. Not a new shirt. Not an expensive one. The same shirt you already own, with its collar stand pressed and its roll set. That is the whole secret, and it takes about four minutes.

My name is Michiko. I am sixty-eight years old, and I have lived in Kyoto my whole life, in a narrow machiya house with paper screens and a garden that is mostly moss. For thirty-two years, from 1976 until 2008, I worked in a small clothes shop on Teramachi Street, a few doors from the old bookshop. I was not the owner. I was the woman at the back who steamed the garments, marked the hems, and listened while customers talked about their sons and their knees.

My mother taught me about collars when I was ten years old. It was the spring of 1968, before the new school year. The owner of the shop, Mrs. Kondo, set up a pressing board behind the counter every morning, and my mother helped her with the school shirts that parents brought in. I remember the smell of hot cotton, the small cloud of steam, and the sound of the iron being lifted and set down, lifted and set down, like a slow knock at a door.

One morning a boy's white shirt lay on the board, and the collar was curled at the tips like a dry leaf. Mrs. Kondo looked at it and said, "This shirt is not tired. It has only forgotten its shape." My mother turned the collar over in her hand, pressed the stand, rolled the leaf over two fingers, and held it there until it cooled. When she let go, the collar sat up on its own. I thought she had done magic. She had done something much simpler. She pressed the part nobody looks at, so that the part everybody looks at would behave.

[whispering] I can still hear her voice. Your clothes speak before you do. I did not understand it then. I understand it now.

A shirt collar is not one piece of cloth. It is two. There is the collar stand, the band that wraps around your neck, and there is the collar leaf, the part that folds down over your shoulders. Under the cloth there is a third layer, a stiffening called interlining, sometimes woven, sometimes fused with glue. The place where the stand and the leaf meet is called the roll. When people say a collar looks cheap, they are almost never talking about the fabric. They are talking about that meeting point. A good roll is a soft curve. A poor one is a hard crease, or no curve at all.

The stand was not always part of the shirt. In my grandmother's time, collars were often detachable, held on with a small button at the back and two at the front. A man might own one shirt and three collars, and a woman would turn a collar inside out when the first side wore thin. The stand existed so the collar could hold its shape under a jacket, and so the expensive part could be replaced without replacing the whole shirt. We have lost that habit, but we have kept the shape. When you press a collar today, you are repeating a gesture that people made every morning for a hundred years. There is comfort in that. A collar is a small thing, but it is one of the oldest pieces of engineering we still wear.

Now, let us do your test. This is the interactive part, and I would like you to stand up for it. Go to a mirror, button your shirt to the very top, and look at yourself from the side, not from the front. Most of us only ever look at our shirts from the front, and the front is the least honest view.

Here is the first check. Look at the fold line of your collar. If it is a sharp, flat razor line, like the edge of a folded paper, the collar has been pressed lying down, and it will cut across your neck and sit away from you. A well-set collar has a curve you could roll a marble through.

Here is the second check. Put one finger inside the collar at the back of your neck. One finger should fit, comfortably. If two fingers slide in, the collar is too large, and no iron will fix that. If no finger fits, the collar is too tight, and it will pull your shoulders forward by the end of the day.

Here is the third check. Turn your head slowly to the left and then to the right. Does the collar travel with your neck, or does it stand still while your neck moves inside it? If it stands still, the stand is too stiff or too high, and the shirt is wearing you instead of the other way around.

Most collars fail for one of three reasons: how they were washed, how they were stored, or how they were pressed. Washing is the gentlest problem. Storage is the slowest. Pressing is the one you can repair this afternoon.

Many shirts today have fused interlining, a thin layer of glue that holds the collar stiff. After twenty or thirty washes, that glue can release in small patches, and the collar begins to bubble. I am not going to promise you that an iron will bring a bubbling collar back to life. It will not. But a collar that has only lost its shape, which is most collars, can be brought back with steam, pressure, and patience.

First, prepare. Take the plastic collar stays out of their little pockets, if your shirt has them, and set them aside. Lay the shirt flat on the board with the collar open, face down. Fill a small bowl with water and wet a clean cotton cloth, then wring it until it is barely damp, not dripping. This cloth is your pressing cloth, and it matters more than the iron.

About those collar stays. Some are metal, cool and slightly heavy; some are thin plastic, and some are nothing more than a folded piece of cardboard. I have seen a man use the cardboard from a box of matches. If the stays are bent, replace them; a bent stay makes a bent collar, and no iron can straighten a collar around a crooked bone. If you lose one, you can cut a new pair from a plastic folder in five minutes, with the corners rounded so they do not pierce the cloth. Put them back only after the collar has cooled completely. Warm cloth will stretch around the stay and dry out of shape.

Set your iron to the setting for cotton, or one step below if the shirt is a blend. Let it heat for a full two minutes. A cold iron dragged across a collar does more harm than good, because we press with heat and lift, never with speed.

Now the first movement. With the collar face down, press the stand, the band, from the inside. Use the tip of the iron, and work in small sections, about the width of your hand. Press for two seconds, lift, move, press again. Do not slide the iron. Sliding stretches the cloth and makes it shine. Let the damp cloth do the work. You will hear a small hiss, and that is the sound of the fibers relaxing.

Press the stand until it is flat and crisp, all the way around, from one tip to the other. Do not press the leaf yet. This is the part almost everyone skips. They lay the collar flat and press the whole thing at once, which flattens the roll into a hard edge. The stand is the foundation. If the foundation is soft, the house leans.

Second movement. Now turn the collar over and fold the leaf down, the way it will sit when you wear it. Do not press the fold line at all. Instead, press the leaf gently from the tip inward, stopping about one centimeter before the roll. This leaves a small soft zone at the fold, and that soft zone is what makes the collar curl the way it should.

Third movement, and this is the one my mother did with her hands. While the collar is still warm, roll it over something round. A rolled towel works well, about the thickness of your wrist. Or use the sleeve board, if you have one. Hold the collar around the towel for a slow count of ten. Then let it cool in that shape, still curved. The fabric does not remember the iron. It remembers the shape it cools in.

If you do not own a pressing board, you do not need one. A thick towel folded twice on a table will do, and a rolled magazine under the collar gives you the curve. For the roll, a clean sock rolled into a tube is enough. The tools are not the secret. I have pressed collars with a pot of hot water and a cloth in a house where the electricity had gone out, and the collar still stood up. What matters is that the stand is flat, the leaf is not creased at the fold, and the collar cools in a curve. A machine cannot give you patience.

[emphatically] This is the sentence I want you to keep: press the stand flat, but let the leaf cool curved.

While the collar cools, let me tell you about a customer named Mrs. Fujita. It was 1974, and her son was taking the entrance examination for a high school across the river. She brought in a white shirt that had been washed many times and had lost all its lift. She could not afford a new one that month. My mother spent eleven minutes on that collar, and when the boy tried it on, his mother put her hand over her mouth. The shirt had not changed. His face had changed, because the frame around it had finally stood up.

That is what a collar does. It is the frame of your face. A painting can be ordinary, but if the frame is crooked, the eye cannot rest. And if the frame is quiet and straight, the eye goes to the painting.

Now, the check before you wear it. This is the one small habit I promised you at the beginning. Button the shirt to the top and stand in front of the window, not the mirror, because daylight is honest. Look at the collar from the side. The tips of the leaf should point slightly downward, not upward, and the fold should be a soft curve.

Then do this. Raise both arms above your head, slowly, and lower them again. Watch the collar. If it lifts away from your neck like a lid, the stand is too stiff, and you need to steam it once more and let it cool curved. If it stays close and returns to its place, the shirt is ready.

One more thing. Turn your head to the side and look at the gap between your collar and your jaw. If you can see a dark line of shadow all the way around, the collar is standing too far from the neck. If there is no gap at all, it is too close. A collar should sit like a hand resting on a shoulder, present but not pressing.

Here is a check for the days when you wear a jacket. Put the jacket on over the shirt and stand in front of the mirror. At the back of your neck, the collar should rise about one centimeter above the jacket. If it disappears entirely, the jacket is sitting too high or the collar stand is too short, and your neck will look shorter than it is. If it rises two or three centimeters, the collar is too tall for that jacket, and it will look like a flag. The eye reads that small edge of white almost before it reads the face. My mother called it the little line. She said a shirt without its little line was a letter without a stamp.

Let me talk about shine. You have seen it, a flat gray patch on a dark shirt, usually near the collar tips. Shine comes from three things together: heat, pressure, and synthetic fiber. The iron polishes the surface, and once that surface is polished, it stays polished. The prevention is the damp cloth and the lift-and-press movement. Never rest a hot iron on a collar while you answer the telephone. I have seen more shirts ruined by a telephone call than by a washing machine.

If a shine has already appeared, dampen a cloth with equal parts water and white vinegar, lay it over the patch, and press lightly with steam for a few seconds. This can soften the look of old shine. It will not remove deep damage, and I will not pretend otherwise. Prevention is the quiet work.

Not every fabric asks for the same hand. Poplin is smooth and tight, and it takes a beautiful press, but it also shines if you hurry. Oxford cloth is rougher, with a small basket weave, and it forgives a heavy hand; press it with a little more steam and let it cool longer. Chambray behaves like a soft denim and wants almost no pressure at all. A flannel shirt should be pressed only on the inside, with a cool iron, or the nap will flatten and never come back. In 1978, a woman brought me a poplin blouse that her sister had lent her, and the collar looked as if it had been polished with a spoon. We steamed it with a cloth and vinegar, and it improved, but the shine never fully left. Since then, I have pressed every poplin collar as if it were a page I did not want to tear.

My friend Mr. Yamamoto, a tailor in Gion, told me in 1982 that a collar should be pressed as if you were writing your name with the iron. Small strokes, a firm hand, and a clear beginning and end. He pressed collars for forty years, and he never once pressed one in a hurry. He said hurry leaves a mark that the cloth keeps longer than the wearer keeps the shirt.

What if the collar sits well but the front still gapes? Sometimes the problem is not the press at all. It is the button. Look at the top button and the buttonhole. On many ready-made shirts, the button sits one or two millimeters too far from the neck. Moving it two millimeters toward the edge can close that gap without touching the collar. It is a small job, and a tailor may charge about five hundred yen for it. If you sew, you can do it at home in ten minutes with a needle and strong thread.

If the gap is larger, a tailor can also move the buttonhole slightly, or add a tiny hidden hook and eye under the collar. These are old shop tricks, and they cost very little. What they cannot do is fix a shirt that is simply the wrong size. If the collar is two centimeters too large, no button and no iron will make it right. Let it go, and use it for gardening.

Before you buy a shirt, do one small thing in the shop. Pinch the collar between your thumb and forefinger. You should feel three even layers, with no lump at the edge and no hard glue near the fold. Then bend the leaf gently back and let it go. A good collar returns to its curve; a poor one stays folded like cardboard. Look at the points: they should be even, and the stitching should be straight, about three millimeters from the edge. None of this costs anything. The shopkeeper may watch you, but that is all right. My mother used to say that a woman who checks a collar is a woman who will keep the shirt for ten years, and shopkeepers respect that.

Now, starch. A little starch can help a collar hold its shape, but too much makes it brittle, and brittle collars crack at the fold. Spray starch lightly on the stand only, never on the leaf. Let it dry for a minute before you press. One can of spray starch costs about three hundred yen and lasts a whole season. My mother used a thin paste of rice starch and water, brushed on with her finger. I still prefer the spray, but I think of her hands every time I use it.

Washing matters more than most people think. Wash shirts in cool water, and do not wring the collar. If you scrub it against itself, you break the fibers at the fold, and that is where holes begin. Use a soft brush on the inside of the stand where it touches your skin. Rinse well, because soap left in the interlining will yellow with age.

Dry a shirt on a hanger in the shade, with the collar buttoned and the top button left open, so the stand dries straight. Do not hang it in strong sun; the sun bleaches and weakens the cloth. And please, do not tumble dry a good shirt. The heat of the dryer is what produces most of the bubbles and the small puckers I see on collars today.

When you store a shirt, do not stack heavy sweaters on top of the collar. Fold the shirt gently at the back, or hang it with space around the shoulders. The collar should have room to keep its curve. Small things, done every day: a little space, a little patience, and the shirt lasts another five years.

Here in Kyoto, we have a rainy season in June and July that my grandmother called the season of small dampnesses. The air holds water, and a shirt that is put away even slightly damp will grow a smell that no washing can fully remove. In 1993, a customer named Mrs. Sato brought me a linen shirt that had been folded in a drawer through the whole of tsuyu. The collar had a green line along the fold. We washed it, dried it in the shade, and pressed it, and the line faded, but it stayed. Now I say this: in the rainy season, iron the collar before you store the shirt, and leave it unfolded on a hanger for a day. Dry cloth is patient cloth.

When you travel, fold the shirt with the collar face up and roll the collar gently around a soft sock. Do not fold the collar in half. When you arrive, shake the shirt out and hang it in the bathroom while you take a warm shower; the steam will do half the work. Then press the stand the next morning, if there is an iron, and set the roll with your hands. A collar remembers the last shape it cooled in, so give it a good one before you leave.

Now I would like to ask you a small favor. If this way of pressing a collar has been useful, please press the like button, and subscribe if you would like to come back and sit with me again. It helps this quiet little channel reach someone else who might need it. There is no need to hurry. The button will wait.

Before I finish, I want to give you the last piece, the one I saved. All of this pressing means nothing if the shirt is put on in a rush. The collar sits on your neck, and your neck sits on your spine. If you dress standing in a hurry, with your shoulders lifted and your chin down, the collar will sit crooked no matter how well you pressed it.

At the shop, we had a mirror by the door, and I used to watch women put on their coats. The ones who looked elegant were not the ones with the most expensive clothes. They were the ones who paused for one breath before they looked at themselves. One breath, shoulders down, chin level, and then the glance. That small pause did more than any iron.

So this is your habit for today. Before you put on the shirt, take one slow breath. Button it from the bottom up. Then look at the collar from the side in the daylight, and check the roll with two fingers. If the curve is soft and the stand is straight, you are ready for the day.
