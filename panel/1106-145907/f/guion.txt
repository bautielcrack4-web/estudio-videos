[emphatically] Carol is seventy. She flew eleven hours from Sacramento to Kyoto, and she stood in my doorway in April with two suitcases and one leopard blouse.

I looked at what she had on her body, and I said: Carol, we are changing six things before dinner.

Not because she is bigger than me. She is not. Not because she bought cheap clothes. Two of those pieces cost more than anything I own. It was the color at her face, the shine of the fabric, and the way the sleeve sat on a woman who had been sitting in a plane for eleven hours. Those three things put years on a face faster than any cream.

And the last thing I swapped, nobody in America has ever heard of it. It costs eight dollars. She wore it home.

Stay with me, because at the end you will know exactly what to change tonight, with the clothes already in your closet. You do not need to buy one thing.

My mother was Fumiko Arai. She was a kitsuke-shi. She dressed brides in Gion for forty years, from 1962 until she stopped, and she put kimono on women the way another woman puts on a coat. She never once looked at a bride's body. She looked at her face. And I lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002. I did it the American way too. I bought the puffy jacket. I washed everything hot. I had a closet full of black. So when I open Carol's suitcase, I am not guessing. I know both sides of this.

Now. Carol arrived on the third of April. The rain had just stopped, the Philosopher's Path was still pink, and she came up the street with her hair flat from the plane and that leopard blouse she gave me in 2002 and that I mailed back to her in a box with a note last year.

She was proud of that blouse. She had it dry-cleaned before she flew. She told me it was her travel blouse.

I did not say anything at the door. I made tea. And then we went to the mirror in the tansu room, and I showed her what I saw. Tonight you can do the same thing, and you do not need me in the room.

Number one. The black turtleneck she wore under her jacket.

Carol loves black. Every woman I met in California loves black, and I understand why. It is safe. It does not show the coffee. It goes with everything, and nobody looks at you.

But get close. Black under a face after sixty is not a neutral color, it is a shadow. Look at the plane photograph she sent me from the airport, the one Yui put on her phone. When she sent it, she wrote, I look tired. She did not look tired. She looked gray. The black turtleneck and the white light of that airport were pulling every bit of color out of her cheeks, and she blamed her face for it.

Here is the test, and it takes one minute. Tonight, take a black shirt and a soft blue shirt, and stand at a window with daylight on your face. Not the ceiling light, not the bathroom light, the window. Hold the black under your chin, and look. Then the blue. Do not look for which one is prettier. Look at your neck, right under the jaw. The black makes a little gray line there. The blue takes it away.

That is the whole trick. It is not that black is bad. It is that black belongs near your hands, your waist, your feet. Near the face it needs a friend between it and your skin: a collar in cream, a scarf in soft color, a shirt in slate blue.

So I gave her one of mine. A linen shirt in slate blue, washed a hundred times, soft as a handkerchief. In Kyoto a shirt like that is about twenty-two dollars at the market, and it does not shine. [whispering] Carol put it on and went back to the mirror and stood there a while.

Number two. The mustard scarf.

Carol chose that scarf herself. We had talked about colors on a video call in February, remember, with the photo on the table, and she went to a shop in Sacramento and bought a scarf the color of a school bus. She was so pleased with herself. She wore it on the plane, wrapped twice around her neck, and in the airport photograph it looked like a warning light.

Mustard is a beautiful color. On a young face it is wonderful. On a woman of seventy who has been on a plane, with no sleep and no lipstick, it turns her skin yellow, and yellow reads as ill.

I took her to the market on Nishiki. Mr. Okada has had his stall there since before I was married, and he keeps the wool for me in a paper bag every winter. He pulled out a scarf in coral, a soft rose with no shine, and Carol held it against her face and started to laugh, because the woman in the little mirror at the stall looked awake again.

Twenty-four dollars. That is all. I am not telling you to throw away your mustard scarf. Wear it with a white shirt, and let it sit lower, near the collarbone, and it will be fine. It is the two wraps up under the chin that are the trouble.

Number three. The leopard blouse.

Now I have to be careful, because that blouse is a piece of our friendship. Carol gave it to me in 2002, when I left Sunnyvale, and I kept it for years, and then I gave it back to her with a note because it was polyester and it made me smell like a hot car. She laughed when she opened the box. She did not laugh when I told her to leave it in Kyoto.

It was on the bed in the tansu room, and the afternoon sun came in through the shoji and hit it, and she saw it too. That shine. Polyester does not have a soft shadow. It has a glare, like a plastic bag, and a glare next to the face is the most aging light there is.

So we made a trade. She left it with me, and I gave her one of my linen blouses instead, in oat, with the soft collar. Mr. Okada had the cloth, a woman on Imadegawa sewed it, and it cost thirty dollars. Carol wore it to dinner that night with the coral scarf.

If you have a shiny thing you love, you do not have to give it away either. Wear a matte shirt underneath it and let the shiny thing be the jacket, not the skin.

Number four. The puffy jacket.

I know this jacket. I owned this jacket. It is the one that folds into its own pocket, and every woman I know in California has one in the car, and they are warm. They are very warm.

But look at what it does. It is a black plastic bag with arms. It crushes in the suitcase and comes out with creases that never leave. It makes a noise when you move. And on a woman of seventy it makes the shoulders round and the neck disappear, and a neck that disappears makes a face look heavier than it is.

In Kyoto it is also not enough. A machiya like mine is thirty-five degrees inside in winter, and this house is wood, and the cold comes up from the tatami. What works here is a thin cardigan in wool, worn under the coat, so the warm air stays near the body and the shoulder keeps its line.

I found hers at the flea market at Kitano Tenmangu on the twenty-fifth, that is the day the market is on, in oatmeal wool, thin as a shirt. Thirty-five dollars. She wore it on the train to Osaka three days later and slept in it.

The check tonight: put on your jacket and stand sideways at the mirror. If your neck has gone, and your shoulders have gone up around your ears, the jacket is doing that, not your body.

Number five. The travel pants.

Carol had on those black pants with the wide elastic waist, the ones that are sold for flying. She loves them. She says they are the only thing she can sit in for eleven hours.

The problem is not the elastic. The problem is the fabric, which is thin and shiny and catches every light in the airport, and the leg, which narrows at the ankle, so the whole line of the body goes down and stops. A woman of seventy wants a line that goes down and keeps going.

We went to the tailor on Imadegawa, the small shop two doors from the tofu place, and she made Carol a pair in cotton with a flat front and a wide leg, in a color like dark ink. Twenty-eight dollars for the cloth, fifteen for the work. Carol wore them on the plane home and told me she slept in them, and they did not shine once.

You can do this with what you own. Take the pants you fly in and check one thing at the mirror: does the leg fall straight from the hip, or does it stop at the ankle. If it stops, it is cutting you in half.

Now, before the last one. If this is useful to you, press the like button, it helps Yui and me more than you know, and subscribe if you want the next one. Because the next one is the suitcase.

[excited] Number six. The one nobody in America knows. The haramaki.

Carol had never seen one. It is a band of cotton knit, about ten inches wide, that you wear around your middle, over the shirt, under everything else. That is all it is. There is no technology in it and there is nothing to plug in.

My mother put one on every bride in Gion, under forty pounds of kimono, in a house with no heating, in February. I have worn one every day since I was fifty. In the summer I wear a thin one in cotton; in the winter, wool.

Why it matters on a plane: a plane is cold at the waist, and a woman of seventy sits for eleven hours with a cold middle, and then she stands up at Kansai and her back is stiff and she thinks the trip did this to her. It did not. The cold did. The haramaki keeps the middle warm, so the rest of the body can relax, and a body that is relaxed stands straighter.

Eight dollars at the market. Mr. Okada sells them folded in a basket next to the tenugui, in navy, in brown, in a deep red. Carol bought three. She wore one home under the linen shirt, and she called me from Sacramento two weeks later and said, Michiko, I have not taken it off.

And that is the whole list. Six things. Black at the face, mustard at the chin, shine next to the skin, a jacket that eats the neck, a pant that stops at the ankle, and a cold middle.

Carol did not buy a new wardrobe in Kyoto. She opened her suitcase on the tatami, we put six things in a paper bag for the flea market, and we found the rest in my closet and in Mr. Okada's stall. The total was one hundred and thirty-four dollars, and half of that was the tailor.

So do not go shopping tonight. Open your closet. Take out one black thing and one soft-colored thing and stand at the window for one minute. That is the whole method. My mother used to say: your clothes speak before you do, so be careful what they say. She also said: if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you.

Carol stood straight at the train station when she left. That is all I wanted.

Tell me in the comments: what color is closest to your face right now, this minute, as you are reading this? Yui reads them to me with my tea, and I answer every one.

And next Sunday, we open the suitcase. The six things that are not going back to Sacramento, and the one thing Carol cried about, on the tatami, with the rain coming back. I will see you there.
