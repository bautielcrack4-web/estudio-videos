Line one. The box.

Here's what nobody explains at the arrangement table. The casket is the biggest line on the bill, and it is the least important thing in the room. I have watched families spend eight thousand dollars on a casket and then ask me, whispering, if we can do the service on a Tuesday, because the money ran out.

The funeral rule in this country — the FTC Funeral Rule, federal law since 1984 — says the funeral home has to give you a written casket price list, and they have to give it to you when you walk in, before they show you anything. Not after. And it says something else, and this is the part they don't say out loud: you can buy a casket somewhere else, and the funeral home cannot charge you a handling fee for it. That's in the rule. Sixteen C F R, part four fifty-three. If they tell you there's a fee, they're breaking the law, and you can call the FTC and say so.

The casket I want is a cloth-covered particle board box. Solid. Square. It seals with a simple gasket, it holds a hundred and ninety pounds of a man who ate too much pan dulce, and it costs eleven hundred and ninety-five dollars. Same box the funeral home buys for six hundred and marks up.

And here's the other thing. Every funeral home will offer you a protective casket. Rubber gasket, locking mechanism, sometimes a warranty that says it will not let water in. Six hundred, sometimes nine hundred dollars more. I have opened graves at ten years. I'll tell you about that in the next video. That gasket did not protect anything. What it does is hold the moisture inside. The embalmer knows it. The salesman knows it. It is the oldest upsell in this business, and it gets sold with a straight face to a woman who is crying.

So. Line one: eleven hundred and ninety-five dollars. Not eight thousand. Not four. [emphatically] Eleven ninety-five.

Tonight, before you go to sleep. If you have a funeral home in mind — your mother's, your grandmother's, the one down the street — call them and ask one question. Can I bring my own casket? If they hesitate, you have your answer about the rest of the list too.

Line two. The hole.

In Texas, and in most of this country, the cemetery will not let you put a casket in the ground by itself. They require an outer container. A grave liner, or a vault. And this is where a family that was careful with the casket gets taken on the ground.

A liner is a concrete box with no bottom. It holds the earth so the grave doesn't sink. In San Antonio, a liner runs around a thousand to fourteen hundred dollars. A vault is a sealed concrete box, sometimes lined, sometimes with a lid that locks, and it runs twenty-five hundred to thirty-five hundred. The difference between them, in the ground, after twenty years, is nothing you will ever see. The cemetery requires a container. It does not require the expensive one. That's the cemetery's rule, not the law.

I asked a cemetery manager on Zarzamora — a man named Rubén, this was maybe fifteen years ago — I asked him straight out. Rubén, what did you put on your own mother? He looked at me and he said, the liner. And then he sold vaults all day.

My line two: the liner. Eleven hundred dollars. And I want it bought from a third party, not from the cemetery, because the cemetery's price is not the only price. Ask for two quotes. You're allowed.

And the hole itself — the opening and closing, the interment fee — that's separate, and that's the cemetery's price, not the funeral home's. In San Antonio, a weekday grave opening runs around twelve hundred. Saturday costs more. Ask. Nobody asks.

Line three. The embalming.

I'm going to say this slowly, because I have had this argument at the arrangement table for forty years and I have never once lost it. The FTC Funeral Rule requires every funeral home in this country to tell you, in writing, that embalming is not required by law in most cases. Not required. They hand you a piece of paper that says it, and then they say the word recommended, and your hands go cold, and you sign.

Texas does not require embalming. What Texas requires is that the body be kept cool until the burial. That's it. Refrigeration in a funeral home costs about three hundred and fifty dollars for a few days. Embalming costs seven hundred to a thousand, depending on the home.

Now, I am not against embalming. That's not cheap. That's love, and it has a price, and it's a fair price.

But that is not what I want for me. I want the lid closed. I want my family to remember me at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee and my reading glasses pushed up on my head. Not in a box under a light. Closed casket. Refrigeration. No embalming. Three hundred and fifty dollars.

The rule here is simple. The price list is not a menu where you have to order the combination. It's a list. You point at what you want, you say no to the rest, and they have to give it to you itemized. That's the whole law. That's what 1984 was for.

Now, parking lot stuff. The paperwork.

Nobody talks about this part, and it's the part that keeps a family up at night for six months after everybody has gone home.

You are going to need death certificates. Certified copies. The funeral home orders them for you and charges twenty to twenty-five dollars each, and they will tell you to get ten. Get twelve. You need one for the bank, one for each insurance policy, one for the title company, one for the mortgage, one for Social Security, one for the pension, one for the credit union, and one you keep with your own papers forever, in a plastic sleeve, because you will lose the others.

In Bexar County you can order them yourself from the county clerk once the funeral home files the certificate, for less than half that price. It takes about three weeks. You have three weeks. Nobody is going to die from the wait.

The disposition permit is the paper that lets the cemetery open the ground. The funeral home files it. You don't file anything. But you do have to sign it, and Rafa, this part is for you. You sign it where it says informant, and you don't let anybody hurry you through it, and you read the name on it out loud before you put the pen down. I have seen a wrong name on a permit. I have seen it twice in forty years. Both times it was a family that was rushed.

And Social Security. The funeral home used to report the death for the family. Many still will. Many won't, and nobody tells you which kind you have. So ask at the arrangement table: are you going to report this death to Social Security for me, and will you give me the confirmation? If the answer is no, you call yourself, and you have thirty days to return the last payment. There is also a two hundred and fifty-five dollar death benefit, and a payment that has to go back. Write it on the paper. On the back: who calls Social Security.
