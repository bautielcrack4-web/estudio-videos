[emphatically] Last time I told you what happens in the cemetery at night, after everybody leaves on Day of the Dead. Tonight I want to tell you what happens in the first two days. In the house. Before the cemetery, before the church, before anybody puts on a black suit.

And I want to tell you the number that still bothers me after forty years. Three hours.

Three hours is what an American funeral home gives you with your own dead. Three hours, in a room with carpet and a sign on the wall that says visiting hours, and then the doors close and somebody locks them. That is not a law anywhere in the United States. That is a custom. And it is also, if I'm going to be plain about it, a business decision.

In Mexico, in my father's funeral home in Analco, in Guadalajara, we gave the family the whole night. And then the next night. And then the morning.

I have been a funeral director for more than forty years. I buried close to seven thousand people, most of them in San Antonio, Texas, on the West Side, on Guadalupe Street, on Zarzamora, on Commerce. I am the son of Don Refugio Navarro, who opened Funerales Navarro in nineteen fifty-eight, two blocks from the church of San Sebastián de Analco. My wife, Rosa, has been a hospice chaplain for thirty-four years.

So I know both sides. I know what a Mexican family wants in the first forty-eight hours, and I know exactly which of those things an American funeral home will tell you no about. There are nine. Nine things. And I'm going to give you all nine with the price of each one, because the Federal Trade Commission makes funeral homes publish those prices and most families never ask to see the paper.

Before the nine, I want to do a little test with you. It takes ten seconds and you don't have to write anything down.

Think of the last wake you went to in your family. Not a funeral home you drove past. A wake where you knew the person. Now count with me.

One. Was there food in the same room as the casket? Not in a hall down the corridor. In the room, on a table, next to the flowers, with a plate of pan dulce and a pot of coffee.

Two. Did somebody fall asleep in a chair at three in the morning and nobody moved them?

Three. Did a child sit on somebody's lap and touch the hand of the person in the casket?

Four. Did somebody open the lid again after it had already been closed once?

Five. Did somebody sing? Not a recording. A person, with a guitar or without one.

[emphatically] If you counted three or more, you grew up in a Mexican wake, and what I'm going to describe tonight is going to feel like your grandmother's living room. If you counted zero, you have only ever been to American funerals.

Number one. In Mexico, the body comes home.

In the United States, when somebody dies, the funeral home picks up the body within the hour. Sometimes before the family has finished crying in the kitchen. That body goes to a building, into a preparation room, then into a refrigerator, and the family sees it again at a scheduled hour, in a scheduled room, with a printed schedule on the wall.

I want to tell you about the Robles family. March of nineteen ninety-eight, two blocks from San Sebastián de Analco. The grandmother died at home, in her own bed, at four in the morning. By nine the casket was in the living room. They moved the television out to the patio, turned the dining table sideways against the wall, and put the casket where the table had been, in front of the window with the lace curtain.

That room was where she ate for fifty years. That is the entire point. The family did not go to her. She came to them.

Why does the American funeral home not do this? Two reasons, and only one of them is real. The one they tell you is that they need the facility and the refrigeration. The real one is that the building earns money and the living room does not. The chapel has a price. The visitation room has a price. A living room on the West Side does not, and you cannot bill for it.

The Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule gives you something here. Every funeral home in the United States has to hand you a General Price List, in writing, at the beginning of the conversation, before you have told them what you want. You do not have to buy anything to see it. If a funeral home will not give you that paper, you walk out of that building.

On that paper you will see the basic services fee, which in most of the country runs between two thousand and twenty-five hundred dollars. The transfer of remains, three to four hundred. The use of the chapel or visitation room, four hundred to eight hundred for one evening. That is what a living room would have cost you. Zero.

Tonight's check, number one. Walk through your own house and find the room. Not because you want to be morbid. Because the night it happens nobody in that family can think, and the person who knows where the casket goes is the person who already thought about it.

Number two. Nobody closes the doors at nine.

In Mexico the wake does not have hours. It has a rhythm. The first night the neighbors come after dinner and stay. The men are outside with coffee, the women are inside with the rosary, the children asleep on two chairs pushed together. At two in the morning somebody tells the story of the time the dead man fell off a roof, and everybody laughs, and then everybody cries, and that is not disrespect. That is the whole thing working.

In nineteen ninety-nine my grandmother died in Analco, and my father, already over seventy and walking with a cane, sat in that room the entire night. He would not go to bed. He put a candle on the floor by her head and did not let it go out. Two doors down there was a mariachi practicing, and around one in the morning they came over and played Amor Eterno for her. Nobody paid them. They just came.

The American side. Guadalupe Street, two thousand eleven, the Salas family. The funeral home closed at nine. The family was still arriving at nine-thirty, because in that family people work until nine. The director locked the front door and the family stood in the parking lot, in the dark, next to their cars, talking about their mother. They had a wake in a parking lot because the building had a schedule.

Why does it happen? Because in the American model the room is a rented asset, and an asset has to be turned over. Two visitations in one evening, one at six and one at eight, is better business than one family all night. And because the staff has to go home. In Mexico the staff is the family. In the United States the staff is a payroll.

What can you change? Ask, in writing, before you sign: what hours does this room have, and what happens if my family is still here at ten. Some funeral homes will give you the room until ten or eleven for three to six hundred dollars more. Some will let a family stay all night in a private room. Ask. If they say our policy does not allow it, ask for the policy in writing. Half the time there is no policy. There is only a habit.

Tonight's check, number two. Ask your family one question: if it happened tonight, who would stay all night, and who would bring the coffee at two in the morning. Write down two names. That is your night shift.

Number three. Food in the same room as the dead.

In the United States, in almost every funeral home I worked in, you cannot bring food into the chapel. No coffee, no tamales, no pan dulce. There may not be a sign, but there is a rule.

In Mexico the food is not a detail. The food is the answer to the question nobody asks out loud, which is: what do we do with our hands. You cannot just sit. So the aunts make coffee, the neighbors bring tamales at eleven at night, and by morning forty people have eaten in that house and every one of them has done something.

I had a family on Commerce Street, two thousand seven. Mrs. Peña, eighty-one years old, had made tamales for the church kitchen for thirty years. Her daughters brought two coolers of tamales to the funeral home and the director told them no food in the chapel. So the daughters sat in the parking lot with the coolers and served tamales out of the trunk of a car. Two hundred people ate tamales out of the trunk of a car, next to a dumpster, because a building had a rule about carpet.

Why? The plain answer is carpet. Carpet costs money. A stain costs money. And a funeral home that lets one family eat has to let the next one eat, and then the chapel smells like food, and the next family walks in expecting it. So the rule protects the asset.

What can you change? Ask on the first visit: can we have food, and where. A lot of them have a reception room or a family room where food is allowed. If they don't, and the weather is decent, ask about the patio. Here is the number: a reception room in a funeral home rents for four hundred to nine hundred dollars. The same food, cooked by the same aunts, in a church hall, rents for a hundred to two hundred fifty. Same tamales.

Tonight's check, number three. Write down the names of three people who would bring food. Not the food. The people. Because the food shows up if the people are asked, and it does not show up if nobody asks.

Number four. The children stay.

This is the one American funeral homes get wrong in a way that hurts, and I want to say it carefully.

In a Mexican wake the children are in the room. They run between the chairs. They fall asleep on the floor under the table with a coat over them. They touch the hand, or they don't, and nobody makes them. And years later, when they are forty, they remember that night, and they remember that death was in the house and the house did not fall down.

In the United States the children are usually taken to a neighbor's house. Sometimes they're told the grandmother went on a trip. And the funeral home is built for that: quiet, carpeted, a room in the back for the kids, and nobody comfortable with a six-year-old asking a question out loud in the chapel.

Zarzamora, two thousand fourteen. A little boy, six years old, his grandmother. His mother kept him home the first day, and then his aunt brought him on the second day, and he sat under the chairs at the back with his shoes off and fell asleep. The director came over twice to ask if someone could take him outside. [emphatically] Outside. The child was asleep under the chair of the woman who raised his mother.

Why does it happen? Part of it is liability, and part of it is that American funeral homes are designed for adults in a state of composed grief, and a child breaks the composition. A child asks: is she cold. A child asks: can I give her my rock. And the building has no answer for that.

What can you change? If you are the parent, you decide. Not the director. You decide whether your child comes and for how long, and you prepare them before you walk in: this is what she will look like, this is what the room will smell like, you can touch her hand or stand by the door, and if you want to leave, we leave. That conversation costs nothing and it is worth more than anything on the price list.

Tonight's check, number four. If you have children, ask them tonight what they remember about the last funeral in the family. Not to teach them anything. To find out what they already think, because they already think something.

Number five. The family opens the lid.

In Mexico the casket is not a locked door. The family opens it when the family wants. Somebody comes late, they open it. Somebody wants to fix the blanket, they open it. Somebody wants to put a letter in, they open it. And at the end of the night the eldest son closes it and everybody stands up.

In the United States the casket is closed at a set time by the staff, and after that it is closed. Sometimes there's a rule. Sometimes it's just the way things are done, and the family does not know they are allowed to ask.

Two thousand sixteen, a family from Laredo. The father died and the service was in San Antonio. At eight forty-five the director closed the lid and the daughter asked if she could have one more minute. The director said, we're getting ready to move him. She didn't get the minute. She's still talking about that minute, nine years later. That is what a closed lid costs.

Why? Because moving a casket is a schedule, and a lid that opens is a delay in the schedule. And because in the American system the funeral director is in charge of the body, and being in charge is part of what you're paying for — the basic services fee is, in the FTC's own language, for the funeral director's services and the supervision of the staff and the facilities. Supervision. That word is on the price list and it is a real thing.

What can you change? Ask on the day you arrange it, not on the day of the service: when is the lid closed, who closes it, and can my family open it one more time before we leave for the church. Write the answer on the arrangement sheet you sign. If they say yes, they will keep it, because they've written it down.

Tonight's check, number five. Ask your mother, or your father, or your aunt: who in this family should close the lid. Somebody will answer immediately. That person knows something you didn't know they knew.

Number six. The music is theirs.

[excited] This one is my favorite, mijo, because it's the one where the difference is not a rule. It's a different idea of what music is for.

In an American funeral home the music is background. It comes out of a speaker in the ceiling or a CD player behind a plant, it is organ music, it is chosen by the funeral home, and it is the same for everybody. Some funeral homes will let you bring recorded songs, and some charge a fee for what they call a musical tribute, usually two hundred fifty to five hundred dollars, to run a playlist.

In Mexico the music is an event. People sing. Somebody brings a guitar and they sing the songs the dead person liked, and if it is a mariachi town, a mariachi comes. In my grandmother's wake in nineteen ninety-nine the mariachi came from two doors down and played Amor Eterno, and my father sat with his cane and cried like a boy, and the whole room stood up.

Why does it happen? Because in the American model the funeral home controls the room, and a live musician is a person in your room who is not on your payroll. Some funeral homes allow it. Many will say it's not permitted, or that the family has to arrange it with the church. And in a lot of cases nobody ever asked.

What can you change? Ask: can we bring musicians, and is there a sound system we can use. If they say no, ask about the graveside — a lot of cemeteries will let a mariachi play at the grave, and I have seen a mariachi at the grave do more for a family than any eulogy. A mariachi costs three hundred to six hundred dollars in San Antonio. The organ is included in the price of the room. Only one of those two will make your uncle cry, and it is not the organ.

Tonight's check, number six. Make a list of three songs. Not for the funeral. For your family. Send it to one person tonight, because the night it happens nobody will be able to think of a single song.

Number seven. The rosary belongs to the family.

In Mexico the rosary is led by whoever knows it. An aunt, a cousin, a neighbor, the woman from the church with her own worn-out booklet. It goes around the room. People take turns. It lasts an hour, and it is not a performance, it is a job the room does together, and then the next night they do it again. And then for eight more nights after the burial, the family prays the novena.

In the United States prayer is usually scheduled. There is a service, there is a priest or a minister, it lasts twenty-five minutes, and then the room is needed for the next group.

Twenty nineteen, the Reyes family, San Antonio. They asked for a rosary at the visitation. The funeral home did not have anybody, so they played a recording of a rosary through the speaker system. A recording. The whole family stood there listening to a stranger's voice pray for their father.

Why? Because the funeral home's model has a list of services, prayer is one of them, and it is delivered by a person they bring in. A family that prays by itself is doing something the funeral home does not bill for.

What can you change? Ask for the rosary to be led by the family, and put a microphone in the room if you can. And ask about the novena: nine nights is the tradition, and a funeral home will not organize nine nights, because they sell one evening. The nine nights are the family's, and they are free, and in my experience they are the part that actually helps.

Tonight's check, number seven. Find the person in your family who knows the rosary. You know who it is. Call them tonight and ask if they would lead it. Not for a funeral. Just so somebody has asked, once, while everybody is calm.

Number eight. What goes in the casket with them.

In Mexico, people put things in. A rosary, a photograph, a letter, a pack of cigarettes, a deck of cards, the little pillow with the grandchildren's names embroidered on it. I have seen a man buried with his tools. I have seen a woman buried with a packet of seeds from her garden. It is not superstition. It is the last thing the family can still give.

In the United States, many funeral homes will tell you nothing can go in the casket, or that the casket must be sealed, or that the items have to be removed for the cemetery. Some of that is the cemetery's rules, some is the casket's lining, and some is just a habit.

My own father died in two thousand three, when I was already in San Antonio. When I went to arrange it, I took the red pencil he used to write the refrendos in his calendar. Forty years of that pencil. [whispering] The director told me objects in the casket were not recommended. And I said, mijo, I have buried close to seven thousand people. Put the pencil in his hand.

Why does it happen? Because the American system is built to keep the body separate from the living, and an object is a piece of the living that goes in with the body. A pencil is not merchandise. It doesn't have a price, it doesn't fit on the price list, and things that don't fit on the price list make the system uncomfortable.

What can you change? Ask before you need to: can we place personal items in the casket, and is there anything the cemetery will not allow. If they tell you the casket can't be opened, ask them to show you in the arrangement paperwork. And put it in writing on the sheet, next to the signatures.

Tonight's check, number eight. Write down one object. One. For one person in your family. Put it in a drawer with their name on it. That is not morbid. That is the cheapest and most useful thing you will do all year.

Number nine. The neighborhood pays for it, one plate at a time.

And this is the one no American funeral home can sell you, because there is no line on the price list for it.

In Mexico, when somebody dies, the neighborhood arrives. The women cook. The men carry chairs from the church hall. Somebody brings a coffee pot, somebody brings a case of water, a stack of paper cups, and at the end of the night somebody puts an envelope in the hand of the eldest daughter and does not say anything about it. Nobody keeps a list. Nobody asks for it back.

In the United States the family pays for everything, item by item, the food is catered, the catering is on the invoice, the invoice is paid by the family, and the family is alone with a number.

Two thousand twelve, the Ortiz family on Guadalupe Street. Three hundred people came through that house in two days. Forty trays of food, all of it brought. The funeral home bill was thirty-four hundred dollars for the transfer, the paperwork and the graveside. If they had done the same thing inside the funeral home — the room, the visitation, the chapel, the catering at eleven dollars a head — that bill would have been close to nine thousand.

Why does it exist? Because in Mexico the funeral is not a product, it's an obligation the community owes and the community pays. In the United States the funeral is a retail transaction between a family and a corporation, and a corporation cannot accept a tray of tamales as payment.

What can you change? Nothing at the funeral home. Everything in your family. Make the list of who cooks, who carries, who drives, who watches the kids, who answers the phone. My wife Rosa has done this for thirty-four years in hospice, and she says the families that do well are not the ones with money. They're the ones with a list.
