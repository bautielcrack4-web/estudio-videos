Mrs. Kinoshita is eighty-one years old, and every morning she walks ten thousand steps on the Philosopher's Path, here in Kyoto.

Last April I asked her what shoes she wears. She lifted one foot and showed me. Fifteen years old. Flat leather, a little scratched at the toe, with a strap across the top.

Then I asked her the other question. What would you never put on your feet again?

She answered without thinking. Five things. Five shoes that half the women in America buy every year to walk more, and then walk less.

I wore three of them myself, for nineteen years, in California. And I paid for it.

My name is Michiko Arai. I am sixty-eight. I live in my mother's wooden house in Nishijin, the weaving quarter of Kyoto, and in my cupboard there is still a pair of shoes my mother made me wear in 1975.

I am not going to talk about knees or bones. I am going to talk about shoes. Just shoes.

By the end you will know which pair to take out of your cupboard tonight, what to put in its place, and you will not have to spend much. Most of the time the answer is a pair you already own.

My mother, Fumiko, dressed brides in Gion for forty years. She tied obi for geiko, knelt on tatami for six hours at a time, and she had a rule about feet. She said, a woman stands on her shoes the same way she stands on her life.

Then I moved to Sunnyvale, California, in 1983, because my husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. And I bought shoes the American way. Soft, thick, tall, a lot of cushioning, because everybody said cushioning was good for you. Nineteen years. I came home to Kyoto with three pairs of shoes I could not walk in.

These are the five shoes I gave away, and the five reasons Mrs. Kinoshita walks ten thousand steps at eighty-one and I can walk with her.

Number one. The thick, tall, soft walking sneaker. The one with the sole like a mattress.

In 1994, in Sunnyvale, I bought a pair for eighty-nine dollars. The salesman told me they were made for walking. The sole was almost two inches thick and it felt like standing on a pillow. I walked three miles a day in those shoes, on the sidewalk near the park, and for two months I told everybody how comfortable they were.

Then one morning the front of my shoe caught on a joint in the concrete, the smallest little step, no higher than my finger. And I went down. Nothing broke. I was lucky. But I sat on the sidewalk with my shopping bag and I understood something. In those shoes I could not feel the ground. Not the slope, not the gravel, not the little step.

Here in Kyoto the lanes are stone. The stone is old, it is not flat, and it is never the same twice. If you cannot feel the ground, the ground surprises you. And a surprise at sixty-eight is different from a surprise at thirty-eight.

So I wear a flat canvas shoe from a shop near Nishiki market. Eighteen dollars. The sole is thin, no thicker than my thumb. The toe box is wide, so my toes lie side by side and not on top of each other. And I feel every stone, which sounds uncomfortable and is the opposite. My foot works again.

Tonight, take your walking shoe and put it on the table. Press the heel down with your thumb, and look at the front. If the heel sits higher than the toe by more than an inch, that shoe was made for a shopping mall with a smooth floor. It is not a walking shoe. It is a sitting shoe that you walk in.

Number two. The backless slide. The mule, the slip-on without a strap behind the heel.

Mrs. Tamura is sixty-six. She is the sister of my neighbour, and she lives near the Kamogawa. Two years ago, in June, she went into a supermarket in the rain with a pair of backless slides, the kind with a soft rubber top, very comfortable, very cheap.

The floor near the door was wet. She took a step, and the shoe did not come with her foot. Her foot went forward and the shoe stayed. She fell against the trolley and she was not hurt badly, but she sat in the car for twenty minutes before she could drive home.

Here is why it happens. To keep a backless shoe on, your toes have to curl down and grip it. Every step. All day. Those little muscles in the toes are the same ones that hold you upright when the bus moves, or when the pavement is wet. After sixty they are not as strong as they were. So you give them a job all day, and then you ask them to save you in the rain, and they are tired.

And in a backless shoe you cannot take a full step. You take a short one, because a long one throws the shoe off. Short steps all day is not walking. It is shuffling.

I still have a pair of slides. They live by the door of the bathroom and never go outside. That is the rule in this house.

If you love a pair like that, take it to the shoe repair man and ask him to sew a strap across the back. Eight to twelve dollars. It becomes a different shoe. Or buy the same thing with a strap already on it. They cost the same.

[whispering] Tonight, put the shoe on and lift your foot off the floor. Then try to shake it off by wiggling your foot. If it comes off, it is a house shoe. Keep it in the house.

Number three. The pointed flat with the hard, thin sole.

This one is my mother's story, and mine.

In 1975 I went with my mother to a wedding in Gion. I was seventeen. I wore a pair of pointed black flats, very beautiful, with a sole like a piece of cardboard. My mother told me to bring another pair in a bag. I did not.

We knelt for the ceremony, we stood for the photographs, we walked to the restaurant. By the fourth hour my little toe was numb and my heel was burning. My mother looked at me once, said nothing, and when the car came she opened the door and took out a pair of flat leather shoes she kept under the seat. For me. She had brought them anyway. That is the whole story of my family in one pair of shoes.

The pointed toe pushes the toes together into a triangle, and the little toe has nowhere to go. And the sole is thin and hard, so every stone in the street goes straight up into the ball of your foot. In a young foot that is uncomfortable. In a foot after sixty it changes the way you walk, and the way you walk changes everything above it.

Before we go on, do one small test with me. One minute, a sheet of paper, and a wet foot.

Wet the bottom of your right foot, not dripping, just wet. Put the paper on a hard floor, not a carpet. Step on it with your full weight and stand still for three seconds. Then step off and look.

If you see the whole foot, with the toes spread a little, like a hand, that is a good foot. If you see a narrow shape, like an oval, with no toes, and the paper is dry where the toes should be, then your toes are being held together all day. Your shoes are too narrow, whatever the number inside says.

Do it with the other foot too, because we buy one size for two different feet.

My own paper test, in 2016, showed me a foot I had not seen since I was a girl. I had been wearing a size too small for years, because in America I was a six and a half, and in Japan a twenty-three and a half, and I had decided the number was the truth and the foot was wrong. The foot is always the truth.

So now, a round toe. Room for the little toe. And a sole with a little rubber under it, half an inch at least, soft but not thick. If you have a pair of flats you love, take them to a cobbler and ask for a rubber half-sole. Ten or fifteen dollars. They will walk differently.

Number four. The tall rubber boot, worn all day.

This one is about the rainy season, the tsuyu, in June. Here it rains for three weeks and the air is wet like a towel.

In 2018 I wore rubber boots to the Nishiki market. I left at nine in the morning and I came home at one. Four hours. My feet were dry, which was the point. But when I took the boots off my socks were wet from the inside, and my legs were tired in a way they are never tired in my normal shoes. Hiroshi looked at me walking to the kitchen and said, you are walking like a samurai in a film. He was right. I was lifting my feet up and down instead of forward.

Rubber does not breathe. Your foot makes about half a cup of water a day, just sitting, and in a rubber boot that water has nowhere to go. It stays next to your skin, and your skin gets soft.

And a boot holds your ankle like a cast. Your ankle is supposed to move a little with every step. If it cannot, the work goes up to your calf, and your calf gets tired, and then you walk less. A heavy boot that you cannot bend also makes you pick your foot up high, and that is the walk of a person who is afraid of falling. People see it. It is not about age. It is about the shoe.

I still keep one pair of rubber boots. I wear them to the car, or to the corner, and then I change. I keep a second pair in a cloth bag by the door, with a small towel, and in thirty seconds I am walking normally.

If your boots are the tall kind, do this tonight. Look inside. If the insole is flat and hard, put a felt insole in it, six dollars, and wear them for short walks only. And buy a can of waterproof spray for your canvas shoes, eight dollars. Canvas with spray is enough for a Kyoto rain, and your foot can still breathe.

Number five. This is the last one, and it is the one nobody wants to hear.

Before I say it, do me a small favour. If this is useful, press the like, and subscribe, because that is how Yui knows which videos to put next. It takes one second, and then you can go back to the shoes.

Number five. The platform. The wedge. The heel over an inch and a half.

I am not going to tell you to throw away your beautiful shoes. My mother would have laughed at me. She wore a heel in Gion for forty years, two hours at a time, and a flat pair sat under the seat of the car.

In 1998 I wore a pair of heels with a small platform to a concert at my daughter Naomi's school in Sunnyvale. They were beautiful and not tall, maybe two inches, but the sole under the front was thick and hard like a piece of wood. I stood at the back of the hall for an hour, because all the seats were taken.

By the end of the concert I was standing on the outside of my feet, my back was aching, and I could not feel the floor. A platform is a wall between your foot and the ground. You do not feel the slope, the step, the wet tile. And all your weight is on the front of the foot, so the toes work all day like fingers holding a bag.

There is a version of this shoe that is fine. A block heel, an inch and a half, wide, not thin, with a strap across the ankle. I have a pair in dark brown leather. I wear them to dinner, to a wedding, to the theatre. And I carry a flat pair in my bag in a cloth pouch. Two hours in the pretty shoes, then the flat ones. That is not giving up. That is planning.

Tonight, stand in your heels on a hard floor and close your eyes for ten seconds. If you feel your ankles moving to keep you up, those are shoes for sitting. Keep them for sitting. That is not a small life. It is a good life, with better shoes in the bag.

Five shoes. The thick mattress sneaker, the backless slide, the pointed flat with the hard sole, the tall rubber boot all day, and the platform.

[emphatically] You do not need to buy five new pairs of shoes. Go to your cupboard tonight and put them on the floor in a line. Then ask each pair one question, the question Mrs. Kinoshita asks. Can I take a full step in this, on a stone lane, in the rain, and still feel the ground?

The ones that say no, put them in a bag. Not in the bin. Give them to a charity shop, or to the shoe repair man, who will fix the strap and give them to someone who can use them.

Keep four pairs, and that is enough for a whole life. One for walking, flat, wide, with a thin sole. One for the rain, or one can of spray. One for sitting, beautiful, in a box. And one by the door, for the bathroom, that never goes outside.

Mrs. Kinoshita changes the insole in her fifteen-year-old shoes once a year. Six dollars. That is the whole secret. Not the shoe. The care of the shoe.

[whispering] My mother used to say, if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you. She said it about a jacket. It is also true about shoes. The right shoe is not the prettiest one. It is the one you forget you are wearing.
