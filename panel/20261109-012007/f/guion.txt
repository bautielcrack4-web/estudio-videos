Hello, please come in. Mind the step. [whispering] It is a cold morning in Kyoto, and I want to show you something before the kettle finishes.

Look at this. Three pieces, laid on the tatami. A thin white undershirt. A fine wool sweater. And one finger's width of tucked fabric. Together they weigh about two hundred and forty grams, less than the iron teapot in my hands. On a February morning, this is warmer than the heavy coat I wore in 1998. [emphatically] Warmth is not weight. Warmth is order.

My name is Michiko. I am sixty-eight years old, and for thirty-one years I helped women choose clothes in a small neighborhood shop near Shijo Street. I was not the owner. I was the woman who stood beside the mirror for forty minutes, watching where a sleeve falls, where a collar sits, how a customer stands when she thinks no one is looking. I do not call myself an expert. I am simply old enough to have seen what lasts.

Today I will give you three quiet steps. A smooth base, a thin knit, and a gentle tuck. Three steps, no pulling, no bulk. Let us take our time.

The first step is the one almost everyone gets wrong. Most women reach for a thicker sweater when they feel cold. I did this too, for years. In December of 1979, my grandmother Sachi dressed me for a walk to the temple. I was twenty-one, impatient, and I put on a thick cotton undershirt with a wide ribbed collar and a heavy ribbed sweater over it. By the time we reached the gate, my shoulders looked round and my neck had disappeared. She stopped me under the lantern and said, "Michiko, you are wearing two coats, and neither one is doing its job."

She was right. Bulk does not come from the sweater. It comes from what is underneath. A thick cotton undershirt holds the shape of its own seams, and the sweater has to stretch over those seams. Every seam becomes a small ridge. The eye reads those ridges as the shape of your body, and the body looks wider than it is.

So the base must be smooth. Very smooth. I wear a fine merino undershirt, or a thin silk one in the coldest weeks. [whispering] Silk is expensive, I know. Mine came from a shop in Teramachi in 2016 and cost about five thousand yen. I have washed it by hand every winter since, and it is still here. A good merino undershirt costs around three thousand yen and lasts five winters if you let it dry flat. That is not a large sum for something you wear under everything.

The check is simple. Put the base on, then run your flat palm from your shoulder to your waist. If you feel a seam catch the skin of your palm, that base is a coat. It should feel like a cool smooth page. And the collar must be low. A high collar under a round neckline creates a small mountain at the throat that no scarf can hide.

Second step: the knit itself. Fine, not thick. Twelve-gauge, if the label says it. [emphatically] One thin knit laid over a smooth base keeps more warmth than three thick layers, because the warm air stays close to you and the outline stays yours.

My mother, Haru, taught me this with a lamp. In the winter of 1991 she held a sweater between me and the window in the shop. "If you cannot see the light through it," she said, "you cannot see the woman through it either." I still do that in the morning. Hold the knit up to the shoji screen. If the light comes through in a soft honey color, the gauge is fine and it will layer. If it blocks the light like a curtain, it will sit on you like a curtain too.

The fit matters more than the fiber. The shoulder seam should sit exactly on the edge of your shoulder bone, not below it. If it drops two fingers past the bone, the whole sweater pulls downward and the sleeves bunch at the wrist. I measure with my hand: three fingers from the base of my neck to the shoulder seam. That is my number. Yours may be four. Find it once, and you will never guess again in a shop.

Do not choose the size that is loose in the hope of hiding the body. A loose thin knit still shows the outline, but it also adds a shadow where your waist is not. Choose the size that follows you, then use the tuck to keep it in place.

Third step: the tuck, and the line where it stops. This is the quiet part, and it is the part that changes everything.

In the shop, I used to watch women push the whole sweater into the waistband. Then they would turn to the mirror and sigh. The fabric gathered in a roll around the hips, and the silhouette became a bell. It is not their fault. No one had shown them where to stop.

Here is my line. Find the top of your hip bone with your thumb. Now go down four fingers. That is the stop line. The sweater is tucked in front only, from the center to about two inches past the hip bone, and it is not tucked behind. The back hangs free. When you move, the fabric moves with you, and there is no roll at the waist. [emphatically] Front, two inches, four fingers down. That is the whole secret.

If the sweater is very thin, you can tuck it a little lower. If it is a fine rib, tuck only the middle, the width of your palm. Ribbing stretches. If you tuck the sides, it will pull the hem into a V shape, and the hem will announce itself all day.

Before we go further, do something with me. This is the test I gave every woman at the shop, and it takes one minute. Stand in front of a mirror with your arms down. Now raise both arms above your head, slowly, and lower them. Look at the hem. If the hem climbed more than four fingers above your waistband, the sweater is too short or too tight, and no tuck will save it. If the hem stayed near your waist, it is the right length. Then sit down on a chair and cross your legs. If the sweater gathers into a thick band across your stomach, it is too long for tucking, and you should leave it out at the back. Try this today, with the sweater you already own. Most of us do not need a new one. We need to know what we have.

Now, the finish. The neckline and the sleeves decide whether the whole thing looks calm.

With the base and the knit on, open the collar of the sweater with two fingers, so the white edge of the undershirt shows in a narrow line at the throat. Not a wide triangle. A line, like the edge of a page. It gives the eye somewhere to rest.

For the sleeves, push them up to just below the wrist bone, then fold once, neatly, so the cuff sits flat. If the sleeve is long and you fold it three times, the arm looks thick. One fold only, and never above the elbow in February, unless you are staying indoors.

The third piece is the cardigan you see me wearing. It is oatmeal, open, and it is not buttoned. I wear it not for warmth but for the line down the front. An open cardigan creates two vertical lines, and vertical lines make the body look longer. If you button it, you close the line and the body becomes a block. Leave it open, even indoors.

Let us pause here, because I want to ask you something small. If these quiet lessons are useful to you, please press the like button, and subscribe if you would like to come back next week. It helps this little room reach other women who are tired of fighting their own clothes. [whispering] Thank you. Now let us finish.

The last step is not dressing. It is what you do when you take the sweater off. The way you store a thin knit decides whether it stays thin.

Never hang it. A hanger pulls the shoulders into two small points, and by March the sweater looks like it belongs to someone else. Fold it in three. Sleeves across the chest, then the body in half, then in half again, and lay it flat in a drawer. I keep each knit in a cotton bag with a piece of cedar from the mountains north of Kyoto. The cedar costs very little, and it keeps the moths away without a smell you can notice.

Give a thin knit a day to rest between wears. Wool has a memory. It needs time to return to its own shape. If you wear the same sweater four days in a row, the elbows will remember it. Two sweaters, worn in turn, will last three times as long as one worn every day. I learned that from a customer named Mrs. Ito, who wore the same gray cardigan for eleven years and always looked as if she had just bought it.

And when a small hole appears, mend it that same week. A hole the size of a grain of rice takes four minutes and a needle. Left alone for a month, it becomes a hole the size of a coin, and then the sweater is a cleaning cloth. My mother kept a small tin of threads by the window. [whispering] Small things, done every day. That was her sentence, and now it is mine.

So, three steps. A smooth base that lets the sweater fall. A thin knit that keeps the warm air close. A tuck that stops four fingers below the hip bone. Three quiet steps, and the mirror stops being an argument.

Now I would like to hear from you. Which of the three steps surprised you most, or which one do you already do without knowing why? Tell me below, and if you have a sweater that never sits right, describe it in a few words. Next week, I will show you the four small stitches that saved my mother's favorite cuff and gave it one more winter. [whispering] Until then, please take your time.
