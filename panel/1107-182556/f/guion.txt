I want to tell you about a notebook.

It's a spiral notebook, the cheap kind, ninety-nine cents at the H-E-B on Bandera Road. The cover is gone. The pages are soft at the corners from being carried in my bag for years. And on those pages, in my handwriting, are the names of about three thousand people I sat with while they were dying.

Thirty-four years. 1991 to 2025. I was the chaplain. I didn't take blood pressure. I didn't decide anything. I sat down, I put my bag on the floor, and I stayed.

And in that notebook, next to some of those names, I wrote one word. A word I never said out loud to anybody. The word is "wrong."

Not because the family didn't love them. Every single one of those families loved them. It's because we are not taught how to talk to somebody who is dying. Nobody hands you a book. Your mother doesn't sit you down and say, honey, here is what to say when it's my turn. You learn it in the doorway, with your heart pounding, and you say the first thing that comes into your mouth.

And that first thing is almost always one of seven sentences.

I'm Rosa Navarro. I'm not medical people, and nothing in this video is medical advice. If there's a question about symptoms in your house tonight, call your hospice team, not me. What I know is what happens in the room. I know what the families said, what the person in the bed heard, and what happened in their face when they heard it.

Last week I told you about the sentence I almost said to my own mother. I'm going to tell you the rest of that today, because my mother is the reason I know number five on this list, and I have never told anybody this part.

Seven things. Thirty-four years. Let's go.

Number one. "Everything happens for a reason."

I know why we say it. We say it because we cannot stand to watch somebody we love be afraid, and we want to hand them something to hold.

But think about what you're handing them. You are handing them a bill. You are saying: this has a reason, and the reason is somewhere, and you should accept it. A woman in her eighties, lying in that bed, she doesn't want a reason. She wants to know if her son is going to be all right after she's gone. She wants to know if she was a good mother. She wants somebody to sit down and not explain anything.

I remember a man in New Braunfels. Mr. Herman Vogel. He was my first family, 1991, and I was so new that my hands were shaking when I knocked on the door. German, retired, a shop teacher, and he had that thing that old teachers have where they look at you like you're late for class. His daughter was there from Austin, and she was a good daughter, and every time he tried to say something about dying she would pat his hand and say, "Papa, everything happens for a reason."

By the fourth day he stopped saying anything at all. He just looked at the window.

I sat with him on the fifth day, alone, and I said, "Mr. Vogel, you look like a man with something to say."

He said, "I have plenty to say. But when I say it, she cries, and then I have to comfort her. So I don't say it."

A dying man, doing the emotional work for somebody who wasn't dying. That's what that sentence does. It doesn't give them a reason. It gives them a job.

What to say instead. This is going to feel too simple. It isn't. You say: "I don't know why. I'm not going to pretend I do. But I'm not going anywhere."

That's it. You take the explanation off the table and you put yourself on the table. Try it tonight. Not the whole speech. Just the first half. "I don't know why." Then stop and let them talk.

Number two. "You can beat this. You're a fighter."

Now this one comes from love, and it comes from the deepest place in us, because we do not want to lose them and we think our hope is fuel for them.

But I want you to hear what a woman told me in 2018. Her name was Mrs. Ophelia Washington. She sang in a Baptist choir on the East Side for forty-one years, and she had a voice that made the walls feel bigger. She was in her bed, and her nephew was there, a big man, a coach, and he kept saying, "Auntie, you're strong. You're going to fight this. You've beaten everything."

She waited until he went to get coffee. Then she said to me, "Rosa. I'm tired of fighting. I've been fighting since March. If I say I'm tired, he looks at me like I've betrayed him."

That's the cost. When you say "fight," you make dying a failure. And the person in the bed is the only one who can lose.

I sat with Ophelia to the end, and I want to tell you something about that last week. She wasn't fighting. She was singing. Quietly. Songs I didn't know. Her nephew learned to sit and listen instead of cheer, and those were the best days they had together in that whole year. He told me that at the funeral. He said, "I wasted four months telling her to fight. I had four months to just listen to her."

What to say instead: "I know you're tired. You don't have to be strong for me." Say that sentence tonight to your mother or your father and watch what happens in their shoulders. God, the shoulders. You can see the whole body let go.

Number three. "Let's not talk about that right now."

This one is the hardest for me, because it looks like kindness. It looks like protecting them. It's the sentence we say when the person in the bed says something like, "I don't think I'm going to be here for Christmas."

And we say, "Oh, don't talk like that. You'll be here."

Now I want you to understand what you just did. That person has been carrying that thought alone for weeks. They have been lying awake with it at two in the morning, rehearsing how to say it, picking the moment when you look calm enough. That was the moment. They picked it. They spent something to say it. And you closed the door.

And here's the thing that took me years to understand: they will not try again. Not because they're angry. Because they love you and they don't want to hurt you twice.

I had a woman in 2003, on the Southside, and I'm not going to give you her name because her family is still here. She wanted to talk about her funeral. Her daughter would not let her. Every time, "Mama, no." Nine days. On the tenth day she stopped talking about it, and the daughter thought, thank God, she's in a better place, she's accepted it.

She hadn't accepted it. She had given up on her daughter.

Then she told me, in the kitchen, at the table, with the door closed, what she wanted. The songs. Who should carry her. A letter she had written and put in a shoebox. I wrote it all down in my notebook, and after she died I gave it to the daughter.

The daughter read it in my car in the driveway and she said, "Why didn't she tell me?"

And I said, "Honey. She tried. Nine times."

What to say instead. When they open that door, you do not walk through it and you do not slam it. You stand in it and you say: "Tell me. I can take it."

And then the hardest part. You don't fix it. You don't say "but." You say "okay," and then you say the next question. "What songs?" "Who do you want there?" "What should I tell your brother?"

You will cry. That's fine. Crying with them is not the same as falling apart on them. There's a difference between a daughter with tears on her face and a daughter who needs to be taken care of. One of those lets the conversation happen.

Now, before I give you number four, I want to do something with you. I do this with families, and it takes two minutes, and I want you to actually do it, not just listen to me do it.

Think of the person you're taking care of right now. Or the person you're afraid of losing. Get their face in your head.

Now I want you to count, on your fingers, how many of these three you have said in the last month.

One: a sentence that told them how they should feel. "You shouldn't be scared." "You should be grateful." "Be positive."

Two: a sentence that promised them something you can't control. "You'll be fine." "We'll get through this." "I'll be there every single day."

Three: a sentence that made the conversation about your feelings instead of theirs. "This is so hard for me." "I don't know what I'll do without you." "I can't lose you."

Go ahead. Count.

Most of you are holding up two fingers. Some of you are holding up three, and you're feeling bad about it right now, and I want you to put your hand down. You are not a bad daughter. You are a person who was never taught this, standing in a doorway at midnight, doing the best you can with no training at all. That's what all of us are.

But here's what I learned in thirty-four years, and I want you to hear it in my voice, because I mean it: [emphatically] the person in that bed is doing the same thing. They are also standing in a doorway. They are also saying the first thing that comes into their head. And they are also afraid of saying the wrong thing to you.

You are both amateurs in the same room. The only difference is that one of you has less time.

Number four. "So what did they say?"

I know. It sounds responsible. It sounds like you're taking care of business.

Here's what it does. It turns your dying father into a news report. He has to sit in that bed and give you the update. And he has to say it in a way that doesn't scare you, which means he edits it, which means he's now managing your feelings from a hospital bed.

And the terrible part is, he doesn't know. He hears half of what the medical team says, because he's tired, because he's scared, because the medical team talks fast in the hallway. So he gives you a version. And then you go home and build your whole week on that version.

I had a family in Leon Valley, 2016. Three brothers. And every single visit, one of them asked their father, "So what did they say?" and every single time the old man gave a different answer, because he was trying to say the thing that would keep the room calm.

So one afternoon I said, "Boys, can I say something? Your father is not the medical team. Stop asking him to be."

The oldest one said, "Then who do I ask?"

And I said, "You call the hospice team. That's what they're for. They get paid to answer that phone. Your father gets to be your father."

Then I gave them a piece of paper and I said, "Write down your three questions. Call her at nine in the morning, when the day is starting. Ask your three questions, write down the answers, and then close the notebook and come sit with him and talk about the Astros."

Three questions. Written down. That's the whole system.

What to say instead, when you're sitting with them: "What's on your mind today?" or "What were you thinking about before I came in?"

You'd be surprised. You'd be very surprised. The answer is almost never about that.

Number five. "I should have come sooner."

Now we're at my mother's kitchen, and this is the one I have to say slowly.

My mother was Doña Refugio Ibarra. Cuca, to everybody. She died in March of 2015, in her own house, with hospice, and I was there. Thirty-four years of sitting with other people's mothers, and then it was mine.

And I have to tell you, I was not good at it. I want you to know that. Thirty years of training and I stood in her kitchen and I said, "Mamá, I'm sorry I didn't come Tuesday. I should have come Tuesday."

She was so tired. She could barely lift her hand. And she looked at me and she said, "Mija. Come here. Are you okay?"

[whispering] My mother, nine days from dying, was comforting me.

I have thought about that for ten years. What I did, without meaning to, was hand her a problem she could not solve from that bed, and the only tool she had left in the world was to be my mother. So she used it. She spent some of the little energy she had left to make me feel better about being late on a Tuesday.

That is what "I should have" does. It is an apology addressed to the person in the bed, but it is about you. And they will pick it up. Every time. Because that's what parents do with their children's pain, even at the very end, even when it costs them something they don't have.

If you have something to apologize for, and most of us do, here is what to do with it. Say it once, short, and then hand them the other half.

"Papa, I'm sorry I wasn't here Tuesday. I'm here now. I'm not going anywhere."

One sentence of apology. One sentence that takes the weight off them. And then never again. Do not bring it back the next day. Do not make them absolve you twice.

And if you need to say more than that, say it to somebody else. To your sister. To your priest. To me. To the hospice social worker, who is free, and who is there for exactly this, and who will not be shocked by anything you tell her.

Number six. "Let me know if you need anything."

Everybody says this. I said it for years before I understood.

Understand what it asks. It asks a person who is exhausted, and overwhelmed, and probably a little embarrassed, to do three things: figure out what they need, find the right moment to ask, and ask you. You have handed them a project.

And notice who you said it to. Most of us say it to the person in the bed. And what is a dying man going to say? "Could you pick up my prescription? Could you sit with me Wednesday?" He's not going to ask. He's a man from a generation that doesn't ask.

I learned this from a woman in Kelly Field. Her husband was Mr. Delgado, a veteran of Korea, and he was in that bed for six weeks, and there were forty people in and out of that house. Forty. And every one of them said, "Let me know if you need anything."

She told me, in the kitchen, with the tea going cold: "Rosa, not one of them has brought a meal. They all ask, and I say we're fine, and they leave. I have not cooked in six weeks and I have not asked anybody for anything."

I want you to hear that. Forty people, and she was alone.

So here's the fix, and it is the easiest thing on this whole list. You do not ask. You announce. You pick a thing you are actually willing to do and you say when you're doing it.

"I'm bringing dinner Thursday. Is six okay?"

"Am I okay to come sit with him Tuesday from two to four so you can sleep?"

"I'm going to the H-E-B. What do I put on the list?"

"I'm taking the trash out and I'm not asking."

One thing. One date. One time. It is worth more than forty offers.

Tonight, before you go to bed, send one text to one family member and put a date and a time in it. Not "let me know." A date and a time.

Number seven. "I'll come back when you're feeling better."

This is the one that sounds the most gentle, and it is the one I have seen hurt the most, and I want you to stay with me here because the reason is not obvious.

When you say "when you're feeling better," you are saying something true and kind to your ears. But the person in that bed hears a schedule. And the schedule they hear is: not yet. And they know something you might not have let yourself know yet, which is that there may not be a "better."

I have watched this happen more times than I can count. Somebody comes on Sunday, they have a good visit, everybody's crying a little in the car, and the last thing said at the door is "I'll come back when you're feeling better."
