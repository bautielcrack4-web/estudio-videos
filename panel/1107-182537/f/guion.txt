Honey, before we start, I want you to look at the nightstand. The one right next to you. Count what is on it.

For thirty-four years, the nightstand I looked at was somebody else's. And it almost always had the same four things on it. A glass of water with a bendy straw. A folded white handkerchief. A little tin of lip balm. And a telephone with a number written on a piece of masking tape, in my handwriting.

Four objects. That is what the last forty-eight hours come down to, honey. Not the big speeches. Not the family reunion in the living room. Four objects on a nightstand, and one person in a chair who is not going anywhere.

I'm Rosa Navarro. I was a hospice chaplain here in San Antonio for thirty-four years. I started in 1991, going house to house for Alamo Home Hospice, and I retired last year. In that time I sat with more than three thousand people at the end of their lives, and with their families, in living rooms and back bedrooms from Prospect Hill to Bandera and back again. I sat with a German man in New Braunfels who was the first family I ever sat with, and with a choir lady on the East Side, and with my own mother, right here in this city, in March of 2015.

I am not a medical person. I was never trained for that and I never pretended to be. I was the chaplain. In hospice, the medical team takes care of the body and the plan, and I sat with the person and with whatever they believed or didn't believe. So if anything I say today makes you worry about a symptom, you call your hospice team. That is what they are there for, at two in the morning too. I am going to tell you what I saw from the chair. Not from a book. From the chair.

Now. Here is the thing nobody tells you about the last forty-eight hours.

They do not look like the movies. In the movies, somebody says something beautiful, everybody cries, and then the person closes their eyes and it is over in ninety seconds. In real life, honey, it is slow. It is quiet. It is boring in a way that will break your heart later, because you will realize that the boring part was the part you got to keep.

And it is different for every single person. I want to say that first, before I say anything else. Every death is as different as every life. But there is a shape to it. After three thousand of them, I can tell you there is a shape, and once you know the shape, you stop being afraid of every little thing and you start being present for the big thing.

So I am going to walk you through it hour by hour. From about forty-eight hours out, down to the last one. What you will see. What you will hear. What will scare you that should not scare you. And the one small thing you can do in each of those hours so that you are not standing frozen in the doorway with your hands full of nothing.

Let's start at the beginning. Forty-eight hours.

The first thing that happens, and the first thing that scares families, is that they go quiet.

Not all at once. It starts like a dimmer switch, not like a light going off. Two days out, your father is still in the room with you, but he is a little further back. He answers you, but the answer comes a second late. He eats three bites instead of a plate. He sleeps in the afternoon, and then he sleeps through the afternoon, and then the afternoon and the evening both.

The family gathers in the kitchen and whispers. Is he giving up? Is he depressed? Should we wake him? Should we make him eat? And somebody always says, we have to keep him strong. Somebody always says that. I have heard it in a hundred kitchens.

I want to tell you about my first one. Mr. Herman Vogel. New Braunfels, March of 1991. He was a retired baker, a German man with big hands and a very neat garden, and he had been in this country for forty years and still said "yes" like "ja." I was twenty-seven years old, I had my bag and my little book of prayers, and I had no idea what I was doing. Lola Benavides, the old chaplain who trained me, sent me there alone for the first time and said, "Sit down. Don't fix anything."

Mr. Vogel stopped eating on a Thursday. By Friday his daughter was standing over him with a spoon, and she was crying, and she said to me, "You people are letting him starve." I did not have an answer. I was twenty-seven. So I did what Lola told me. I sat down. And I watched.

By Saturday afternoon, that man was sleeping most of the day. His daughter was exhausted from fighting him, and he was exhausted from being fought. And somewhere in the middle of that Saturday, she put the spoon down on the tray, and she sat down next to me, and she said, "What do I do now?"

I said, "You hold his hand. That's the job now."

And she did. And the last thing that man did before he let go, on Monday morning, was open his eyes, look at his daughter, and squeeze her fingers. That's all. A squeeze. She talked about that squeeze for twenty years. She wrote me a letter about it after her own husband died.

Lola used to say something to me that I have repeated ten thousand times. She used to say: the dying are never in a hurry. We are.

That's the whole first day, honey. We are in a hurry, and they are not. They are doing something slow and old and nobody on this earth has ever been able to rush it. The body is turning inward. It is not disappearing. It is packing. Like when you are leaving on a long trip and the night before you don't want the television, you don't want the phone, you don't want to talk about the itinerary anymore. You just want quiet. You are getting ready.

So the change, for this first stretch, is small and it costs nothing. You stop being the coach and you become the company. You do not wake him for the cousin from Laredo. You do not stand over him with a plate. You let him sleep, and when he opens his eyes, you are there, and you say, "Hi, Dad. It's me. I'm right here." That's it. That's the whole speech.

Now, I promised you a test. And I want you to do it now, not later, because later you will be tired and you will forget. It takes one minute.

Stand up. Go to the doorway of the room where your person is. Not into the room. In the doorway. And stand there, and look at the bed, and count five things that a person lying in that bed could reach without getting up, or that you could reach without leaving the chair.

Five things. Water. The call button or the phone. A light. Something to wipe their mouth. And something of theirs that is not medical — a photograph, a rosary, a little radio, a folded letter. That is five.

If you cannot find five, then tonight, before you go to sleep, you fix that. And I am going to tell you what I used, and what it cost, because I have set up that same nightstand three thousand times.

A bendy straw, because a person lying down cannot drink from a straight one. A pack is three dollars. A small lamp you can turn on with one hand, the kind with the switch on the cord, twelve dollars at the hardware store on Culebra. A wireless doorbell, the kind with two pieces, one by the bed and one in the kitchen, nineteen dollars, so she does not have to yell when you are washing a dish. A spiral notebook and a pen, two dollars and fifty cents, because you are going to forget things and you are going to think you won't. And one thing that is hers. Her glasses. Her handkerchief. The picture of her sister. That one costs nothing and it matters more than all the rest.

That is the doorway test. Five things within reach. If you have them, you are already doing better than half the families I ever met.

Now let's go to thirty-six hours.

The second thing you will see, and this is the one that makes people cry in the hallway, is the hands.

Around the second day, the hands start to move. Not big movements. Little ones. Picking at the sheet. Picking at the edge of the blanket. Lifting a hand a few inches and letting it fall. Reaching for something that is not there.

Families hate this. They think it means pain, or fear, or that the person is looking for something and can't find it. And sometimes it is discomfort — a full bladder, a wrinkled sheet, a room too warm — and that is exactly when you call your hospice team, because that is their job and they know what to look at. I never once guessed at that. I called them. Every time.

But a lot of the time, honey, it is not pain. It is the body doing what it does. And sometimes, it is something else.

Mr. Delgado. Kelly Field. A veteran of Korea, ninety-one years old when I met him, in a little house off Zarzamora with a screen door that banged. He had a folded flag on the shelf and a picture of a brother who died in 1951, a brother named Ramiro, and in forty-eight hours Mr. Delgado started reaching for the corner of the room and saying, "Ramiro. His daughter was frightened. She thought he was confused, or that something was wrong with him. She said, "Chaplain, he's calling for his brother. His brother is dead."

I said, "I know, mija. That's the point."

Now, I am not going to tell you today what I believe about that. That is another video, and it is a good one. What I will tell you is what I saw. I saw it over and over. I saw a man who had not spoken his brother's name in years, and in the last two days he spoke it like Ramiro was standing right there by the window. And the terror left his face. That is what I noticed most. Not the talking. The calm that came after it.

So what do you do with the hands? You hold them. That is the first thing, and it is free.

But here is the change I want to give you, and it costs about two dollars. You write it down. You keep the notebook on the nightstand, and when your father says a name you don't know, you write the name and the day and the hour. Not to prove anything. Not to argue with him. Just to keep it.

Mr. Delgado's daughter did that. She wrote down every name, every sentence about Ramiro, for two days, in a spiral notebook with a green cover. After he died, she showed it to her cousins, and two of them started crying, because the things he said were things only Ramiro would have known. She copied those pages for the whole family. That notebook is the only record that exists of that man's brother in that house.

You cannot buy that later. Two dollars and fifty cents, and a pen. That is the change.

And the tonight check, for this hour: sit with your person for twenty minutes and don't do anything else. No phone. No dishes. Just watch the hands, and listen. You will learn more in twenty minutes than in three phone calls with relatives.

Now. Twenty-four hours.

This is the part that scares everybody, and I want to talk about it plainly, because I have seen grown men stand in a doorway with their back against the wall because of a sound.

The breathing changes.

It gets shallow, and then deep, and then shallow again, and then there is a pause. A long pause. Long enough that the family stops breathing too. And then it comes back.

I have heard it called a lot of things. The hospice team has a name for it, and they will explain it to you better than I can, and you should ask them. What I can tell you is what it sounds like in a quiet room at three in the morning, and why it frightens people so much.

It frightens people because it sounds like the end, every single time. Every pause sounds like the last one. And after the fourth or fifth pause, the family is exhausted, because they have been saying goodbye at every pause for two hours.

I sat with a family in Leon Valley, a son and two daughters, and the son had not slept in two nights. He was standing by the window with his fists closed, and every time his mother paused, he would step forward. And I finally said to him, "Honey, sit down. She is not in a hurry. If you stand up at every pause, you will be standing for a day and a half."

He sat down. He cried for about a minute. And then he held her hand, and he did not stand up again until it was over.

Here is what I want you to understand. In hospice, we say hearing is one of the last things to go. So even in that breathing, even with the eyes closed, we talk to the person. We tell them the score of the game. We tell them the grandkids are fine. We tell them who is in the room. That is not for us. That is for them.

And the change for this hour — I am going to give you two, one free and one that costs about fourteen dollars.

The free one: you stop narrating the breathing to each other. You stop saying, "Did you hear that? Is that normal? Is that worse?" Not because you can't be scared, but because that room has two conversations going on, and yours is louder. Let the room be quiet. Let them hear your voice, not your worry.

The fourteen-dollar one: a long phone charger. Ten feet. Because the chair is not next to the outlet, and you are going to sit in that chair for hours, and your phone is going to die at the exact moment somebody needs to call somebody. Fourteen dollars at any drugstore. I bought that same cord for families more times than I can count, and I always left it there.

And the check for tonight: put your phone on the charger, sit down, and hold the hand. Not the wrist, not the pulse. The hand. If you are counting the seconds between breaths, you are watching. You are not sitting with them. Both are allowed, honey, but only one of them will be the thing you remember.

Now, before I go on, I have to ask you for something, and then I will let you get back to the bed.

Your mom? Your dad? Your husband? I read every single one of them, right here at this table, with my cafecito, and I answer as many as I can.

Okay. Twelve hours.

And here is where I have to tell you about Walt Pruitt, because families hear about this and they get their hopes up, and I don't want you to get your hopes up the wrong way.

Walt Pruitt was a rancher from Bandera. Big man, sun on his face like leather, hands like shovels. He had been sleeping almost all day for two days. His daughter was in Houston, and she had been told to come, and she was on the road.

And on the morning of the third day — I am going to tell you the truth, because I was there — that man sat up. He asked for eggs rancheros. He asked for the newspaper. He called his daughter on the telephone and talked to her for eleven minutes about the fence on the north pasture and about her boy's baseball team. Eleven minutes.

His wife looked at me with her eyes wide and said, "Rosa, he's better. He's back."

And I had to sit down on the edge of that bed and say, "No, honey. He's not back. This is a gift. Take it."

In hospice, some families call it the rally. Some call it the last lucidity. It does not happen to everybody. But when it happens, it is real, and it is often the last good hour. And the terrible thing about it — the thing I want to tell you — is that some families spend that hour arguing with the hospice team, or calling the office, or saying, "See? He's eating. He's going to be fine." And they miss it.

Do not miss it. That hour is not a sign that the road turned around. It is a window. And windows close.

What I tell families is this: when the window opens, do not use it to talk about the will. Do not use it to fix the argument from 1998. Use it for the small thing. Ask him about the fence. Let him tell you the story about the horse. Say the thing you have been carrying in your pocket for a month. That.

Walt's daughter got there at four in the afternoon. He was awake for about twenty minutes when she walked in. And what he said to her — that is another video, and I will tell you the whole thing in it, because it is the best thing I ever heard in thirty-four years. Today I will just tell you that she got there. She got there in time, because somebody called her the night before and said, "Come now, not next week."

So the change for this hour: call them. Whoever is far away, whoever is "coming when it gets serious." Call them today and tell them to come now. Because you do not get to choose when the window opens, and neither do I.

And the check for tonight: if the window opened today, write down what was said in it. The same notebook. Write it that night, before you sleep, because by next week you will have it half wrong and you will not believe me until you see it.

Now. Six hours.

This is the part I can only tell you from the chair, because there is no chart for it.

Something changes in the room.

I know how that sounds. I am not a mystic. I am a woman who made tamales for forty years and buried a husband's clients and went to Mass on Sundays, and I am telling you that after three thousand rooms I stopped being surprised by this. The room gets quieter. Not the sound. The air. Like the house itself is leaning in.

Families start whispering. Not because anybody told them to. They just do. And people who have been in that room for three days suddenly don't want to leave it, and people who have been avoiding it for three months suddenly drive across town at midnight.

And this is when the ones who have gone come back.

Mr. Delgado and Ramiro. A woman in Alamo Heights who called for her father, in Spanish, and her father had been dead since she was nine. A man who reached up — not out, up — and smiled at the ceiling, and his son said to me, "Chaplain, what is he looking at?" And I said, "I don't know, honey. But look at his face. Does he look afraid to you?"

He didn't. That is the thing I want you to hold in your pocket. In thirty-four years I saw a lot of faces in the last six hours, and I can count on one hand the ones that were afraid. Most of them were doing something else. Waiting. Listening. Sometimes, I swear to you, visiting.

Now, some of this can be the mind wandering. Some of it can be fever, or the medicine, or the disease, and that is why you call the hospice team if something changes fast or frightens you. I am not going to pretend I know which is which. I am a chaplain. I sat with it, I did not explain it.

But here is what I learned to do, and it costs nothing, and it is the thing families thank me for years later.

You say yes.

When your mother says, "Is my sister here?" — and her sister died in 2004 — you do not say, "Mom, no, she's dead." You say, "Is she? Where is she standing?" And you let her tell you. You do not correct them. There is nothing to correct. They are not confused about the past. They are somewhere else, and they are telling you what they see from there, and the kindest thing you can do is listen like it is the most normal thing in the world. Because in that room, honey, it is.

And if it frightens you — if you are the one who is scared — you step out into the kitchen and you call your hospice team, or you call the chaplain, or you call your brother. You do not have to be brave in the room. You just have to be there.

The check for tonight, for this hour: ask them. Not "do you know who I am." Ask them, "Who is here with you?" And then be quiet. Write down the answer. You will be amazed what comes out of that mouth when nobody is arguing with it.

The last hours are not dramatic. They are small. The eyes may stay closed. The mouth gets dry. The hands get cool, and families grab the wrist and start counting and looking at each other. The breathing gets softer, and further apart, and sometimes there is a long, long pause, and then one more.

And the hearing — the hearing is still there. I believe that with my whole heart, and hospice believes it too, and it is why we tell families: keep talking. Tell them who came. Tell them the kitchen is full. Tell them the dog is fine.

So what do you do in the last hours? You say the five things.

I learned this from Lola, and I have taught it to more families than I can count. There are five sentences, and you do not have to say all five. But if you have been lying awake at night wondering what to say, start here.

I forgive you. Forgive me. Thank you. I love you. And — this is the hard one — goodbye.

The hard one is goodbye, because saying it feels like giving permission, and families are terrified of giving permission. They think if they say goodbye, they are the ones who did it.

Honey, listen to me. You are not doing it. You are doing the opposite. You are telling them it is alright to go.
