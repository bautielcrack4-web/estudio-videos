[sighs] In 2011, a woman in San Antonio paid nine thousand eight hundred and forty dollars for her own funeral. One afternoon, at a desk like this one. She signed the paper, she got a folder, and she went home feeling like she had taken care of her family.

She died last March. Her daughter brought that folder to my office, and the funeral home told her it covered four thousand six hundred dollars of the bill. The other six thousand two hundred was due before the burial.

Forty years in this business. Close to seven thousand people. And that folder still keeps me up at night.

In 2009 I sat my own mother down at her kitchen table and told her never to buy one of these. Today I'll show you why, and I'll show you what she did instead, because what she did cost her less and kept her in control.

===

First, the words, because the person across the table is going to use about five of them and none of them mean what you think.

A prepaid funeral. A pre-need contract. A prearrangement. A pre-plan. A final expense policy. They are the same product wearing different suits. You sign now, you pay now, and the funeral happens later, sometimes twenty years later.

There are only two ways the money sits. Either it goes into a trust, which is a pooled account the funeral home opens with a bank, and the state watches it. Or you buy a small life insurance policy, and the funeral home is the beneficiary. That is it. Trust or insurance. If the salesman cannot tell you which one in one sentence, stop him and ask again.

Here is the first thing nobody tells you. The person selling you that contract is usually paid when you sign. Not when you die. When you sign. In Texas, a prepaid funeral seller needs a permit from the Texas Department of Banking, and that permit has a number, and you can ask for it. Ask for it.

In 2014 a gentleman named Tomás Reyes came to my office on Guadalupe Street with a folder from a home that had already closed. He had bought it in 1998. He paid four thousand dollars, in cash, to a man in a nice suit who came to his house. That man was not a funeral director. He was a salesman, and by 2014 he was selling something else in another city.

A contract is a promise from a company. Not from the person who smiled at your kitchen table. That is the whole thing. The company can be sold. The company can close. The person who sold it to you can be gone in a year.

So tonight, before anything else, find the folder. If you do not have one, find the person in your family who does. Open it and look for one page that says trust or insurance, and look for the name of the bank or the insurance company. If that page is missing, you do not have a contract. You have a receipt.

===

Now I want you to do something with me. It takes one minute, and it will tell you more than the rest of this video.

Go get the contract. Not tonight on the phone, right now, if it is in the house. Turn to the page that lists the services and the merchandise, the casket, the vault, the viewing. Halfway down that page there is a word, and the word is guaranteed.

Read it out loud. Guaranteed, or non-guaranteed.

If it says guaranteed, the funeral home is taking the risk. If the price of that casket goes up between now and the day you need it, they eat the difference. That is a real thing, and it has value.

If it says non-guaranteed, or if it says nothing at all, then the number on that page is not a price. It is an estimate. On the day of the funeral, your family pays the difference, at that day's prices, at the worst moment of their lives.

And if you do not have a contract, do this instead. Take a piece of paper and write down the name of the person who would sit at that desk if you died tonight. Not the funeral home. The person. The one who would have to make forty decisions in two days while they are crying. You have ten seconds. Write the name.

If you could not write a name, that is the real problem. Not the contract. The name.

[emphatically] The word guaranteed is the most expensive word in that folder, and it is printed in the smallest type.

I will tell you where I learned it. In 2017, the Ramos family, South San Antonio, off Zarzamora. The father had bought a contract in 2006. The casket was listed at two thousand four hundred dollars. When he died, that same casket, same model, same handles, was three thousand nine hundred. The contract said non-guaranteed. The family paid the difference in the parking lot, in cash, because the burial was the next morning.

Nobody lied to them. That is the part that gets me. The paper was correct. They signed it. They just did not know that one word turned a price into a suggestion.

So here is the change, and it costs nothing. If the contract is non-guaranteed, ask the funeral home to write a guaranteed price for the casket and the vault, in pen, on the contract, and initial it. Some will. Some will not. If they will not, you have learned something about the company before you gave them a dollar.

And for the people who do not have a contract yet: do not sign in your house. Take the paper home. Forty-eight hours. A legitimate funeral home will still be there on Thursday.

===

Now, the price lock. Because this is the promise that sells the contract. They tell you, you are locking in today's prices. And in one narrow way, that is true. In the way that matters, it is not.

A prepaid contract locks the services of that funeral home. The basic services fee, the embalming if you choose it, the use of the room, the hearse, the staff. Those are the things they control.

But a funeral is not only the funeral home. There are what the price list calls cash advance items. Death certificates, which in Texas run about twenty-one dollars each and you usually need ten or fifteen. The obituary in the newspaper. The clergy or the officiant. The flowers. The grave markers. And then the cemetery, which is a separate business with a separate price list.

The National Funeral Directors Association puts the median funeral with viewing and burial at about eight thousand three hundred dollars. That is their 2023 survey. And that number does not include the cemetery plot, the vault, or opening the grave.

Here is what that looks like in real life. 2015, the Salinas family, also on the West Side. Their mother had a prepaid contract for six thousand dollars. She was proud of it. She had paid it off in four years. When she died, the cemetery charged four thousand eight hundred dollars for the plot, the vault, and the opening and closing of the grave. None of it was in the contract. None of it had to be.

Why does it happen? Because the cemetery and the funeral home are two different companies, and the salesperson at the funeral home does not sell cemetery property. And because the Funeral Rule, the federal rule that makes them hand you a General Price List, covers funeral providers. It does not cover the cemetery the same way. So the cemetery answers to state law, and their price list comes when you ask for it, not when you walk in.

The change. Before you sign anything, call the cemetery. Ask for their price list in writing, for a plot, a vault, and the opening and closing. Add those three numbers to the contract in front of you. That is your real price. If the salesman has never mentioned the cemetery, that is not an accident. It is a hole in the number he is showing you.

Tonight's check. Take the contract, and on the back of an envelope, write down three things: the plot, the vault, the opening and closing. If you do not know those three numbers, you do not know what your funeral costs.

===

Now I have to tell you the part that the salesman cannot tell you, because he does not know it and he will not be there.

The company that sold you that contract can be sold. In 2019, the home where I worked on Guadalupe Street was bought by a company out of Houston. The sign stayed the same for about a year. The people changed. I stayed, because I was close to the end, and because the families kept asking for me.

The contracts came with the building. All of them. Nobody called the families. The contract moved, and the families found out when they walked in and saw a different face at the desk.

That is legal, and in most cases it is fine, because a good company honors the paper. But the new owner honors the paper. Not the promise. Not the conversation at the kitchen table. The paper. If the paper says non-guaranteed, the new owner reads non-guaranteed, and he is not being cruel. He is reading.

And if the home closes? In Texas, the contract goes to another provider, or the money comes back out of the trust, and the family has to find somebody to take it. It works, usually. Usually is a hard word when you are sixty miles away and the funeral is on Saturday.

2021. A lady named Ruth Klemens, eighty-four years old, a contract from 2004. The home closed in 2016. It took her eleven months and nine phone calls to get that contract moved to a home on the other side of town. And when it finally moved, the casket on the contract did not exist anymore. The new home substituted the closest model. That is in the fine print too, the substitution clause. It is always in there.

The change. When you read the contract, find the sentence about successors and assignment. It will be near the back. It will say the contract may be assigned to another provider. That is normal. What you want to know is whether the guaranteed price survives the assignment. Ask it in writing, and keep the answer in the folder.

Tonight's check. Look at the top of the contract, the name of the company, and look at the name of the funeral home where the service will happen. If they are different companies, ask which one is promising you the price.

===

Now the exit. What it costs to change your mind. Because this is the number they skip, and it is the number that matters if your life changes, if you move, if your mother decides she wants to be buried next to her sister in another state.

Every one of these contracts has a cancellation paragraph. In Texas, you have a window at the beginning where you can cancel and get your money back. After that window, the seller is allowed to keep part of what you paid, and the contract has to state how much. Some keep a percentage. Some keep the sales commission and the interest. Some keep almost everything until the funeral is delivered.

2013. A man named Beto Cárdenas, seventy-one years old, a retired mechanic. He bought a contract in 2011 for seven thousand two hundred dollars. In 2013 his wife got sick and they needed the money. He went to cancel. The contract let him have four thousand one hundred. Two years, and three thousand one hundred dollars were gone. He had not used one service. Not one.

Where did the money go? Part of it went to the salesman. Part of it went to the company. Part of it was the fee for cancelling. And the rest was still sitting in the trust, waiting for a funeral that might not happen for twenty years.

I am not telling you that every contract is a trap. I am telling you that the money is not in a savings account with your name on it. It is in a product, and products have fees, and the fees are written in the paragraph nobody reads.

The change, and this is the one I would do myself. Read the cancellation paragraph before you read anything else. Not the price. The cancellation. Because the price is what they want you to see, and the cancellation is what happens if you are wrong. Ask them to circle the number you would get back after one year, and after five years, and write it on the front page. If they will not write it, that is your answer.

Tonight's check. Open the folder to the cancellation page and read it with a pen in your hand. Every number you do not understand, underline it, and call the Texas Department of Banking and ask. That is what they are there for. It is free.

===

Now, one more thing before I get to my mother, because I do not want to be unfair to the product. There is one situation where a prepaid funeral is the right tool, and I have used it, and I will tell you the truth about it.

If you or your husband or your wife may need Medicaid for a care home in the next few years, the funeral can be the one thing you are allowed to keep. An irrevocable prepaid contract, set up correctly, is not counted as an asset when the state looks at what you own. That is real. That is the law in Texas, and in most states.

Irrevocable means you cannot take it back. That is the price of the protection. You are trading control for eligibility. If that is your situation, it can be the right trade.

2018, the Ortiz family. The husband went into a care home, and the wife had a prepaid contract she had bought in 2012. Because it was irrevocable, and because it was set up with an attorney, it did not count against him. Without it, they would have spent it down on the care, and there would have been nothing left for the funeral.

But notice what made it work. It was set up correctly, with the right word in the right box, and a lawyer read it before it was signed. Not a salesman. A lawyer who works with older families in your state.

So the change is small and it is free. If Medicaid is anywhere in your future, do not sign a revocable contract and hope. Ask for the word irrevocable. And take it to an elder-law attorney before you sign, not after. One hour of that lawyer's time is cheaper than one year of a mistake.

Tonight's check. If you already have a contract, look for the word irrevocable or revocable. Write it on the front of the folder. That one word tells you whether you can change your mind.

So. My mother. Analco, Guadalajara. 2009.

I flew down for a week in October. The neighbor across the street, Doña Cuca's sister, had died in July, and a young man had come to the house two days after the burial, with a folder and a smile, selling a plan de previsión. Thirty-eight thousand pesos. She had the folder on the kitchen table when I walked in. She was going to sign it on Friday, because the young man told her the price would go up in January.

I sat down, and I said, Mamá, do not sign that. And she said, mijo, but then everything is taken care of. And I said, no. Then you have given a company thirty-eight thousand pesos, and you still have to die, and I still have to call them, and they still have to say yes.

Here is what we did instead. Four things. It took one afternoon and it cost her less.

One. She bought the plot, directly from the cemetery, and she got a receipt with her name on it. Not a contract for a plot. A deed. The ground is hers. Nobody can sell it, nobody can lose it, and if she changes her mind, she can sell it herself.

Two. She opened a separate savings account at her bank, in her name, with me as the beneficiary on death. Four thousand two hundred dollars, that first year. She put in a little every month. That money is hers. She can see it. She can take it out for a plane ticket to see her grandchildren, and nobody can stop her, and nobody keeps a commission.

Three. One page, in her handwriting, with the names and the phone numbers. Mine, my brother's, the priest at San Sebastián, the cemetery office. One page. Because the person who has to make the calls should not have to guess.

Four. Everything in a yellow folder, in the drawer next to the stove, and a photograph of every page on my phone. Yellow, because yellow is the only color I can find in a drawer at two in the morning.

My mother is ninety-one now. That account has grown. The folder is still in the same drawer. And she has never had to call a salesman, and no company has ever held her money.

That is what I would tell my own mother. That is what I did tell her. And I will tell you the same thing, in the same words: you are not buying a funeral. You are lending your money to a company for twenty years, and hoping it is still there when you need it. Keep the money. Write the page. Name the person.

===

Now, the questions. If after all of this you still want to sign, because sometimes it is the right thing, or because your family wants it, then here are the seven questions to ask at that table. Ask them slowly. Write down the answers. If the salesman does not like it, that is information too.

One. Who holds the money, and can I see a statement once a year? If it is a trust, the bank's name. If it is insurance, the policy number.

Two. Is this contract guaranteed or non-guaranteed? Say the word back to him. Make him say it.

Three. What is not covered? Ask for the list in writing, and make him write the cemetery, the vault, and the death certificates on it.

Four. What happens if you sell this business or close? Who honors this contract, and does the guaranteed price survive the sale?

Five. What do I get back if I cancel in one year, in five years, in ten years? Three numbers. Write them on the front page.

Six. Can I move this contract to another funeral home, in another city or another state? If my daughter lives in Phoenix and I go to live with her, what happens?

Seven. What is the total I will pay, in dollars, including every fee, from today until the burial? Not the monthly payment. The total. Make him write the number.

And then the last thing, the one I have been saving.

Remember the signature line. Page two, at the bottom, where you sign. Before you sign, take your own pen, and above your name, write three words in your own handwriting. Guaranteed, or refundable. Six words, I am sorry. Guaranteed or refundable.

If they let you write it, and they initial it, you have a contract with a spine. If they tell you no, if they tell you it is not allowed, if they take the paper away from you, then you have your answer, and you have it before you gave them anything. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, the paper comes back to you. And you walk out with your money.

[emphatically] The signature line is the only line in that folder that belongs to you. Use it.
