[excited] Seven handbags. That is how many were hanging behind my door in Kyoto the year I turned sixty. Seven. And six of them were quietly adding years to my face.

It is not because I am thinner than you. I am five feet tall and I am not a model. It is not because I am richer. It is the weight, the strap, and where the bag sits when I talk to somebody.

The safe advice says: buy one good black bag, put your whole life in it, and match it to your shoes. I did that for years. That advice is the reason I looked tired in every photograph from that time.

The last one on this list is the one nobody in America talks about, because it is not about buying anything. It is about a box in a closet. And at the end you will know exactly what to change tonight, with the bags you already own. Nothing to buy.

My mother, Fumiko, was a kitsuke-shi in Gion. She dressed brides and geiko in kimono for forty years, from 1965 until her hands gave out. I also lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002, because my husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. So I know your closet. I had the big American bag too. I did it the American way.

Mistake one. The bag that carries the whole house.

Carol Whitfield is my best friend from Sunnyvale. She is seventy now, she lives in Sacramento, and she calls me every Sunday night, which is Saturday morning for her. In September she told me she bought a new bag for her trip. She was very proud of it. I asked her to put it on her kitchen scale, empty. Four point six pounds. Empty. That is a bag that has already decided how your day will go.

My neighbour Mrs. Kinoshita is eighty-one. She walks ten thousand steps on the Philosopher's Path every morning. Her bag weighs eleven ounces. I have weighed it, because she let me, and then she laughed at me.

Here is the part nobody says. The bag does not have to hurt to change your face. When I carried four pounds on my left side, my shoulder rolled forward, my chin came forward to balance it, and my whole face went down with it. In photographs I looked like I was listening to bad news. I was not. I was carrying my umbrella, my water, my papers, and a book I never opened.

So, before I go on, stand up. I will wait. Put your bag in one hand, arm straight down at your side, and count to thirty out loud. Then do the other arm. If one arm wants to drop before thirty, that bag is not your bag any more. Nothing is wrong with you. The bag is heavy.

The change: empty it on the table tonight and weigh it. Under two pounds empty. Mrs. Kinoshita's rule, and now mine: if it does not fit in the bag, it goes in the coat pocket.

Mistake two. The bag on one shoulder, with the strap that slides.

You know this one. The strap slips, you lift your shoulder, it slips again, you lift again. Sachiko-san, who owns the cleaner's on the corner of my street, told me in February that I shrugged eleven times while we talked. She counts things. She once told Hiroshi he had worn the same cardigan for three days, and she was right.

[whispering] Eleven times in one conversation. That little shrug is the gesture of a tired woman. It is not the bag that looks tired. It is you, doing that, all day.

After sixty my shoulder is not a shelf any more. The strap has nothing to sit on, so it slides, and I spend the whole day holding it up with my neck.

The change is a strap across the body. Not on the shoulder. Across. The bag sits at the hip, in front, and it does not move. If your bag has no long strap, a strap extender is about twelve dollars, and any bag can take one. I moved the strap on three of my bags and I did not buy a single new bag.

Tonight, put it across, walk to the kitchen and back, and lift both arms above your head. If the bag stays where it is, keep it that way.

Mistake three. The bag carried in the hand, or hanging from the crook of the elbow.

This is the one I see most in America, and it is the one that ages the hand. A bag hanging from two fingers pulls the tendons up, the veins come forward, and the whole arm hangs straight down like it is tired of you.

In June we have the rainy season here, tsuyu. I was at the station stairs behind Mrs. Kinoshita, and she had an umbrella in one hand. Her bag was across her body, so her other hand was free for the railing. Behind her, a woman my age had a bag hooked on her elbow and an umbrella in her fist, and she had to stop at the top to change hands. Mrs. Kinoshita went down those stairs like a girl.

The change: a thin strap, forty-seven inches, worn across. If the bag came without one, Daiso sells a clip-on strap for about nine dollars, and I have three of them in different colours. My mother wore kimono, so she carried her things in the obi. Her rule was simple: the hands are for the hands.

Tonight, put the bag on, then open a door, hold a cup, and pick up a shoe from the floor. One hand free. That is the whole test.

Mistake four. The bag you cannot close.

An open tote is a beautiful thing in a photograph and a bad thing at the Nishiki market on a Saturday.

Mr. Okada sells fabric and tenugui in Nishiki. I have known him since I was a girl, because my mother bought lining silk from his father. In November I was at his stall and he said to me, in Japanese, "the open bag is an invitation." He said it quietly, the way he says everything, and then he went back to folding.

Two weeks before that, my phone slid out of my own bag at Kawaramachi and a schoolgirl ran after me with it. I did not even feel it go. That is the part that stays with me. I did not feel it.

After sixty we carry more, not less: the glasses, the second pair of glasses, the keys, the tissues, the paper with the address. And we look down less, because looking down is a decision now. An open bag is a bag you are trusting everybody else to be kind about.

The change: a zip, or a flap. If your bag has neither, put everything in one small zip pouch inside it, nine dollars, and in a crowd wear the bag in front with your hand on the zip. Not behind you. In front, like a small animal you are carrying.

Tonight, reach behind your back without looking. If your hand finds an open top, that bag is for the garden, not for the train.

Mistake five. The black bag held up at your face.

Here is what my mother did with every bride in Gion. Before the bride left the room, Fumiko held the edge of the obi up under her chin, and then she walked her to the window. Not to the lamp. The window. She said a colour must be tested where the real light is, because a colour that is beautiful under a bulb can be a grey line under the chin at noon.

A bag held under the arm, or a clutch at the chest, is that test. It is a block of colour right under your face, and most of us choose black, because black is safe and it goes with everything.

After sixty the colour leaves my face before it leaves my hands. Everyone tells me this is rude to say. It is not rude. It is just true, and it means black under my chin takes the last of the light with it.

The change: hold the bag low, at the hip, where it is not part of your face, or choose a warm colour: camel, mustard, a dull red like the maples in November. I do have a black bag. It hangs at my hip, and it stays there.

Tonight, stand at the window with the bag under your chin. Then lower it to your hip. Look at your face, not the bag. You will see it in ten seconds.

Mistake six. The little clutch with no strap.

At sixty-eight your hands are busy in a way they were not at forty. There is the railing. There is the umbrella. There is the tray at a party. There is a grandchild's hand, and you do not want to be the grandmother who cannot hold it because both hands are full.

Last December I was at a wedding in Gion, at a reception where everybody stands. A woman my age held a small clutch under her arm for four hours. She never once used her left hand. She could not hold a cup and the bag at the same time, so she put the cup down, picked up the bag, put the bag down, picked up the cup. All evening. Nobody said anything. Everybody saw it.

The change: a small bag with a thin strap that crosses the body, about eighteen dollars, or a chain you add to the clutch you already own. A small bag with a strap is a big bag with free hands.

Tonight, put the clutch on, then open a door, then hold a cup, then pick up a shoe, all with one hand.

Mistake seven. The good bag in the box.

This is the one nobody in America talks about, and it is the one I want you to hear.

My mother had a small black leather bag with a brass clasp, bought in 1978 in Gion. She kept it in a box in the oshiire with a sachet of dried camellia, and she wore it four times a year: a wedding, a funeral, the New Year, and once, I remember, to the dentist.

When she died in 2009 I opened the box. The leather had a crack across the flap, a real crack, like a dry river. Twelve years in a box with a sachet, and the bag had dried out waiting for a good day.

I carry it now. Every day. To the market, to the post office, to the train.

[emphatically] We save the good things for a special day, and after sixty we learn that the day you can walk to the market is the special day.

So before the last part, if this has been useful, press like. It tells Yui which videos to keep making, and she is the one who films me, so be kind to her.

The change: take the bag out of the box tonight. A little neutral cream, nine dollars, the kind for shoes, rubbed in with a cloth. Wear it tomorrow morning. Not to a wedding. To the market.

Seven mistakes, and none of them needed a new bag.

One: the bag that carries the house. Weigh it.
Two: the strap that slides. Put it across.
Three: the bag in the hand. Free your hands.
Four: the open bag. Close it, and wear it in front.
Five: black under the chin. Hold it low, or choose a warm colour.
Six: the clutch with no strap. A thin strap, eighteen dollars.
Seven: the good bag in the box. It is for tomorrow morning.

Do not buy anything. Open your closet and put the seven bags on the bed, and let three of them go. Not because they are bad. Because they are not yours any more.

My mother used to say: if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you. She said it about kimono. It is the same for a bag. The right bag makes you stand straight, and then your face does the rest.

Tell me in the comments what is in your bag right now, all of it, including the thing you have been carrying for a year and never used. Yui reads them to me with my tea, and I answer the ones about the box.
