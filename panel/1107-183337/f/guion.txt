The first goodbye my mother ever said to me came in an empty Blue Bell ice cream tub. The one-gallon kind, homemade vanilla, the one she kept buttons in. She handed it to me on a Sunday afternoon in October of 2014, five months before she died. She said, "Mija, take this home with you. I don't need it anymore."

I opened it in my car, in front of her house on West Mistletoe. Inside was her good rosary. A pair of earrings my father gave her in 1962. A holy card from my first communion. And a folded paper with my grandmother's handwriting on it, a recipe for capirotada that nobody in this family had made since 1979. I sat in that car and cried like a baby. Because by then I had been a hospice chaplain for twenty-three years. I had sat with more than three thousand families. And I still did not recognize the goodbye when it was handed to me in a plastic tub.

That's what I want to talk about today, honey. Not the last breath. That one is usually quiet and short. I mean the other goodbyes. The ones that start a year before, sometimes two. The ones that don't look like goodbyes at all. They look like a mother giving you a tub of buttons. They look like a father asking you, for the third time, how the water heater works.

Today I'm going to show you seven of them. What they sound like, why they happen, and what to do in the moment. Because there is something to do, mija. There is always something to do.

My name is Rosa Navarro. I was born in Guadalajara, in Analco, and when I was seven my family moved to San Antonio, to the West Side, a few blocks from Our Lady of Guadalupe on El Paso Street. I studied pastoral theology at Incarnate Word, and in 1991 I started going house to house for Alamo Home Hospice. Thirty-four years. I retired last year. My husband Ernie is a funeral director, so between the two of us we have seen it all, and we joke about it, because you have to joke a little in this work or you cry every day.

One thing before we start. I was never the one with the medicine. I was the chaplain. In hospice, the team takes care of the body. I sat with the person and with the family, and with whatever they believed. My job was simple. Nobody dies alone, and nobody's family is alone either. So anything medical, you call your hospice team. That's what they are there for, at two in the morning too. What I'm telling you today is what I saw from the chair for thirty-four years. Not from a book. From the chair.

Now do something with me. Even if it's two in the morning, go to the kitchen, get a pen and a piece of paper, and write these five questions with space under each one.

One. What has my mom or dad given away in the last year, even small things?

Two. What have they asked me to do for them, that they always did for themselves?

Three. What story have they told me more than twice since last Christmas?

Four. Who have they mentioned lately, that they haven't seen in years?

Five. What have they said to me that felt strange, and I laughed it off?

Those five answers are your map. Not mine. Yours.

Number one. They start giving things away. And not the way you think.

I told you about my mother's ice cream tub. What I didn't tell you is that there were three more after that one. One in November, one in January, the last one in February, a couple of weeks before she went into the hospital. My brother called me and said, "Rosa, what is she doing? Is she giving up?" He was scared. That's all. He thought that if we stopped her, if we put the things back, we could stop the whole thing.

So I told him what I tell you now. She is not giving up. She is placing things. When you pack for a long trip, you don't throw your whole house in a bag. You go through it. You decide what matters, and who should have it. Your mother is doing that same thing, only she is not going anywhere she can come back from, and she knows it.

There was a woman on the South Side, I'll call her Mrs. Salas, eighty-nine, who crocheted for sixty years. In her last year she started calling her grandnieces, one by one, and giving each one a blanket. Not the pretty ones. The ones with the mistakes in them. One of the girls said, "Why does she keep giving me the ugly ones?" And I said, honey, go look at the mistake. I bet it's in the corner, and I bet it's where she stopped and started again. She is giving you the hours of her life.

Why does this happen? Somewhere in that last year the person starts to understand something the family does not. They are not going to need these things, and the things are starting to feel heavy. Not heavy like a box. Heavy like a responsibility. Handing them to you is a way of saying, I trust you with my life.

But there is another kind of giving away, and it worries me. If your father suddenly gives everything away, all at once, in a week, and he is crying and not sleeping and says "I'm no good to anybody" — that is not the quiet goodbye. That is a person who is depressed, and that needs help right now. Tell your hospice team exactly what you are seeing.

So when the tub comes to you, you take it. Don't say "Mom, keep it, you'll need it." She knows what she needs better than you do. Take it, thank her, and ask one question. "Who do you want to have this?" Not why are you giving it away. Who. Because that question lets her keep placing things, and it shows you the map of her heart.

And write it down. A composition notebook from H-E-B costs about two dollars. The date, what it was, who gave it to you, what she said. In two years you are not going to remember what she said. You are going to remember that she said something, and it will kill you that you didn't write it down.

And if you can, take a picture of her hands holding it. Not her face. Her hands. I have one picture of my mother's hands on a tub of buttons, and it is the only picture from that whole year I can look at without crying.

Tonight's check. Go into your parent's house and look for one container that has things in it that are not trash. A tub. A box. A plastic bag in the hall closet. Open it. Ask yourself, is this my mother placing things? Then sit with her tomorrow and ask who she wants to have it.

Number two. They start telling you the stories again. But this time they tell you why.

You know the story. You have heard it a hundred times. The trip, the cousin, the job, the day your father proposed. You can say the next sentence before she says it. And you tune out. Not because you don't love her. Because you already know it.

Mrs. Ophelia Washington was from the East Side, and she sang in the choir of a Baptist church over there for forty-some years. In her last year she told the same story, over and over, about a choir bus going to Corpus Christi in 1968. The bus broke down outside Karnes City. They were stuck on the shoulder for six hours in the heat. And the choir started singing right there, and people pulled over, and by the end there were strangers standing around the bus crying.

Her daughter had heard that story since she was a little girl. And one afternoon in the spring, Mrs. Washington told it again, and at the end she said something new. She said, "I was so scared that day, baby. I was twenty-two, and I was pregnant with you, and I hadn't told anybody. And I sang so I wouldn't cry."

Her daughter called me that night. She said, "Rosa, I have heard that story my whole life. And I never knew that." All those years, and eleven words that were never in it before.

That is what is happening. They are not repeating. They are editing. Every time they tell it, they go back into that day and take one more thing out of the drawer. And they need somebody in the room who is not tired of it. Because when they finish telling it the last time, with all the pieces in it, that story is finished. And so is that part of their life.

Why does this happen? I think a person at the end of a long life has a house full of rooms, and they go from room to room, turning off the lights. Some rooms they turn off quick. Some rooms they sit in for a while. The stories are the rooms. They are going back to the places where they were most alive, and looking one more time before they close the door.

So get out your phone. Not to film her, honey. To record her voice. There is a voice memo app on that phone. It costs nothing and it is already there. Put the phone face down on the table and let it run. If she asks what you're doing, tell her the truth. "I want to remember your voice." Most of them will say, "Ay, why?" And then they keep talking. They always keep talking.

And then ask her the inside of the story. "What did that feel like?" "Who else was there?" "What did you not say?" Those questions take her deeper into the room, and that is where the last pieces are.

I did this with my own mother and didn't know I was doing it. I have a recording of her telling about the day she met my father, at a dance at the old pavilion. At the end she laughs and says, "Ay, Rosa, he was so handsome, and he was such a bad dancer." And then she is quiet for a second, and she says, "I would do it all again." I have listened to that fifty times. Every time it is the same. She would do it all again.

Tonight's check. Pick the story you are tired of. The one you could tell yourself. Ask her one new question about it. "What did that feel like?" And then be quiet. Let the silence do its work.

Number three. They start teaching you how to do something they always did.

This is the one families refuse. And I understand why. Because when your mother says, "Come here, let me show you how I make the caldo," what you hear is, "I'm not going to be here to make it." And you cannot bear that. So you say, "Mom, you're going to be fine, you don't have to teach me." And she stops. She doesn't insist. She puts the pot back, and something closes in her face that you will remember the rest of your life.

Mr. Aurelio Campos. Everybody called him Lelo. Eighty-one, a retired mechanic with a shop on Zarzamora for forty years. Hands like a catcher's mitt, and a cigar he never lit, just chewed. In April of his last year he had his daughter Yolanda out in the driveway with the hood of her car up. He made her check the oil, check the tires, find the spare, change a fuse. She kept saying, "Dad, I'll just take it to the shop." And finally he looked at her and said, "Yoli, the shop doesn't love you."

She told me that at his funeral, and she laughed and cried at the same time. Because she understood it by then. He was not teaching her about a car. He was teaching her the one thing he knew how to do, the thing that had fed his family for forty years. It was the only inheritance he had. He had a house, a toolbox, and a daughter. And the toolbox was the part he could give her.

Why does this happen? In a family, everybody has a job. Somebody makes the caldo. Somebody knows where the papers are. Somebody remembers the birthdays, and which cousin is not speaking to which, and how to fix the screen door. When the person with that job is getting ready to go, they need to know the job is covered. Not because they are vain. Because the family is their life's work, and they are not going to leave it unfinished.

So you accept the lesson. All of it. Even if you already know how. Even if you are never going to make that caldo in your life. You stand there and you let her teach you.

And you write it down the way she says it. Not the way it should be. "A little of this." "Until it looks right." "A handful." My mother's capirotada recipe has no measurements at all. It says, "bread, until it is enough." Until it is enough. I have made it twelve times and it has never once been the same, and every time it is her.

Get an index card box. About a dollar at any store. Write the recipe on a card, in her words, in her order, with the date and her name at the top. Not because you are going to cook from the card. Because your daughter is going to hold that card one day and see her grandmother's name written in your handwriting.

And then do it with her. Once. Badly. Let her correct you. Let her put her hand on top of your hand and move it the way she wants it moved. That is not about the food, honey. That is a mother touching her daughter's hand. You will not get many more of those.

Tonight's check. Ask her this. "What do you want me to know how to do, that only you know how to do?" And then wait. It might take her a minute. It might take a week. But she has a list.

Number four. They start saying the quiet apology.

This one is hard, because most families don't even hear it. It comes out sideways. About something small. "I'm sorry I never bought you that bicycle." "I'm sorry I made you wear your sister's coat." "I'm sorry I wasn't there when you were sick." And the son or daughter says, "Oh, Mom, don't be silly, you were a wonderful mother." And the apology goes back in the box.

Mrs. Esther Ybarra, from the South Side, was eighty-four. Two children, Danny and Sylvia, who had not spoken in eleven years. Over a house. Over their father's house, who was going to get it, and who said what at the lawyer's office in 2005. Mrs. Ybarra had carried that the whole time like a rock in her chest.

She asked me to call Danny. So I called him. He came from Houston on a Saturday, sat down next to her bed, and she took his hand and said, "Mijo, I'm sorry." He said, "For what, Mom? You didn't do anything." And she said, "For not making you two talk. I let it go, because I didn't want anybody mad at me. And now I'm leaving, and you two still aren't talking. That one is mine. That's on me."

Danny sat there a long time. Then he said, "Okay, Mom." That's all. Okay, Mom. And he called his sister from the driveway. They talked for the first time in eleven years, on the phone, in front of that house. Mrs. Ybarra died nine days later. At the funeral, Danny and Sylvia stood next to each other and held hands, and did not let go.

Why does the apology come at the end? Because the person is doing the last accounting. They go through their life and find the places where they owe something. And they are not asking you to tell them it doesn't matter. They know it matters. That is why they brought it up. They are asking you to receive it, to take it out of their hands so they don't have to carry it further.

So don't argue. Don't say, "You were perfect." She knows she wasn't perfect, and now she also knows you are not listening. Say this instead. "Thank you for telling me." And then, if you can, the rest. "I forgive you." "I love you." "It's okay."

There are five sentences I gave to families for thirty-four years. I forgive you. Forgive me. Thank you. I love you. Goodbye. You can say all five in one minute. Most families never say them, and then spend twenty years wishing they had.

And if the person she needs to talk to is not in the room, a brother in Laredo, a cousin in Mexico, a friend she hasn't spoken to since 1994, you help her do it. A phone call is free. A letter costs one stamp. Fifty-eight cents to take a rock out of your mother's chest. I have seen families drive four hundred miles for less.

Tonight's check. Has your parent said "I'm sorry" about something old this year? Something you waved away? Go back to it. Say, "Mom, remember what you said the other day? I've been thinking about it. Thank you for telling me."

Number five. They start making you promise something. And some of those promises, you should not take.

I know. This is the one that will make some of you angry at me. But I have watched too many daughters carry a promise like a stone for years, and I have to say it.

"Promise me you'll never put me in a nursing home." That is the one. Every family hears it. It usually comes at night, in a small voice, and the son or daughter says, "I promise, Mom. I promise." And then hangs up and sits in the dark.

Don Chuy Maldonado was eighty-six, from Leon Valley, with a little house and a pecan tree in the back. His daughter Alma lived twenty minutes away. In his last year he made her promise. "Mija, promise me I die in this house." And she promised. Of course she promised. You would have promised too.

Eight months later Alma called me, crying so hard she could barely talk. Her back had gone out. She had been lifting him by herself. She hadn't slept in a week. She said, "Rosa, I promised him. I can't do this anymore, and I promised him."

So we went to him together. I sat on one side of the bed, Alma on the other, and she told him the truth. She said, "Dad, I promised you something I don't think I can keep, and I need to tell you now, while you can hear me. I will keep you home as long as I can. And if there comes a day when I can't, I need you to know it's not because I stopped loving you. It's because I'm not strong enough. And I need you to forgive me for that."

And Don Chuy looked at her for a long time. Then he said, "Mija, that's not what I was asking." He said, "I was asking that you don't leave me alone. That's all. I don't care about the house."

He died at home four months later. But the promise nearly broke her, and it was never what he wanted.

[emphatically] Why do they ask for these promises? Because they are afraid. Not of a building. Of being left. Of being a burden and then being put somewhere and forgotten. The promise is not a contract. It is a question, and the question is, are you going to stay with me? And you can answer that without promising something you cannot control.

So don't promise the thing you can't control. Promise the thing you can. Say, "Dad, I can't promise you the future. Nobody can. But I promise you this. I will be in the room. I will not let you be alone. I will fight for what you want, and I will ask you what you want, and I will not decide for you." That is a promise you can keep.

And there is help, honey. This is not all on you. Medicare's hospice benefit pays for the hospital bed, the aide who comes to help with bathing, the supplies, the team, the social worker, and up to five days of respite in a facility so you can sleep, and that one has a small copay, five percent. And there is a social worker whose whole job is to help you figure out what is possible in that house. Ask for them.

Tonight's check. What did you promise this year that you are already afraid you can't keep? Write it on that paper. Then call the hospice social worker tomorrow and say, "I need help with this." They will not judge you. They have heard it a hundred times.

Number six. They start saying goodbye to places.

This one looks like restlessness. Sometimes like confusion. The person who has not left the house in months suddenly wants to go for a drive. Or they want to see the old house. Or they want a plate of food from a restaurant that closed in 1998. And families say, "Why do you want to go there? There's nothing there. It's not the same."

Mrs. Hortensia Ríos. Everybody called her Tencha. Seventy-nine, from the West Side, and she raised five children in a little house on Guadalupe Street. In the fall of 2021 she started asking her daughter to drive her past that house. Just past it. She didn't want to get out. She didn't want to knock. She wanted to look at it from the car.

Her daughter called me. "Rosa, is this the dementia? Is she confused?" And I said, no, honey. She is not confused. She is saying goodbye to the street. Take her.

So they rented a van. About eighty dollars for the afternoon. They put her in the back with a pillow and a blanket and drove slow. Down Zarzamora. Past Our Lady of Guadalupe, where she was baptized, where she was married, where all five of her children were baptized. Past the house on Guadalupe Street. She looked at it a long time and said, "The tree is still there." That's all. The tree is still there.

Then she asked for barbacoa from a little place on the corner her husband used to go to on Sunday mornings. They got it. She ate two bites and was happy. She died in February.

Why does this happen? Because a person who is leaving wants to see the world they built one more time. Not because they forgot it. Because they are taking leave of it. It is a farewell tour, honey. And if you say "there's nothing there," you are telling them their whole life is nothing.

So take them. Or bring the place to them. If they cannot travel, bring a photograph. A video somebody took on their phone. The food, even if it comes out wrong. Sit with them and let them tell you about it. You are bringing the place into the room.

If you need help with transport, call the hospice social worker. There are wheelchair vans. There are volunteers. A tank of gas is about forty dollars. That is the price of a goodbye to a street.

And take a picture. Not of her face. Of her in front of the door, if she can. Or the tree, with her looking at it. You will want that picture. I promise you, you will want that picture.

Tonight's check. Is there a place your parent keeps mentioning? A house, a church, a street, a restaurant, a town? Ask about it tomorrow. Then ask, "Do you want to go?" And if they say yes, honey, go.

Number seven. They start saying things that sound like nothing.

This is the one I want you to remember, because it is the quietest, and it gets missed the most.

"I've had a good life." "You're going to be fine without me." "I'm tired." "Take care of your brother." "Don't worry about me." "It's okay."

They sound like small talk. Something you say at the door. And families answer them like small talk. "Dad, you've got years left." "Don't talk like that." "You're not going anywhere." And the door closes.

Mr. Clarence Whitfield. Bud. Eighty-eight, from Alamo Heights, a retired schoolteacher. He taught history at the same high school for thirty-one years, and his mind was sharp right to the end. In his last year, every time his daughter left the house, he said the same thing at the door. "You know, I've had a good life."

And she would say, "Dad, you have plenty of life left. Don't talk like that." Every time. And every time he would smile and nod, and she would drive home.

One day she said it to me in the kitchen. "Rosa, why does he keep saying that? It scares me." And I asked her a question. I said, honey, what if he is not asking for reassurance? What if he is asking you to agree with him?

She looked at me like I had slapped her. Then she cried. Then the next time she went over, at the door, he said it again. "You know, I've had a good life." And she said, "You have, Dad. You really have." And he said, "Thank you." And he never said it again.

He died six weeks later. She told me at the funeral, "Rosa, that thank you was the last real conversation I had with my father. And I almost missed it, because I was too busy telling him he was wrong."

Why do they say these things? Because some of these sentences are a door. The person is testing, quietly, whether you can hear what they are really saying. If you argue with the sentence, you close the door. If you agree with it, you walk through it with them.

So what do you say? Three things. "You have." "I know." And "I'll be okay. And I'll miss you every day." That's the whole answer. You don't have to fix it. You just have to say yes.

And this is not giving up. I am not telling you to agree that your father is dying. I am telling you to stop arguing with the one thing he is trying to tell you. There is a difference between hope and denial. Hope says, "I will be here with you, whatever comes." Denial says, "Don't say that." One of them is love. The other one is fear wearing love's coat.

Tonight's check. This week, listen for one sentence that sounds like nothing. One. "I'm tired." "You're going to be fine." "It's okay." And when you hear it, don't correct it. Just say, "I know, Dad. I know."
