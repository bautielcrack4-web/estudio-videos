[warmly] Hello, please come in. Sit by the window, where the light is soft. The garden is quiet this morning, so we have time.

Last spring, the week I turned sixty-eight, I cleared one drawer in my bedroom. It was the drawer where I kept the tops I no longer wore but could not throw away. I laid everything on the tatami and counted. Ten tops. I gave every one of them away, to my neighbor, to my daughter-in-law, to a charity box near the station, and I have not missed a single one.

Here is what was in that drawer, because naming them is half the lesson. A black turtleneck, tight at the throat. A stiff white shirt with a pointed collar. A sleeveless shell in shiny satin. A cropped boxy jacket. A blouse with flowers the size of saucers. A ribbed jersey top that clung to my ribs. A thick acrylic turtleneck sweater. A zip-up fleece vest. A top with small sequins sewn all over it. And a long tunic with balloon sleeves that swallowed my hands.

Ten tops, and every one had a reason. None of those reasons had anything to do with my body being wrong. The reasons were in the cloth, in the cut, in the way a seam sits on a woman of sixty-eight.

I am not an expert. I say that clearly. I am simply a woman who stood behind a counter in a small neighborhood shop in Kyoto for many years, watching how a collar sits and how a woman stands when she feels right in what she wears. My mother did the same work, and her mother before her. We sold ordinary clothes to ordinary women.

It began in my mother's shop, near the Nishiki market. I was nine years old, folding socks behind the counter. In 1987 a woman came in, Mrs. Okada, who had five children and a beautiful laugh. She asked for the same blouse she had bought the year before, and then she said, in a small voice, one size smaller, please. I brought her the smaller blouse. She stood in front of the mirror and pulled at the waist and pulled at the shoulders, and her face told me everything. I went to my mother. My mother looked at Mrs. Okada for a long moment, then brought her the size she had worn the year before, and a different blouse with a softer shoulder. Mrs. Okada put it on and stopped pulling. She stood still. That was the first lesson I remember: the number is not the point. The cloth falling is the point.

I want to tell you something else. This is not about looking younger. I have no interest in that, and I have nothing to sell you. This is about clothes fitting the body you have today, with the ease a real body deserves. Your body has carried children, and bags of rice, and years of small ordinary work. It does not need to be corrected. It needs to be dressed properly. Let us take our time.

Now, before the ten, let me give you something to do, because watching is not the same as feeling. Stand up, take off your outer layer, and stand in front of a mirror in ordinary daylight.

First, put two fingers on the small bone at the base of your throat. Look at how much skin is visible between that bone and the neckline of your top. If the cloth comes all the way up to your throat, and your chin presses down when you look at your phone, that top is asking your neck to do work it cannot do.

Second, roll your shoulders forward and back, slowly. Does the fabric follow you, or does it hold you? If the shoulder seam pulls toward your ear, the top is too small in one place and too big in another, and no size tag will tell you that. The tag measures the body the shop imagines. It does not measure you.

Third, lift your arms as if you were reaching for a jar on the top shelf. Does the hem climb up past your waist? If it does, and you have to pull it down every few minutes, then the top is not your top. Nothing is wrong with you. The cut is wrong. Write those three things down: the neck, the shoulder, the hem. That is your test, and it takes four minutes, with nobody watching and nobody selling you anything.

Now let me go through the ten, in the order I took them out of the drawer.

The first was the black turtleneck. The problem was the neck. A tight turtleneck holds the throat like a hand. On a woman of sixty-eight, whose skin has softened and whose jaw has settled a little lower, it makes the face seem to sit on top of the cloth instead of inside it, and it asks the eye to look exactly where we do not want it to look. I remember Mrs. Sato, in 2004, who wore one to her grandson's wedding and spent the whole ceremony with a finger inside the collar. She told me afterward that her throat felt hot. Of course it did. The fabric had no air. What I wear instead is a white cotton scoop-neck t-shirt, about a thousand yen, with the neckline low enough to show the collarbone and wide enough that the cloth falls away from the throat. The collarbone is the one line that never disappears, even as the years go. Show it, and the whole face lifts without anything being lifted. If you want warmth at the neck, use a scarf you can remove, not a collar sewn shut. Today, take the tightest thing you own near your throat and put it in a bag for the charity box. Notice how you breathe when you do it.

The second was the stiff white shirt with the pointed collar. The problem was the point. A crisp point is beautiful on a flat chest and a straight back, and it was designed for a body that stands still. When the shoulders soften and round a little, as mine have, the point turns up at the corners and the shirt looks as if it is arguing with you. In 2012 I sold a shirt like that to a teacher named Mrs. Ono, who taught calligraphy. She came back three days later and said the collar was standing up in all the photographs, and she looked cross in every one. She was not cross. The collar was. What I wear instead is a collarless linen-cotton shirt, about three thousand two hundred yen, with a soft band at the neck that sits flat against the skin. Without a point, there is nothing to twist. The line of the neck goes up and the face stays the focus. Or I wear it open over a white tee, and the two soft edges make a quiet V. Today, look at your collared shirts from the side in a mirror. If a corner lifts away from your neck, it will do that in every photograph you take.

The third was the sleeveless shell in shiny satin. The problem was the shine. Satin catches light and turns a surface into a shape, and it catches every small change of the body underneath. It is a fabric that announces. If you are comfortable in it, that is fine, and I would never tell a woman what to wear. But for me, at sixty-eight, the shine was doing all the talking and I was doing none. In 1998 my cousin Yuriko wore a satin shell to a family dinner, and every photograph of that night is about the fabric and not about her. What I wear instead is a sleeveless top in matte cotton-linen, about two thousand four hundred yen, in a color close to my own skin but a little deeper, so it does not glare. Matte cloth absorbs light. It lets the eye rest and then find the face. The armhole matters more than the shoulder: it should sit under the arm without pinching, and the undergarment strap should not show when you move. Today, hold a piece of fabric up to a window. If the light shines through it like a mirror, it will shine on you too.

The fourth was the cropped boxy jacket. The problem was where it stopped. It stopped exactly at the widest part of me, and a hem that ends at the widest place draws a line across it, and the eye follows the line. I bought that jacket in 2017 at a shop near Shijo, and I wore it twice. The second time was at a concert with my friend Kiyoko, and in the photograph the jacket looked like a box that had landed on me. I laughed, and then I understood something. What I wear instead is a long unlined linen jacket, about four thousand eight hundred yen, that falls to the top of the thigh. It has no structure, no shoulder pad, no lining to stiffen it. It moves when I move, and the hem ends in a quiet place in the middle of the body, where nothing is being announced. Unlined is important. A lining makes the jacket hold its shape, and the shape it holds is not always yours. Today, try on your shortest jacket and turn sideways. If the hem sits at the widest part of your hip, put it aside.

The fifth was the blouse with the flowers the size of saucers. The problem was the scale. A large print is a loud voice, and a loud voice is wonderful in a room where you want to be heard. But it competes with the face. On an older face, which is quieter and more detailed than a young one, a huge print pulls the eye away and leaves the face behind. I learned this from a customer in 1991, Mrs. Hayashi, who wore a blouse with roses the size of cabbages to meet her daughter's future in-laws. She was charming and kind, and nobody could see her. What I wear instead is a blouse in the same family of color, tone on tone, with a small woven texture and no print at all, about two thousand eight hundred yen. If I want a pattern, I choose something small and close to the color of the cloth, so it reads as texture from a distance and as detail up close. The face should be the loudest thing in the room. Today, hold your favorite printed blouse at arm's length from the mirror. If the flowers are still the first thing you see, they are too big.

The sixth was the ribbed jersey top that clung to my ribs. The problem was the grip. Ribbed jersey holds the body like a second skin, and a second skin shows every line of what is under it: the seam of the undergarment, the small fold at the waist, the shape of the shoulder blade. For thirty years I thought that was the point, that a good top showed the body. Then I understood that grip is not fit. Fit is when the cloth hangs from the shoulder and falls, touching here and there, but never holding. In 2022 I wore that ribbed top to a lunch with my old colleague Aiko, and she touched my sleeve and said, Michiko, you look tired. I was not tired. The top was pulling me down. What I wear instead is a medium-gauge knit in cotton with a little silk, about four thousand yen, with the shoulder seam dropped a little so the fabric leaves from the shoulder rather than from the arm. Dropped shoulder is a word to remember. It means the seam sits lower, the sleeve is softer, and the cloth has room to fall. Today, put on a tight knit and pinch the fabric at the side of your waist. If it springs back against your fingers like a rope, it will do the same all day.

The seventh was the thick acrylic turtleneck sweater. The problem was the weight. Acrylic is light in your hand but warm in a heavy way, and a thick sweater makes a mass at the shoulders and the neck. It holds heat and does not breathe, so by early afternoon you feel damp and tired without knowing why. I wore one like that for years in February, and I would come home at three o'clock and want to lie down. I thought it was simply what afternoons had become. It was the sweater. What I wear instead is a fine merino wool knit in a light gauge, about five thousand five hundred yen, thin enough to wear under a jacket and warm enough that I do not need a second one. Fine wool breathes. It sits close to the skin without gripping. And because it is thin, I can wear a scarf and take it off at lunch, which is better for a neck than a collar sewn on. I am not talking about health here. I am talking about heat, and comfort, and the way a heavy sweater makes the shoulders tired by the end of the day. Today, put your heaviest winter top on a kitchen scale. If it weighs more than four hundred grams, it is doing more work than you are.

The eighth was the zip-up fleece vest. The problem was the zipper and the armhole. A vest with an armhole that cuts straight down makes a square of the shoulder, and a zipper down the front makes a hard vertical line in the middle of the body. Together they turn a soft woman into a column. I bought that fleece for a trip to Kanazawa in 2019, thinking it was practical, and I felt like a coat rack the entire train ride. What I wear instead is a cotton overshirt, about three thousand six hundred yen, with a soft collar, buttons down the front, and sleeves I can roll to the elbow. It works as a light jacket in spring and as a layer in autumn, and the armhole is cut round, not square, so the shoulder stays soft. When I want warmth without bulk, I wear a thin knit under it and I am done. Today, stand with your arms down in your vest and look at the armhole. If it cuts a straight line from the shoulder to the underarm, the shoulder will look square no matter what you do.

The ninth was the top with the sequins. The problem was the noise. Sequins are beautiful under candlelight, and they are also a texture that catches on everything and makes a small sound when you move. I wore mine once, in 2015, to a New Year's dinner at the house of my friend Sachiko. By the end of the evening I had a sequin stuck to my sleeve, one on the tatami, and one in the teacup. What I wear instead is a blouse in silk-cotton with a soft, quiet sheen, about six thousand yen in a good shop, and a fraction of that in a secondhand shop, which is where I found mine. The sheen is in the weave, not on the surface. It catches light when you turn, and then it lets it go. That is the difference between a fabric that shines at you and a fabric that glows. Today, look at your going-out top in daylight, not in the shop light. If it sparkles when you are standing still, it will be doing that all night.

The tenth was the long tunic with the balloon sleeves, the one that swallowed my hands. The problem was the amount of cloth. A balloon sleeve gathers at the wrist and puffs above it, so all that fabric hangs from the shoulder and the whole garment pulls down. And a tunic long enough to reach the knee, worn over trousers, makes two long lines that compete. A dear friend, Mrs. Tanabe, wore a tunic like that to her retirement party in 2003, and she looked exhausted in every photograph, though she was the happiest woman in the room. The cloth had tired her. What I wear instead is a shorter tunic, about three thousand four hundred yen, with a narrow sleeve that ends at the wrist bone and a hem that ends at the top of the thigh. The narrow sleeve shows the wrist, and the wrist is a beautiful place on any woman, at any age. The shorter hem gives the trousers room to be seen. Today, raise your arm in your widest-sleeved top. If the cuff slides back over your hand and you have to pull it down, the sleeve is too wide.

Now let me tell you what happened after the drawer was empty. I did not go and buy ten new tops. That would have been the same mistake with new cloth. I bought three: the white scoop-neck tee, the collarless linen shirt, and the thin merino. Three, and I wear them in rotation with what I already had. The drawer is not full any more, and getting dressed in the morning takes me four minutes, because everything in it fits and nothing in it argues with me. That, to me, is elegance. Not the number of things, but the absence of argument.

There is one more thing I want to give you, and it costs nothing. Before you buy anything, hold it up and look at the shoulder. The shoulder is where a garment begins. If the shoulder is right, the rest can be fixed. If the shoulder is wrong, nothing can be fixed. Then look at the hem. Then look at the neck. Shoulder, hem, neck. That is the order my mother taught me, and it has not failed me in fifty years. And when you stand in a shop, in that flattering light they use, remember this: the shop light forgives everything. Take the garment to the doorway, where the daylight is honest, and look again. If it still fits by the door, it will fit in your kitchen.

[emphatically] Your clothes speak before you do. My mother said that to me when I was ten years old and I did not understand it. Now I am sixty-eight, and I understand that the clothes are not a costume and they are not a promise. They are the first sentence of the day. They should be true.

So here is my question for you, and I would like to read your answer. Which top in your own drawer is the one you keep pulling at? Not the one you dislike the most, but the one your hands keep touching and adjusting without you noticing. Tell me its color, and tell me what you pull at. I will read every reply, here by the window, with my tea.

Let us take our time. Small things, done every day. And next week, please come back, because I want to show you the one drawer where I keep my scarves, and why three of them are enough. Until then, take care of your hands. They do more for you than you remember.

[softly] Thank you for sitting with me. Goodbye.
