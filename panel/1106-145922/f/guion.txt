Carol called me last Sunday from Sacramento. She is seventy. She is five foot two. And she has worn the same black leggings every day since she retired. Then she sent me a photograph. She was standing in her kitchen at seven in the morning with a coffee cup in her hand, and she looked tired. Not tired from her life. Tired from the clothes. She asked me one question. Michiko, she said, why do my leggings make me look like I gave up?

Carol. Leggings do not make a woman look sloppy. Three things do. And none of them is your body.

I am five feet tall. Five feet, zero inches. I am sixty-eight, I am not thin, and I have worn leggings in this city for thirty years. Under a coat in February at thirty-five degrees. In August at ninety-five. In the photographs where I look put together, I am doing seven things. In the ones where I look tired, I am missing one of them.

The seventh one almost no woman in America knows. A tailor behind the Nishiki market does it for twelve dollars, in two days. By the end of this video you will know exactly what to change tonight, with the pair you already own. You do not have to buy anything.

My name is Michiko Arai. I live in the wooden house my family has kept in Nishijin, the weavers' quarter of Kyoto. My mother was Fumiko. For forty years she was a kitsuke-shi. She dressed brides and geiko in kimono in Gion. Three thousand women, and almost every one of them stood in a doorway ten minutes before a wedding, and she had to fix them with her two hands. What my mother knew about a hem, she knew from that doorway.

I also lived nineteen years in California. Sunnyvale, from nineteen eighty-three to two thousand two. My husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. In nineteen ninety-four I wore leggings to the supermarket in Sunnyvale and I thought I looked fine. I have the photographs. I did not look fine.

Every week I see videos that tell you: no leggings after sixty. Long trousers only. [excited] My mother would have laughed. In Kyoto, women in their seventies wear leggings every day. Under a skirt. Under a long shirt. What they never do is wear them the way they were sold to you.

So. Seven things. We go in order, from the ankle up.

Number one. The hem.

The mistake is buying a pair that is long, because long feels safe, and then letting the extra fabric sit on the ankle like a sock that fell. On a tall woman it is a small thing. On a five-foot woman it is the whole leg.

Two years ago my granddaughter Yui took a photograph of me at the Kamogawa. She was fifteen then. She looked at her screen and said, Grandma, your legs look short in this one. I looked. She was right. There was one inch of black fabric folded over the top of my sandal, and it took an inch off my leg.

My mother used to say a hem is not where the cloth ends. It is where the leg ends.

After sixty the calf loses a little shape, so the fabric stops being held up by the muscle and it hangs. And the ankle is exactly where a short woman cannot afford to lose anything.

What to change. Put the pair on barefoot and stand on a wood floor, not carpet. The hem should stop at the ankle bone, and not one centimeter below it. If it is longer, take it to the tailor on Imadegawa-dori, the one beside the rice shop, and ask her to shorten it and keep the original hem. Eight dollars. Or, tonight, turn the hem up inside, one fold, and pin it. One fold. Two folds make a cuff, and a cuff on a short leg is a brick.

Number two. The shine.

The mistake is the fabric itself. Not the cut. Not the size. The fabric.

In twenty sixteen I took a pair of leggings to Sachiko-san, who owns the cleaning shop at the corner of our street. She has a nose like a dog and eyes like a hawk, and she does not soften anything. She held my leggings up to the window with two fingers and said: Michiko, this one is plastic. She was right. They were a cheap pair with a very high stretch, and every time I stood up, the knee caught the light.

Here is why it happens after sixty. When you walk, the knee is the joint that moves most, so it is the place where the fabric is pulled thinnest. A high-stretch fabric goes shiny where it is pulled. In a photograph, a shiny knee reads as a grey patch. People do not see cheap fabric. They see a tired knee.

What to change. Matte cotton with some stretch, not the slick kind. The pair I wear now cost fifteen dollars at the shop on Kawaramachi, and they are cotton on the outside. They do not shine.

Here is the test. Hold the legging up to the window at arm's length, with the light behind it. Put your hand behind the fabric. If you can see the outline of your fingers, that pair is for sleeping. It is not for the street. Cotton with a little stretch lets light through as a glow, not as a hand shape.

Number three. The waistband.

The mistake is pulling them up as high as they will go, over the belly, because it feels safe. At noon there is a red line across your stomach, and the whole body looks like it is being held by a rope.

In two thousand two, the week we left Sunnyvale, Carol brought me a pair of black leggings with a wide elastic band. I wore them on the plane to Kansai. There is a photograph of me at the airport with Hiroshi, and you can see the band through my shirt, as a line. Twenty-two hours in the air, and that line is the only thing I remember.

After sixty the waist widens a little and the band starts to roll. On a petite body it is worse, because a high band eats the torso, and the torso is the length you actually have. If the band comes up to your ribs, your legs start at your ribs, and the eye reads a short woman sitting down.

What to change. Mid-rise. The band sits on the hip bone, not above the belly. And the top you wear over it has to come down past the band, to the hip. Not to the waist. To the hip. That is free, and it is the whole fix.

Tonight, sit on the lowest chair in your house. Cross your legs. Put your hand on the band. If it has rolled down into a rope, that pair is for sleeping, and you know it.

Now let us do the test together, because three tests in one mirror is how you actually see it. Go to your bedroom tonight, take three minutes, and do these in order.

One. Barefoot, on the wood floor, look at the ankle bone. That is the hem test.

Two. Take the pair off and hold it up to the window. The hand test. That is the fabric.

Three. Put them back on, sit on the lowest chair, and cross your legs. That is the band test.

If a pair fails one of the three, it is not a bad pair. It is a pair with one job. The ones that fail all three go in the drawer for sleeping, not to the bakery. That is the change, and it costs nothing.

Number four. Leggings as trousers.

The mistake is thinking that because they are black, they are opaque. They are not. And you never see your own back in the mirror.

In twenty twenty-two my granddaughter Yui came home from Doshisha in the rain, in a pair of thin leggings, and she stood in the genkan and laughed and said, Grandma, I have been walking around like this all day. The rain had made them transparent at the seat. She is twenty-two and she did not know. I am sixty-eight and I only knew because a cleaner held my leggings up to a window.

After sixty the cotton thins where you sit, and it thins slowly, over years, in a place you cannot see. You look in the mirror in the morning and everything is fine. You are looking at the front.

What to change. A shirt or a tunic that comes to the middle of the thigh. Not a jacket. Not tied at the waist. A straight shirt, untucked, that ends mid-thigh. The one I wear most is linen. Mr. Okada, at the fabric stall in the Nishiki market, keeps the ends of the bolts for me. Nine dollars for a piece long enough for a shirt. My neighbor sews it in an afternoon.

Tonight, stand in the doorway with the light behind you, and put one hand inside the fabric at the back. If you can see the shape of your hand, the shirt is too short.

Number five. The color at your face.

The mistake is the black top over the black leggings, because it is easy, and because someone told you black is slimming. Black is slimming. It is also the color that takes the light away from your chin.

I told you about my mother's box of obi scraps in the video about the eight color pairs. She kept silk ends from forty years of dressing women, and before she chose anything for a woman, she held a piece under the woman's chin. Not near the shoulder. Under the chin. She said the face is the only thing a stranger reads.

After sixty the skin loses a little color, all of us, and black takes what is left. A black legging from the knee down is not the problem. Black at the chin is.

What to change. Nothing. Open the closet and take out three tops you already own: one cream, one soft blue, one in a warm color, mustard or rust. Hold each one under your chin at the window and look at your face, not the shirt. In ten seconds you will see which one makes your eyes come forward. Wear that one. The black leggings stay.

Number six. The line at the ankle.

The mistake is the shoe. A white sneaker with a black legging cuts the leg in two at the ankle. On a tall woman it is a style. On a five-foot woman it is a ruler.

My neighbor Mrs. Kinoshita is eighty-one. She walks the Philosopher's Path every morning, ten thousand steps, in the same flat leather shoes she has had for fifteen years. Last spring she bought white trainers, and she wore them with dark trousers, and in the photograph from that morning her legs looked shorter than mine, and she is taller than me. She gave the trainers to her grandson.

Why it happens is simple. The eye counts the leg from the hem of the legging to the ground. If the shoe is a different color, the eye stops at the ankle and counts again. Two pieces. Two short pieces.

What to change. A flat shoe in the same family as the legging. Dark brown, black, navy. The shoe shop on Sanjo has them for twenty-five dollars, and they last. If you love sneakers, wear the sneaker in the same color as the legging, so the line continues.

Tonight, put the shoes on and stand at the mirror. Look only at the ankle. One line, or two? You want one.

Number seven. The last one, and almost no woman in America knows it.

The mistake is the shape of the leg. Most leggings are cut to taper, narrow at the ankle, because that is what a model's leg does. On a short woman, a taper makes the leg look like a cone, and a cone looks short.

After sixty the calf changes shape, so the taper does not follow the leg any more. It grips.

Here is what the tailor does, and it takes two days. Turn the legging inside out. Look at the seam on the outside of the leg, the long one. From the knee down to the ankle, that seam curves in. He takes in one and a half centimeters along that seam, from the knee to the ankle, and sews it again. Twelve dollars. The leg becomes straight. It hangs, it does not grip, and the whole leg looks longer, because your eye can finally travel down it.

The tailor I use is on the second floor behind the Nishiki market. He has a measuring tape around his neck from morning to night. The first time I brought him one pair, and he looked at it for a long time. Then he said: the hem was never your problem. And he was right.

My mother said: if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you.

Tonight, turn the pair inside out and look at the outside seam, below the knee. If it curves in, that is the one and a half centimeters. That is the whole secret, and it costs twelve dollars.
