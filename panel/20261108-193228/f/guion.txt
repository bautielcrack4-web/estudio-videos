Hello, please come in. Sit here, by the window, where the light is good.

I want to begin with a number. Two minutes. That is how long my mother stood at the basin in our kitchen every evening, washing her hands. Not two minutes with a cream, not with a bottle from a shop. Two minutes with warm water, a plain cotton cloth, and the small towel she kept folded on a hook by the window. She was sixty-one then, and her hands were the first thing people noticed about her. Not because they looked young. Because they looked warm.

I am Michiko. I am sixty-eight years old, and I have lived in Kyoto my whole life. I grew up in a machiya house, the kind with a long narrow entrance and a garden you could see from the kitchen. For many years I worked in a small neighborhood shop, helping women choose clothes. In that work, you learn to look at hands. A woman lifts a sleeve, turns a hem between her fingers, holds a collar up to the light, and her hands tell you how she lives. Some are always cold. Some are rough from garden work, some from paper and ink, some from years of holding a child. I am not an expert in anything. I am only old enough to have seen what lasts.

Tonight I want to give you one small habit. Not a product, not a treatment, not a promise. A two-minute evening ritual for hands that feel warm and soft. You can do it tonight, at your own basin, with what you already own. Let us take our time.

Before we begin, let us look at your own hands together. This is the part where you do something, not just listen.

Turn on one lamp, and hold your hands palm down under it. Look at the back of your hand for a moment. Do you see the small lines at the knuckles? Some are there from making a fist all your life. They are not damage. They are a record. Now press the skin on the back of your hand gently with one finger, hold it for three seconds, and let go. Watch how it comes back. Now rub your palms together, slowly, for five seconds, and stop. Do they feel warm? Or do they stay cool? Just notice. That is all I ask for now. Nothing more.

Now. The first thing, and the one most people get wrong.

We wash our hands in the hottest water we can bear, with a soap that foams like a cloud, and then we wonder why they feel tight one minute later. In the winter of 1979, my mother was handling silk for a shop near Shijo, folding and wrapping it all day. Her hands became so dry that the silk began to catch on her skin. My grandmother, her mother-in-law, watched her for a week and then told her to stop using hot water. That was all. Stop the hot water. My mother did, and by the end of that winter the silk no longer caught.

Why does it happen? Hot water takes away the oil your skin makes by itself. A soap with strong foam takes a little more. Then the dry air of a Kyoto winter takes the rest. Your hands are not damaged. They are simply bare, and nobody has covered them again.

So here is the change, and it is small. Use lukewarm water — the temperature of your wrist, not the temperature of tea. Use the soap you already have, but less of it, a drop the size of a grain of rice. And rinse for the full two minutes. Count them slowly if you like. Two minutes is not long when you are counting, and it is enough to let the water warm the skin without stripping it.

[emphatically] And do it today. The next time you stand at the tap, turn it a little toward cold. Just a little. That is the whole first step.

The second thing is the one almost everyone forgets. Drying.

We dry our hands the way we close a door. Quickly, on a towel, palms only. My grandmother did the opposite. I remember her in the winter of 1968, standing at the basin with a plain cotton cloth, drying each finger from the base to the tip. Then she turned the cloth and dried between every finger, one by one, slowly, as if she were counting them. She left the cloth folded in a neat square on the edge of the basin. I asked her why she took so long. [whispering] She said, water left in the folds keeps the hand cold.

She was right, and it is easy to see why. The space between two fingers is where the water stays longest. The towel never reaches it if we only dry the palm. When the evening air cools, that little bit of water makes the hand feel cold, and the skin there is the first to turn rough.

So change this: use a plain cotton towel, and dry for thirty seconds instead of five. Dry each finger separately. Then go between each finger, one by one. That is the entire change. Any cotton cloth will do. A good one costs perhaps five hundred yen in any shop, and the truth is you already own one.

[emphatically] Tonight, after you wash, dry each finger by name in your head. Thumb. First finger. Middle. Ring. Little. Then the spaces between them. You will feel the difference before you go to bed.

The third thing is the slow part, and it is the part my mother loved most. The massage.

In the spring of 1985, a customer came to the shop for a light coat. Her name was Mrs. Ito, and she kept her gloves on the entire time she was choosing. When she finally took them off to feel the fabric, her hands were red and stiff, and she apologized for them. Michiko, she said, I do not know what to do with my hands anymore.

I showed her what my mother did, and I will show you now. No cream, no oil, nothing from a bottle. A plain cloth, folded twice, and two minutes of slow work. Start at the palm, and press in small circles, ten times. Then take each finger between your thumb and the cloth, and roll it slowly from the base to the tip. Then turn your hand over and rub the back of it, gently, with the flat of the cloth. That is all. The speed matters more than the pressure. Go at the speed of a clock's second hand.

I am not going to tell you it changed her hands. I am only going to tell you what I remember. She came back in May without her gloves, and she bought a lighter coat.

Why does it matter? Because hands work all day and are never asked to rest. They hold the bag, the kettle, the steering wheel, the door, the grandchild. Two minutes of slow rubbing brings warmth back into them, and warmth is what makes a hand feel soft in the evening. Not a cream. Warmth, and time.

And here is the fourth thing, which is the smallest and the most important. Where the habit lives.

A habit that is done when you remember is not a habit. It is a hope. My mother did not do this when she remembered. The kettle went on at seven o'clock every evening for tea, and while the water was heating, she stood at the basin. The two minutes fitted inside the kettle's two minutes. I still do it that way, fifty years later, and I do not have to decide anything.

So find your kettle. Find the one small thing you already do at the same time every evening, and put the ritual inside it. Fold the cloth and leave it by the basin, so your hands find it without thinking. That is all. A cloth, folded, in the same place.

And if you want to remember all of this, press the like button, and subscribe. It is a small thing for you, and it helps this little room reach someone else.

The last thing, and then I will let you go. The edges.

Long nails and rough edges catch. In the shop, I once watched a woman's nail pull a single thread from the sleeve of a silk blouse. She did not notice at first, and when she did, she put the blouse down and said nothing. My mother's rule was simple. Keep them short, and file in one direction, never back and forth. Back and forth makes the edge ragged; one direction makes it smooth.

So keep a small file where you keep your gloves, and use it once a week, for twenty seconds per hand. One direction, from the side toward the tip. That is the whole housekeeping of it. Twenty seconds, once a week, and the cloth does the rest.

Now let me ask you something, and I would like you to answer me in the comments. What did your mother or your grandmother do with her hands at the end of the day? Was there a basin, a cloth, a little jar, a prayer, a radio program? And is there one small thing from her that you still do, without thinking, at seven o'clock?

I read those comments with my tea. I really do.

Next week, we will talk about the collar. Why a collar sits the way it does, and the one fold that changes how an entire coat hangs. Bring a coat, and sit by a good light.

Until then, small things, done every day. Thank you for coming in.
