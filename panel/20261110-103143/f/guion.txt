Forty-two years on that stool. Picture it. The same wooden stool, worn smooth in the middle. Behind me, four shoe boxes on the shelf, each with a card in front in my handwriting, because I never trusted a label I didn't write myself. By the end, those four cards are the only four kinds of shoe I'd tell you to buy after sixty-five. Not four brands. I don't do brands. Four kinds of shoe, and what to look at in each one so you don't get taken.

My father opened this store in nineteen fifty-one. One window. One bench. One brass bell on the door, because he said a customer should hear that they arrived. I was twelve when he handed me my first shoe and said, Sal, bend it. So I bent it. Folded right in half, like a taco. He says, would you walk to church on that? I said, no. He said, good. Now you know more than half my customers.

For forty-two years I've been on the customer side of that bench, and I've fitted feet on men and women from seventeen to ninety-four. I've seen feet measured wrong at nine in the morning in socks the thickness of a coin. And I've seen the look on a face when the right shoe goes on for the first time. That look. That's why I still unlock the door.

Here's the part nobody tells you. Your feet change after sixty-five. The arch can flatten a little. The toes can spread. The size you wore at fifty is not always the size you are at seventy. I have an index card in that file box back there. Mr. Rossi, and that's not his real name, but the card is real. It says nine D. Underneath, in my own hand, nine E, wide, high instep. Same man. Eleven years between those two lines. His feet didn't grow. They spread. When feet spread, a shoe that fits you in width keeps you standing up straight.

Now the four. And I'm not going to sell you anything. I want to hand you a pair of eyes, so the next time you're in a store, you see what I see when I'm on that stool.

[emphatically] Number one. A shoe with a removable insole. Take the insole out and look underneath. A good shoe has a solid board in there. A bad shoe has a soft cardboard pad that tells you the whole bottom is cardboard and foam. If you use a doctor's insole, that shoe has to have a removable one so you can put yours in. I had a woman, Mrs. Bellini, in the winter of two thousand eleven, came in with shoes two sizes too big. I took the thick felt insole out, put a firm one in, and she said, Sal, I can feel the floor again. She didn't get a new foot. She got a shoe where she could feel the floor.

Number two. A wide toe box. Not a wide shoe, a wide toe box. A wide shoe can still pinch the toes if it's cut close in front. Stand in the shoe with your weight on it and press your thumb down in front of your big toe. You should feel a little room, about the width of your thumb. If your toes touch the end, that shoe is too short, no matter what the box says. By the time I was six, my father had a picture of me doing it. A little boy, sitting on that stool, thumb in a shoe, like it was a piano.

Number three. A low, stable heel. Not no heel, but low, wide at the bottom, and seated flat on the ground. My father called a shoe with a high heel a chair with a nervous foot. I had a fella, Mr. Duffy, came in the spring of twenty-seventeen with walking shoes that looked carved from a bar of soap. High heel, soft in the middle. He said his balance was fine. Three months later he came back with a cane. I'm not saying it was the shoes. I'm saying the shoes weren't helping him.

Number four. A firm heel counter. That's the back of the shoe, the part that holds your heel. Grip it with two fingers and press. It should be stiff, like a good book spine. If it collapses under your thumb, like a sock with a dream, your heel slides around all day, and a sliding heel means a tired foot. That's my rhyme, by the way. The only one I've got.

Now the three traps. Because you'll walk into a store and this is what you'll see on the shelf.

The first trap is a soft sole. Pick up the shoe and bend it in half. If it folds like a paper towel, put it back. A shoe should bend at the ball, in the front third, where your foot bends. Not in the middle. A shoe that folds in the middle gives your arch nothing to push against. On the shelf it feels like a slipper. Ten blocks later your feet are telling you the shoe wasn't doing any work. So bend it, then twist it. Twist it like you're wringing a towel. If it twists easy, all the way, it's got no spine. Go for a firmer one, and your arch will thank you on the way home.

The second trap is the open heel, the high heel, whatever they're calling it this year. On carpet, it's fine. On a sidewalk with a crack in it, it's not fine. If you've had a fall, ask your doctor before you do anything I say, and think twice about that heel.

The third trap is the wrong size. This is the big one. The size on the box is a suggestion, not a law. It was decided by a company in an office, for an average foot they have never met. Take the shoe off the wall, sit down, put it on the way I'd put it on you, with the shoehorn, and stand up. Walk five steps. Then do it again with a half size down and a half size up. Seven out of ten times, the one you first picked isn't the one you buy.

Now. I'd like you to do something today. Get a piece of paper, bigger than your foot, put it on the floor against a wall, and stand on it with your heel against the wall. Mark the end of your longest toe. Take a ruler and measure. That's your length. Then measure across the widest part, the ball. That's your width. Write it on a card and put it in your wallet. And do it at four o'clock in the afternoon, not at nine in the morning. Your feet are bigger at four o'clock than at nine. Buy the shoe at four, for the afternoon foot, the one that walks home on the sidewalk. I have two marks on the wall by the door from my own feet, and they're a half size apart, and they're both me.

And now the part I owe you. A shoe is a tool. It has a job. Walking, church on a Sunday, padding around the house, getting through a winter. Those are four different jobs, and a shoe that's good at all four isn't very good at any of them.

The walking shoe. Removable insole if you use one, a toe box you can spread in, a low heel, a firm back. Then look at the outsole, the rubber on the bottom. In a place with rain, you want a pattern cut into it. My father called a smooth sole a skating rink with a shoe on it. A walking shoe should feel like a hand under the arch, not a cloud. Clouds don't stay clouds for long.

The dress shoe. The church shoe, the wedding shoe, the one for the funeral of a friend. Most people buy these too small. The dress shoe should be a half size bigger than your walking shoe, because there's less give in the leather. Pick a wide toe box even in a dress shoe, because that's exactly where they cut the most corners. My father would shine his shoes on a Saturday night and say, Sal, a man who doesn't shine his shoes doesn't trust the morning.

The house shoe. This one has hurt more people than any other. The sole is a piece of fabric, or a soft leather skin, so your foot is walking on the floor and the slipper is just along for the ride. I've had people sit on this stool and tell me they can't stand on the bathroom tile in the morning. The first question I ask isn't about the tile. It's about the slipper. A good house shoe is a shoe with a sole, low, wide, and a back to it.

The winter shoe. This is the one that keeps me up. Not the cold. The ground. In a winter shoe, the sole has to hold onto ice, not just wet pavement. Look at the tread first, a deep pattern. Winter shoes are often cut short because the maker adds insulation and eats the length. So take the winter shoe a half size larger, and try it on with the thick sock you'll wear in the winter. Mr. Coyle, his first winter after his wife passed, came in alone and bought the wrong size, and I fitted him three times that afternoon. He said, thank you Sal, I feel like I can walk to her grave now.

Take those four jobs to any store, and do one thing. Sit down. Take your time. Put the shoe on. Walk. Take it off. Put on the next. Your foot is not a child. It's not going to learn. It's going to ask the same thing every day, and you're the one who answers.

If you've stayed with me this far, I've got one more thing. Forty-two years is a long time on one stool. I've fitted feet with a boy on my knee and I've fitted those boys' sons. I've done it all with the same three things. A metal shoehorn, a little notebook in my pocket, and my thumb, right where the toe meets the shoe, to feel for room. The thumb doesn't lie. The box lies all the time. Your thumb is the truth. Bend the shoe. (laughs) Go on, bend it. Your thumb is better than the label. Older shoes need care, and care is cheaper than a new pair. A leather shoe wants polish and a brush and ten minutes on a Saturday. Stuff the toe with newspaper for the season and keep them off the radiator. Let them breathe. My father kept a brush by the register like other men keep a pen.

Now, if you liked sitting here with me, hit that like button. I don't have a store to send you to, and this thing is my bench now. If I say something worth keeping, I want it to find the next person who needs it. And subscribe, because every week I'll have another one. The next one's about the shelf, and how to keep a pair alive a winter longer than it wants to go.

So here's my question for you. Tell me below which one you'll check first. The four kinds, or the traps. I'd check the heel counter first, myself. Press it with two fingers and tell me what happened. And then measure your feet at four in the afternoon, and tell me what the ruler says. My father used to say, every man walks on his own two feet, but nobody ever asks them how they feel. I'm asking now. How do they feel? Thank you for sitting with me on that stool.
