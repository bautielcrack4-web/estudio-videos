Thirty-four years I sat beside dying people in this city. And the hardest week of my whole life was not in anybody else's house. It was in my mother's bedroom, in March of 2015, five blocks from Woodlawn Lake. And I was not ready. Not with all those years behind me. Not even close.

Here's what nobody tells you, honey. When it's your own mother, the years don't help you. They only let you see, in slow motion, every single thing you are about to do wrong.

So I made a list. On the back of a receipt from the H-E-B on Culebra, sitting in my car with the engine off, because I couldn't walk through that door yet. What I would never let happen to my own mother in her last week. Eight things. And tonight I'm going to say them out loud, because I think some of you are sitting in a car right now too.

My name is Rosa Navarro. I have no medical training at all. None. I was the chaplain on the hospice team for thirty-four years, most of it at Alamo Home Hospice, in people's houses all over this city. So anything about her body, anything at all, you call your hospice team. Not me. What I know is the other part. The house. The family. The things that get said, and the things that never get said.

If you saw the one I did about the seven things families say at the bedside, this is the other side of it. That one was about words. This one is about the week.

And I'll be plain with you. I'm not telling you what a good daughter does. I'm telling you what I did, and what I saw in almost three thousand houses, and what I would do again.

But before the list, I want you to do something with me. Not later. Right now.

Think of the last ordinary Tuesday.

Not the last week. Not the last day. The last Tuesday when nothing was happening. When she was in her chair, and the TV was on, and somebody was in the kitchen making noise with the dishes.

Who was in the room?

Say it in your head. Not who came to visit in the final week. Who was in the room on an ordinary Tuesday.

Because that's the whole video. Everything I'm going to tell you tonight is about that room, on that Tuesday, and what the last week does to it.

Most families, when I ask that question, go quiet. And then they say something like, I was at work. Or, my sister was there, I think. Honey, that's not a confession. That's just a life. But the last week is built out of ordinary Tuesdays. And if you want to change the last week, you start with a Tuesday.

Number one. I would never let my mother's room fill up with people who came to see themselves.

Mr. Delgado was a veteran of the Korean War, from over by Kelly Field. I sat with him in 2003, in a little house on Guadalupe Street, and by his last week that living room had more people in it than a wedding. Cousins he hadn't seen in twenty years. A neighbor who brought a cake and stayed four hours. A man in a nice shirt who stood in the doorway and told everybody about his own father's passing for forty minutes.

And Mr. Delgado, who had been talking to his brother Ramiro for two days — his brother who died in 1951 — turned to me and asked, very quiet, in Spanish: Rosa, ¿quiénes son todas estas personas? Who are all these people?

[whispering] His own cousins. In his own living room.

That's what a full house does. From the doorway it looks like love. From the bed it feels like a crowd. And I want to say this gently, because I've been on both sides of that door. Most of us visit so that we can be seen visiting. We stand where the family can see us. We bring the flowers where the flowers will be noticed. And the person in the bed gets the leftovers of the afternoon.

Why does it happen? Because the silence scares us. Sitting in a quiet room with somebody who is leaving is the hardest thing a human being does. Talking is easier. Filling the room is easier. Being busy is easier.

So here's what I did. One chair. Not five. One chair beside the bed, and it stays there, and whoever sits in it does not get up for anybody. In my mother's room I put a folding chair from the hardware store on Zarzamora. Thirty dollars, the metal kind with the vinyl seat. And I told my brother: this chair is the whole job. Whoever is in the chair, that is who she is with.

And at the door I put an eighteen-dollar wireless doorbell. The kind with a button and a little plug-in chime. I put the button outside her door and the chime in the kitchen, turned down low. Why? Because when somebody walks in, the person in the chair doesn't have to jump up and play hostess. The chime tells the kitchen, somebody's here, come and get them. It sounds like nothing. It changed that house.

Tonight, go and stand in her room and count the chairs. If there are five chairs around the bed and nobody is sitting in any of them, honey, you don't have a room. You have a lobby.

Number two. I would never let my mother be alone at two in the morning.

Señora Inés Garza had dementia. I visited her for eleven months, out on Bandera Road, and her daughter Elena did everything. Baths, food, the laundry, the appointments, the whole house. And she did it alone, because she was the only one left in San Antonio.

One night, around nine, Elena ran out to the H-E-B to buy coffee. She was gone thirty-five minutes. She came back and her mother had died, by herself, in that room, in the dark.

I got there twenty minutes later. Elena was sitting on the front step with the bag of coffee still in her hand, and she looked at me and said: I left her alone to buy coffee.

That sentence stayed with me for years. Because it wasn't true, what she said. She didn't leave her mother alone. We did. All of us. The family, the neighbors, me, the whole world that tells a daughter that the loving thing is to do it all yourself, night after night, until you drop.

Here's what I know after thirty-four years. Death does not keep an appointment. It doesn't wait for the shift change or for the daughter to get back from the store. And the families who did this well — and some did it beautifully — they all had the same thing. They had a night list.

A night list is three names and three phone numbers on a piece of paper, taped to the refrigerator, with one rule. From two in the morning until six, nobody is alone in that room. It doesn't have to be family. It can be a neighbor, a woman from the church, a cousin's husband who doesn't talk much. That's fine. He doesn't need to talk. He needs to sit.

For my mother, it was Marisol on Tuesdays, my niece on Thursdays, and Ernie — my husband, Ernie — on the weekend nights, in the recliner, with the radio on so low you could barely hear it. Three names. That's all.

The cost is almost nothing. A baby monitor, the old kind, no wifi, no camera on the internet, forty dollars at the store on Culebra. A magnetic whiteboard for the refrigerator, six dollars, and I wrote the three names on it in marker. That's it. That's the whole setup.

And here's the part nobody wants to hear. Asking for help is not a failure. Sitting in that room alone at two in the morning, night after night, until you are so tired you can't see straight — that's not devotion. That's how families break. And a broken daughter is no good to anybody in that bed.

Tonight, before you go to sleep, answer one question. Who is on your two a.m. list? If the answer is me, that's not a plan, mija. That's a countdown.

Number three. I would never let the papers be a mystery in that house.

Walt Pruitt was a rancher from out in Bandera. Big hands, a truck that needed paint, and a daughter in Houston he hadn't talked to in a while. In 2011 he had one of those days — you'll hear me talk about those days in another video — where he sat up, clear as morning, asked for huevos rancheros, and called his daughter in Houston and talked to her for an hour.

His family had been paying an aide out of their own pocket for five months. Five months. Bathing, dressing, the bed, the supplies, the pads, all of it, cash every week, because they thought that's what it cost. Nobody had told them that the hospice benefit under Medicare Part A already covers the aide for the bath, the social worker, the chaplain, the hospital bed, the wheelchair, the oxygen, the supplies, and the medications for the terminal diagnosis, with a copay of no more than five dollars per prescription.

Nobody told them about respite, either. Up to five days in a facility so the family can sleep, with a five percent copay. Nobody told them about continuous care at home during a crisis. Nobody told them about the bereavement support for the family, up to thirteen months after. Nobody told them they could change agencies once during a period. And nobody told them about the periods themselves: two of ninety days, and after that sixty days at a time, with no limit.

I've sat at kitchen tables where a family spent three thousand dollars, five thousand, eight thousand dollars of their own money on things that were already paid for. Not because they were careless. Because in this country we hand people a benefit and we don't hand them the words.

So here's what I would do, and what I did. One binder. A plain three-ring binder from the office store, nine dollars, and on the front I wrote the name of the agency and the night number in big letters with a marker. Inside: the name of the hospice team, the social worker, the chaplain, the aide and her schedule, the equipment that had been delivered, and one page called What We Already Paid For, where I wrote down every single thing the family had bought with their own money. Every week. Because that page is where the money comes back.

Why does it work? Because grief makes people forget, and paper does not forget. The daughter who is exhausted at eleven at night cannot remember what she was told at the kitchen table on a Tuesday in March. But the binder can.

Tonight, go and open the drawer where the papers are. If you don't know what's in there, honey, neither does the person who will need it at three in the morning. And ask your hospice team this exact question: what does the benefit already pay for, and what are we paying for ourselves? Write the answer down in the binder. Not in your phone. In the binder, where the whole family can see it.

Number four. I would never let my family fight in the hallway.

I was in a house in Leon Valley in 2008 with three brothers and a mother in the back bedroom. The mother was in her last days. And those three men spent two afternoons in the driveway arguing about a truck. A 1978 truck. Who was going to get it, who had paid for the tires, who had driven it to Laredo in 1994.

And one afternoon I came out of the bedroom and the mother had heard all of it through the wall. She asked me, very quiet: Rosa, are they fighting about the truck?

She was leaving in a few days. And the thing she was listening to was a truck.

I want to tell you why this happens, because it isn't about the truck. It never is. Fear comes out of people looking for something to hold on to, and money is the easiest thing to hold on to. The will, the house, the truck, the jewelry, the plot at the cemetery. When a family is terrified, they start counting things. It's the only language some people have for love.

But the person in that bed hears everything. Everything. The tone in the driveway, the sigh in the kitchen, the phone call in the garage. They may not know what day it is, but they know when their children are at war. And I have watched people hold on longer than they wanted to, because they were waiting for the house to be quiet.

So here's the rule I would put in my mother's house. One voice. One person talks to the hospice team, so the team isn't getting four different stories. And one whiteboard on the refrigerator, twelve dollars, with three things written on it: who is sleeping there tonight, what time the aide comes, and the night number in big numbers. Everything else — the truck, the will, the money, who did more — happens in the driveway, out of earshot, with the door closed.

And if there has to be a family meeting, it happens in the kitchen, before the person wakes up, with a piece of paper and a pen, and one question: what would she want? Not what do we want. What would she want.

Tonight, ask yourself: who is the one voice? And if two people in your family answer at the same time, honey, you just found your first fight. Fix it now, while she can still hear you be kind.

Number five. I would never send the grandchildren out of the room.

Mrs. Ophelia Washington sang in the choir at a Baptist church over on the East Side. Sixty-something years in that choir. And in her last week her granddaughter — nine years old, missing two front teeth — stood at the foot of the bed and sang Amazing Grace. Off key. Every verse a little bit in the wrong place.

And Mrs. Washington, who had not opened her eyes in two days, moved her fingers. Not much. Just her fingers, on the sheet, in time.

Now I want you to notice what the family almost did. The mother of that little girl had her hand on the child's shoulder, ready to pull her back. Because we do that. We send the children to another room. We tell them, go watch TV, honey, the grown-ups are talking. We think we are protecting them.

We are not protecting them. We are teaching them that this — the bed, the dying, the last week — is a thing that happens behind a closed door, and that they will find out later, from somebody else, in a hallway, at a funeral.

Children understand much more than we give them credit for, and they need the goodbye as much as we do. What they don't need is to be left with a hole where a goodbye should have been. I have met adults in their fifties who still carry that closed door.

So this is what I would do. Five minutes. Not the whole afternoon. Five minutes, with a job. Bring the water. Hold the hand. Draw a picture and tape it on the wall where she can see it. That's the job. It gives the child something to do with their fear, and it gives the person in the bed the one thing that still makes them smile.

The cost is three dollars for crayons and four dollars for tape. Four dollars and a roll of tape, and a child gets to remember that she sang to her grandmother instead of remembering that she was sent to the other room.

Tonight, think about this week. Was a child sent out of the room? Go and ask that child, quietly, away from everybody: what did you want to say to her? And then find the five minutes.

Number six. I would never let my mother think she was a burden.

This is the one that I got wrong.

My mother was Doña Refugio Ibarra. Cuca, everybody called her. She raised us in Prospect Hill, a few blocks from Our Lady of Guadalupe, and she was a woman who could make a meal for eleven people out of nothing and then wash every plate before anybody else stood up.

In March of 2015 she was in her own bed in her own house, and I was the one on the chair. And she started apologizing. For the mess in the room. For the smell. For the money. For the nights. She apologized so much that I stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing the fan in the summer.

One night, around three in the morning, she took my hand and said, in Spanish: mija, perdóname por todo esto. Forgive me for all of this. For the trouble.

Thirty-four years. Almost three thousand houses. I had said every kind word to every stranger in this city. And my own mother was apologizing to me for dying in her own bed.

I sat there with my mouth open. And what I said was: [whispering] Mamá, you are not a problem. You have never been a problem. You are my mother, and I would do this for a hundred years.

She didn't answer. She patted my hand, twice, the way she did when I was a girl and I had done something foolish. But I saw her shoulders come down. I saw it. That's the only thing I'll take with me from that week, honey. Her shoulders coming down.

Why do they apologize? Because they think they have become a cost. And where do they get that idea? From us. From our faces. From the sigh when she asks for water for the third time. From the phone we look at while she is talking. From the way we say, no, it's fine, when it is clearly not fine.

So here is the sentence, and here is the rest of it. The sentence is: you are not a problem, you are my mother. The rest of it is that the sentence only works if your face agrees with it. So take the sigh out of the room. Take the money talk out of the room. Put the phone in the kitchen, face down, and don't bring it back in. If you're angry at your brother, be angry at your brother in the driveway. Not in front of her.

And when she says she's sorry, don't say, it's okay. Look at her. Take her hand. And tell her what she is.

Tonight, watch yourself. Not her. Watch what your face does when she asks for something for the third time. She is watching your face, mija. She has been watching it her whole life.

Number seven. I would never move her out of her own room unless she asked me to.

Mr. Herman Vogel was my first family, in 1991, out in New Braunfels. A retired man, German, a house that smelled like coffee and wood. His family had done what so many families do. They brought in the hospital bed and they set it up in the living room, facing the television, because that's where there was space.

And one afternoon he asked me to turn him toward the window. He couldn't do it himself. He wanted to look at the birds at the feeder his wife had put out years before.

I turned the bed. It took two of us, and it took about four minutes. And he looked out that window for the rest of the afternoon and didn't say a word. That was my first lesson in this work, and it came from a man who was leaving, and it was this: the bed goes where the life is.

By the last week, most houses have turned into a small hospital. The pictures come down because there's no room. The curtains get closed. The furniture goes against the wall. The room starts to look like a place where something is happening to a person, instead of a place where a person lives.

And I understand why. There's equipment. There are people coming and going. It's easier to clean. But honey, she is not going to remember the equipment. She is going to remember the quilt, and the light, and whose picture is on the wall.

So here's what I did in my mother's room, and what I would do in yours. The bed goes by the window, so she can see the lemon tree in the patio. Her own quilt goes on top of the hospice blanket — not instead of, on top of, because the blanket is there for a reason and the quilt is there for her. The pictures stay up, all of them, even the ones that are crooked. The little radio goes on the nightstand, on the station she likes, low. And if the room is dark, a lamp, the cheap kind from the store, fifteen dollars, on the table, so the light comes from the side and not from the ceiling.

Why does this matter? I'll tell you exactly why. Because a room that looks like a hospital tells a person that she is a case. And a room that looks like her room tells her that she is still home. And people who are still home — I have seen this, over and over — they talk more. They eat a little. They let you sit with them.

Tonight, go and lie down on that bed, on your back, and look at what she sees. If the answer is a wall, or the back of a television, fix it this week. It costs less than a pizza, and it is the cheapest thing on this whole list.
