[whispering] Last Sunday Carol sent me a photo from Sacramento. She is seventy. In the photo she is wearing a mustard scarf she bought for Kyoto, and her face is grey.

The scarf was beautiful. Her face was grey. Not tired. Grey. The yellow was eating the color of her skin, and she could not see it, because she was looking at the scarf.

I wrote back two words. Not that one.

Here is what took me thirty years to learn. The color nearest your face is not decoration. It is light. It is the lamp you carry into a room. My mother put eight color pairs next to the faces of brides and geiko in Gion for forty years, and she never began with black.

Not because her women were thinner. Not because they were richer. Because of what black does under the chin at three in the afternoon. A color that is wrong at the throat does not make you look heavier. It adds years to your face, and you cannot see it in your own bathroom mirror.

Eight pairs. Each one has a color at the face and a color below. The last pair is the one nobody in America knows, and it is made from a piece of silk that costs about six dollars.

By tonight you will know exactly what to change, with what is already in your closet.

For forty years my mother, Fumiko Arai, was a kitsuke-shi in Gion. She dressed brides and geiko by hand, in a room with one window and a heater she never turned on. In that room the collar was everything. The eri is the only part of a kimono that touches the face; all the rest is below. She would hold a length of silk under a woman's chin, step back, squint, and say one word. No. Or yes.

I lived nineteen years in California, in Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002. I learned to wear black there. Black to the office, black to dinner, black because it is safe and it hides the coffee stain and the extra pound. When I came home I brought a suitcase of it. My mother opened the suitcase and said nothing. She took one black blouse, held it under my chin at the window, and waited.

I am not going to tell you that you are doing it wrong. I wore black for nineteen years. I am going to tell you what I saw in that window.

There is a video that tells you black is slenderizing and every woman should own a black column. My mother would have said the opposite, and here is her reason. A column of black under a face at sixty is a shadow with a woman inside it.

Before the pairs, do one test with me. Three minutes. You need a window, not a lamp.

Go to the window at midday. Take the black top you wear when you want to look serious. Hold it under your chin, against the skin, and look at the shadow under your jaw. Not your eyes. The jaw. Count to three.

Now do the same with a navy shirt, then with an ivory one. Squint a little, the way you squint at a photograph.

Most women see what I saw. With the black, the shadow under the jaw gets deeper, and the line from the nose to the mouth sharpens. With the ivory, the shadow softens. The black is not making you slim. It is lighting you from below.

Why does this change after sixty? Because skin loses color as the years pass. Not only the wrinkles. The color. The pink goes first, then the warmth. At sixty-eight my skin is cooler than it was at forty. A heavy, cool color next to a cool face leaves nothing to bounce back. Black takes the light. Ivory returns it.

That is the whole idea, and it is why my mother worked in pairs. One color at the face, one below. Eight pairs now. You can do every one of them without leaving the house.

Pair one. Ivory at the face, navy below.

The problem is not black itself. It is black at the throat. In 1998, in Sunnyvale, I had a black turtleneck that I wore to every dinner. I felt elegant in it. In every photograph from that year my face looks like it is floating in a dark room. My mother saw one of those photographs in 2002 and asked me if I had been ill. I had not been ill. I had been wearing black.

The neck and the jaw are where the eye lands first. A dark band under the chin draws a line under the face, and a line under the face reads as a shadow, and a shadow reads as tired.

The change is simple. An ivory blouse at the throat, navy trousers or a navy skirt below. Not white. White is too bright, and it turns blue under most ceilings. Ivory, the color of plain paper. Mine came from a linen stall at Nishiki market for eighteen dollars.

Tonight, put the ivory at your throat and the navy below, stand at the window, and look at the shadow under your jaw. If it is softer than it was with the black, that is your pair.

Pair two. Pale blue at the face, cognac brown below.

Mrs. Kinoshita is eighty-one. She lives across the street, and every morning she walks the Philosopher's Path, ten thousand steps, in the same brown leather shoes she has worn for fifteen years. In March, when the cherry buds were still closed, I met her at the top of the path. She was wearing a pale blue cotton shirt she has had since her husband was alive, and a brown belt. Her face looked rested. She told me she had not slept well.

Pale blue does something no other cool color does. It keeps a little warmth inside it. It bounces light without turning the skin grey, and the brown underneath gives the blue something to sit on.

After sixty the whites of the eyes go slightly yellow and the skin goes slightly cool. Pale blue meets both of them halfway.

The change: a pale blue shirt, or a twelve-dollar cotton scarf if a shirt feels like too much, and anything cognac below. You already own the brown.

Tonight, hold the blue under your chin, put the brown bag on the table beside you, and look at the two together, not separately.

Pair three. Apricot at the face, taupe below.

Sachiko-san owns the dry cleaner on the corner. She can smell a garment and tell you how many days you wore it, which is why Hiroshi hides his sweaters from her. In 2019, in February, she was behind the counter in an apricot cardigan with a coffee stain on the cuff. The winter had been long. She said, this is the color I wear when the winter has been long, and she was right. That cardigan was the only warm thing in the shop.

Apricot puts back the pink that the years take away. It is the color of the inside of a peach, not the skin. If it is too orange it becomes a costume, so look for the soft one, almost pink with a little yellow.

Below, taupe. Taupe is grey with warmth in it, and it keeps the apricot from looking sweet.

The change: an apricot cotton top, about sixteen dollars, and the taupe trousers you already wear with everything.

Tonight, hold the apricot under your chin in the kitchen light at nine o'clock, which is the worst light in the house. If the apricot survives the kitchen, it will survive anything.

Pair four. Butter yellow at the face, denim blue below.

My granddaughter Yui is twenty-two and studies at Doshisha. In 2023 she came home with a butter-yellow sweater and put it on me, over my blouse, and said, Grandma, this is your color, not mine. She was half right. Yellow on my shoulders is too much yellow. Yellow at my throat, in a narrow scarf, is exactly enough.

Butter is the safe yellow. Not lemon, not gold. The pale one, the color of the butter you put on bread in the morning. It brings out the silver in grey hair, and it puts a little light under the chin.

Below, denim blue. Blue jeans, or a denim shirt if you prefer, because denim is the one blue that never looks like a uniform.

The change: a strip of cotton from Mr. Okada's stall at Nishiki, nine dollars, hemmed by hand into a narrow scarf. He keeps the remnants for me in a paper bag under the table.

Tonight, put the scarf on, then take it off, and look at your eyes, not your skin. If the eyes look brighter with the scarf, keep it.

Pair five. Sage green at the face, cream below.

Hiroshi has three shirts. He is very happy. In 2021 I bought him a sage-green cardigan for his birthday, and he wore it once, looked in the mirror, and said, it is the same as the grey one. It was not the same as the grey one. He put it back in the drawer and forgot it. I took it, and now it is mine, and he has never noticed.

Sage is a green with grey in it. It does not compete with the face. It quiets the redness around the nose and the cheeks, which is the thing most of us have after sixty and hate the most in photographs.

Cream below keeps it soft. A cream knit, a cream skirt, the cream trousers you wear in summer.

The change costs nothing if your husband has a green cardigan he does not wear. If he does not, a sage linen shirt at the market is about fifteen dollars.

Tonight, hold the sage at your chin and look at your cheeks, not your jaw. If the redness calms, it is yours. Ask your husband first. He will say he never liked it.

Pair six. Dusty rose at the face, warm grey below.

In 2016 Carol sent me a dusty rose blouse from a store in Sacramento, with a note that said, wear this and stop looking like a lawyer. I wore it to the wedding of Naomi's friend in Osaka, and three women asked me where I bought it. I told them California. It was the only time in my life that California was the fashionable answer.

Pink after sixty has to be dusty. The sweet pink of a little girl's room will make you look tired, because it is brighter than your skin and it wins. Dusty rose is quieter than you are. It lets your face be the brightest thing.

Warm grey below takes the sugar out of the pink. Not the grey of a school uniform. The grey with a little brown in it.

The change: a dusty rose blouse, about twenty dollars, or the rose scarf in the back of the drawer you stopped wearing because it was too much with black.

Tonight, put the rose on and look at your mouth. If your lips look pale, the rose is too sweet. If they look pinker, it is right.

Pair seven. Teal at the face, sand below.

In August of 2019 I walked the Kamogawa with Naomi, my daughter, who lives in Osaka and visits less than I would like. At six in the evening the river turns a color that is not blue and not green. She pointed at it and said, Mama, that is your scarf. I had the scarf in my bag. I had bought it in Gion in the spring and never worn it, because I thought it was too much.

Teal flatters grey hair more than navy does, because it has warmth in it. It is also the one color that still looks alive at night, under a lamp, when every other color has gone to sleep.

Sand below keeps the teal from becoming a jewel.

The change: the teal scarf you already own in the back of the drawer, and the sand trousers you wear in summer.

Tonight, try the teal under the lamp in the hallway, at night. If it still looks alive, it is the pair for dinners.

Pair eight. Pale lilac at the face, plum below. This is the pair nobody in America knows.

In the tansu in my room there is a piece of silk the color of plum. It is a remnant of an obi my mother tied on a bride in Gion in 1975, the year before I met Hiroshi. She kept it because the bride cried on it, and she said the silk had seen something. I have kept it for fifty years in a drawer that smells of paulownia.

The rule is two purples. The lighter purple goes at the face, the deeper one goes below. Lilac at the throat lifts the chin and softens the line of the jaw, and the plum underneath gives it weight, so it does not look like a nightgown.

Purple is the color of the Kyoto winter, the murasaki of the Heian court, and it is the one color that makes grey hair look deliberate instead of accidental.

The change: a lilac scarf at the neck, about six dollars from a remnant table, and the plum cardigan or the plum skirt you already own. That is the whole pair. Six dollars, and a color that no American catalogue will ever suggest to you.

Tonight, this is the pair for the evening. Lilac at the neck, plum below, and stand in the hallway light with the door open.
