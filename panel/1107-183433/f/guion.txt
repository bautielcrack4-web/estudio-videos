The first thing: the closing of the casket.

In a Mexican wake the casket stays open. The family stays all night. In Analco the women bring coffee at eleven and tamales at two in the morning, and the men stand outside in the cold telling the same three stories until the sky gets light. Then comes the last look. Everybody in the room knows what it means without anybody saying it.

Then the funeral director asks the family to step into the next room. And in the five minutes that follow, things happen to the person you love that you are not invited to see.

For forty years I closed that lid. I put the key in and turned it. And before I did, I checked the inside of that casket with my hand. The pillow under the head, the hands crossed the way the family left them, a letter from a granddaughter folded into the lining. Anybody in my business who says they don't do that is lying, or they're new.

But here's the part the family never hears. The casket they sold you, the one with the word sealed in the brochure, is not sealed. It has a rubber gasket and a latch, and it will hold water out for a while, and then it will hold water in. That's what the manufacturers write in the small print. No state requires a sealed casket. No law requires a casket at all, except the one the cemetery makes for itself. And the difference between the same casket with the gasket and without it is about eight hundred dollars. Sometimes eighteen hundred.

In 2013 a widow came to me, Mrs. Salas, on Zarzamora. She'd already signed a contract at one of the big chains for a protective casket, because the man at the desk told her it would keep the water out forever. She paid twenty-two hundred dollars more than she had to, on a credit card in her own name, because her husband had never let her see the accounts.

I didn't sell her a casket. I showed her the price list, the General Price List the law makes every funeral home hand you the moment you ask in person. If a funeral home won't give it to you, you walk out and you call the state.

What can you change? You can buy the casket somewhere else. Federal law says the funeral home has to accept a casket you bought from a third party, and it cannot charge you a handling fee. In San Antonio there's a casket store twenty minutes from the West Side where the same box costs half. Saves you between eight hundred and two thousand dollars.

And the check for tonight. Write the name of the one person in your family who will be in the room when the lid closes. Not the whole family. One person. Because when the director says everybody step out, everybody steps out, and then nobody is there. That person should know the pillow is straight and the letter is still in the pocket. Ask for it. I never once said no.

The second thing: the ride.

In Mexico the family walks behind the casket. The carroza goes slow, the men take turns carrying, and the whole street knows what's happening. In the United States the family rides in a limousine behind the hearse, and the casket rides alone, in front, with two men nobody in the family has ever met.

Your mother is in the car in front of you, and the only people with her are the driver and a young man in a dark suit who was assigned to her that morning. He's twenty-four. He's careful. He's done this a hundred times. But he doesn't know her name, he doesn't know she sang in the choir at San Fernando, and he doesn't know she was afraid of the dark. He knows the route.

Between the funeral home and the grave there's a stop nobody explains. The hearse pulls into the cemetery office, and there's paperwork, and that's where the first cemetery charge appears, the one that wasn't in the funeral home's number. In many cemeteries it's called a weekend fee, a holiday fee, an after-hours fee. On a Saturday in San Antonio I've seen two hundred and fifty dollars for arriving after three o'clock.

In 2016 I buried Mr. Fuentes, a baker on Guadalupe Street for thirty-one years. The family was told the burial was at ten thirty. The priest was late, the church was full, and the cars reached the gate at eleven. The cemetery charged two hundred and fifty dollars for the reschedule, and the funeral home passed it through, because nobody had written down the time. A phone call.

So the change: the burial time is not the funeral home's time. It's the cemetery's time, and it has to be on paper from the cemetery, with the name of the person who took the call.

The check for tonight: call the cemetery, not the funeral home, and ask two questions. What time is the grave scheduled, and who's going to sign for it? If they can't give you a name, you don't have a time.

The third thing: the grave is not ready.

In the United States the funeral home and the cemetery are two different businesses. The funeral home sells you the casket, the service, the cars, the preparation. The cemetery sells you the ground. And the funeral home's quote, the one you signed at the kitchen table, usually doesn't include the ground. Not the opening of the grave. Not the closing. Not the vault.

A vault, or grave liner, is a concrete box that goes in the ground before the casket. Some cemeteries require it. It's not for your mother. It's so the ground doesn't sink and the mower doesn't fall in. That's the real reason, and nobody at the desk tells you that, because the box costs between nine hundred and fifteen hundred dollars, and the cemetery sells it. Opening and closing is another twelve hundred to eighteen hundred. Perpetual care, a few hundred more.

And here's what most families don't know. The funeral home is regulated by the federal government. The cemetery is not. The Funeral Rule covers the funeral home. It does not cover the cemetery. So the cemetery can quote one price on the phone and another at the gate, and no federal rule stops it.

In 2019 a family from Laredo buried their father at San Fernando Number Two. The funeral was nine thousand two hundred dollars. The day before the burial the cemetery called the daughter: the vault had not been ordered. Eleven hundred dollars, before the grave could be opened. She paid it on her phone, in the parking lot of a grocery store, with her kids in the car.

The change: before you sign anything at the funeral home, call the cemetery and ask for a total burial cost in writing. Ground, opening, closing, vault, perpetual care, marker installation. One page. If they won't send it, go to another cemetery, because there's always another cemetery. Saves between two thousand and thirty-five hundred dollars that were never in the number you agreed to.

The check for tonight: write the name of the cemetery, the phone number, and six words. Total burial cost in writing. That's the whole check.

Minute four. Go and get the price list. I'll wait. Pause this, go to the folder, come back.

This is the General Price List. Since 1984, federal law says any funeral provider has to hand you an itemized list of prices the moment you ask in person, and give you prices over the phone if you call. In 2024 the rule was updated, so if the funeral home has a website, the prices have to be on it. That's the Funeral Rule, the only piece of consumer protection most families never use, and it's free.

Find the line that says transfer of remains to funeral home. It's usually near the top. Circle it. Now look at the package you bought. Look for the words does not include. Every package has that list, and the transfer of remains is on it more often than not. So the family pays it once inside the package and once on the line, and nobody notices, because the package price is one number and the line is another number on a different page.

While you have it, circle three more lines. The basic services fee, which is the funeral director's time and the building, and which by law cannot be declined. The embalming, which federal law does not require, and most states don't either. And the casket, which you can buy somewhere else.

Mrs. Delgado deserves her own video. Eleven thousand dollars, three lines that shouldn't have been there, and when we crossed them out the funeral cost her five thousand four hundred dollars less. Same funeral. Same priest. Same casket, bought two streets over.

Three questions for the phone. Write them down. One: can you email me the General Price List today? Two: if I buy the casket from somebody else, is there any fee for that? Three: what exactly is in the basic services fee, line by line? If the person on the phone can't answer those three, ask for the manager. If the manager can't, you have your answer about that funeral home.

That's the test. Four minutes, and it's the most useful four minutes in this video.

The fourth thing: the lowering.

There's a machine at every grave in the United States. A frame with four straps, sitting over the hole, and the casket goes down a few inches at a time while the family stands on the grass. It's called a lowering device, and it's older than most of the people standing around it.

In Mexico, in Analco, when my father was alive, there was no machine. There were ropes. The men of the family held the ropes, four of them, sometimes six, and they lowered the casket by hand, and the whole street could see the ropes moving. It was the last thing the family did for that person with their own hands. It was hard. That was the point.

In the United States the cemetery staff does it. The family watches. And when the family turns around and walks back to the cars, the men with the shovels come in. They take the lowering device apart. They put the dirt back. In a lot of cemeteries the grave isn't even filled that day. It's filled the next morning by a crew that arrives at seven, before anybody visits.

[whispering] When my father died in 2003 I was in San Antonio. I flew in the next morning. The men had already filled the grave. My brothers were there the day before, and they told me it was fine, it was a good burial. But I didn't see the dirt go in. Forty years in this business, close to seven thousand burials, and I didn't see the dirt go into my own father's grave.

So the change isn't money. The change is a question: can we stay for the filling?

In Mexico the answer is yes. The sepulturero waits. There's a man in the Mezquitan, Don Chema, thin as a rope, a straw hat, a cigarette behind his ear that he never lights, and he doesn't put the first shovel of dirt in until the widow nods. That's his rule, and it's older than he is.

In the United States some cemeteries will let you stay, some won't, and the ones that won't will tell you it's for insurance, or safety, because the equipment is dangerous. Ask anyway. Ask before the burial, not at the grave. If the answer is yes, stay. It takes twenty minutes. It's the last thing you'll ever do for that person with your own body in that place.

The check for tonight: ask the question. Write it on the paper. Can we stay for the closing?

The fifth thing: the flowers.

A Mexican funeral without flowers is not a funeral. The coronas, the wreaths, the sprays, the cross of white roses, the bouquets the neighbors bring wrapped in newspaper because they cut them from the yard. In 2018 a florist on Guadalupe Street delivered four hundred dollars of flowers to a funeral and the family had ordered six hundred. The other two hundred stayed in the cooler, because the florist ran out of lilies and didn't call.

After the burial the cards are separated from the arrangements. The cards go into a box, or a drawer, or the trash, depending on the funeral home. The flowers go into the flower room, the good ones to a nursing home or a church, the rest into the dumpster behind the building, usually within twenty-four hours. The family thinks the flowers went to the grave. The family is at home, exhausted, and nobody tells them.

In Mexico the coronas go to the grave and stay, and by the end of the week the cemetery workers have collected them, because the cemetery has a schedule and the flowers aren't on it. The family comes back on Sunday, the grave is bare, and they think somebody stole the flowers. Nobody stole the flowers.

So the change: tell the florist in writing, half to the church, half to the grave the morning after. And tell the funeral home you want the cards, all of them. They're the list of everybody who came, and if nobody asks, they're gone. The flowers are already paid for. What the cards save you is two months of thank-you notes.

The check for tonight: pick the person who will collect the cards on the day of the burial. One person. Put the florist's number in that person's phone, with the words half to the grave.

The sixth thing: the bag on the shelf.

This is the one I think about when I can't sleep.

When a person dies, the family brings things to the funeral home. The glasses. The dentures. The watch. The wedding ring. The rosary from the first communion. The wallet, with the ID and the six dollars and the card from the granddaughter. Sometimes a letter, folded, that goes into the casket pocket.

The funeral home is supposed to inventory those things. In Texas there's a form, the personal effects form, and the family is supposed to sign it at the end of the arrangement. In practice, in a busy week, the things go into a plastic bag with a piece of masking tape on it, and the bag goes on a shelf in the back room. And it stays there until somebody asks.

Sometimes nobody asks. In 2012 a woman came into my office two years after her mother's funeral and asked if we had a ring. A thin gold band, a small diamond, worn on the right hand because her mother had been a widow for nineteen years. I went to the shelf. The bag was there, the masking tape had her mother's name in blue marker, and the ring was inside, in a little envelope that had never been opened. She cried for ten minutes, and then she put it on and said, I thought it was gone. It was on a shelf, in a bag, for two years, because nobody told her it existed.

And I have to tell you the other side, because it happens too. In 2016 a family in San Antonio couldn't find the watch. The bag was there. The watch wasn't. We called the cemetery. We called the crematory, because the brother had been cremated, and in a cremation the jewelry has to come off, and it goes into the bag, and sometimes the bag goes to the wrong shelf. We never found it. It was a thirty-year retirement watch. Nobody insures a watch that was already on a dead man's wrist.

So the change is a list, made before the wake, not after. Photograph every item on your phone. Name one person who takes the jewelry home before the wake. And if your mother wants to be buried with her ring, write that down and tell the director, because the person who takes the ring off at the crematory is not the person who took it off at the hospital.

What it costs: a photo and five minutes. What it saves: the ring.

The check for tonight: open the notes on your phone and write one line. Jewelry goes home before the wake. List taped inside the closet door.

The seventh thing: the bill that arrives after.

The funeral home takes your money the day of the arrangement, or the day of the burial, or sends a bill with thirty days. Then, three to six weeks later, the other bills come.

The death certificates. In Texas you pay for every certified copy, twenty to thirty dollars each, and most families need six or eight, for the bank, the insurance, the house, the car, the pension, the Social Security office. Nobody tells you at the arrangement that the copies are extra, because the funeral home orders them and passes the cost through, and it's a small line on a big bill. The obituary is about three hundred dollars with a photograph. The priest's stipend, the musicians, the hall for the reception, the food, all separate.

And the marker. The flat granite marker with the name and the dates is not included in the funeral. It's sold by the cemetery or by a monument company, it costs between twelve hundred and three thousand dollars, and in some cemeteries you buy it from them or pay an installation fee if you don't. Nobody mentions the marker at the arrangement, because the marker comes six months later, when the ground has settled and the family has stopped thinking about the funeral.

In 2021 the Ortiz family buried their father in San Antonio. The funeral was seventy-eight hundred dollars. The cemetery was twenty-six hundred. The certificates were two hundred and ten. The marker was nineteen hundred. The obituary was three hundred and forty. The total was twelve thousand three hundred and fifty dollars, and the family had agreed to seventy-eight hundred. The daughter showed me the folder at the kitchen table, every receipt, and she said, nobody told me about the marker. Nobody did.

So the change: ask for the total in writing, including the cemetery, the marker, the certificates and the obituary, before you sign. The funeral home has to give you an itemized statement of the goods and services you selected. That's the Funeral Rule too. Ask them to add one line at the bottom. Estimated total, including cemetery, marker, certificates and obituary. If they won't write it, write it yourself and make them initial it.

What it costs: one question. What it saves: four thousand dollars that arrive in the mail in October.

The check for tonight: if you have a folder from a funeral in the last two years, take out every receipt and add them up. Write the real number at the top. Most families have never done that, and the number is always bigger than the one they remember.

The eighth thing: the nine nights.

In Mexico, after the burial, the family prays for nine nights. The novenario. The house fills at eight, the rosary starts, the neighbors bring coffee and pan dulce, the altar with the photograph and the candle stays lit until the last night. In San Antonio it's the same, in a living room with plastic chairs and a statue of the Virgin on the television stand, the women in the kitchen, the men outside with the cars.

The funeral home was there for three days, and now it's done, and the family goes home.

Here's what nobody tells you about the nine nights. The first three the house is full. Forty people, fifty, the street parked solid. The sixth, the same five women who always come, the ones who lost somebody themselves and know the sixth night is the hard one. The ninth, the family, the priest, the coffee. And the tenth, nobody.

The tenth night is the one that gets people. The house is quiet. The dishes are done. The cousins have gone back to Dallas, the daughter has gone back to her job, and the person who lost their mother is sitting in a kitchen at nine o'clock at night with a refrigerator full of food nobody is eating and a phone that has stopped ringing. That's when it happens. Not at the burial. Not at the rosary. The tenth night.

Rosa will tell you the same thing after thirty-four years. The families she sits with don't need the casket. They need somebody on the tenth night, on the thirtieth day, on the first birthday, on the first Day of the Dead, when the whole neighborhood is putting out flowers and their person is the one on the altar.

So the change costs nothing and it's the most important thing in this video. Before the burial, name one person to be the caller. Not the person closest to the deceased, because that person can't do it. A cousin, a neighbor, a friend from church. That person makes three calls. The tenth night, the thirtieth day, the first birthday.

And if you're the one who was buried this year, make one call yourself. Call the person you've been meaning to call and say, I'm still here, and I'm not fine, and I wanted to hear your voice. That's not weakness. That's the work.

The check for tonight: open your phone and put three dates in it. The tenth night, the thirtieth day, the first birthday of somebody you know who buried a parent this year. You know who it is. You've been meaning to call them.

The ninth thing: ten years later.

And now the one I promised you. The envelope.

In most cemeteries in Mexico the ground is not sold. It's rented. A grave is a temporal, a temporary grave, for six years, or ten, or fifteen. You pay for the grave, you pay for the opening, and then every few years you pay again. It's called the refrendo. The renewal. And if nobody renews it, the cemetery waits, then sends a notice, then the remains are exhumed and moved to the common ossuary, and the grave is rented to another family.

In 1999 I spent the last Day of the Dead with my father. He was in his seventies, he walked with a cane, and he went to the cemetery in Analco in the morning to clean the grave of his own mother, and that night he sat in the office with a candle and did not sleep. He said the rosary at midnight. The mariachi from the corner, the one who plays for the families who pay, played Amor Eterno for somebody two streets over, and my father listened to the whole thing without moving.

At two in the morning he asked me a question. He said, mijo, do you know where the paper for this grave is?

I said yes. I didn't know. I was in my forties and I'd been in this business my whole life and I didn't know where the paper was.

At dawn he gave me a yellow envelope. Inside was the title of the grave, the receipt of the last refrendo, and a piece of paper in his handwriting. Enter on the right, next to the big pepper tree, the third grave.

My father died in 2003, while I was in San Antonio. The paper was in the envelope, the envelope was in the desk in the office, and my brothers knew where it was because he'd told them at the same kitchen table. The refrendo was paid. The grave is still there.

Most families don't have the envelope. The notice goes to the address on the title, and the address on the title is the house the family sold in 1998. The cemetery sends the notice to nobody. The grave expires. Ten years later somebody in the family goes to visit, and the grave has another name on it, and there's nothing to do, because nobody ever asked the question my father asked me at two in the morning.

So the change is this. If your family has a grave in Mexico, ask for the title. Photograph it. Ask the cemetery, in writing, when it expires and who is the holder on the title. Put the date in your calendar with an alarm six months before. And leave a copy with the youngest person in the family, not the oldest, because the oldest is the one who's going to need it.

What it costs: the refrendo, which in Guadalajara is less than one night of the wake. What it saves: the grave. The alternative, the ossuary, costs nothing and cannot be undone.

The check for tonight: call the cemetery and ask one question. When does this grave expire, and who is the holder on the title? If they can't answer, ask for the office and go in person, with the name of the person in the grave. Somebody has a book. Somebody always has a book.
