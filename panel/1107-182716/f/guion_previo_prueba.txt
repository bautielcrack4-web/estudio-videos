Last Tuesday a woman came into my office in San Antonio with a piece of paper folded into four, the way you fold a receipt you don't want to lose. She set it on the desk and she said, "Ernie, they gave me this at the door. What am I supposed to do with it?"

That paper has a name. It is called a General Price List, and in this country a funeral home is required by federal law to hand it to you in writing, in person, before anybody discusses a single arrangement with you, if you ask for it. The law is called the Funeral Rule. It has been on the books since 1984, and the Federal Trade Commission is the one that enforces it.

Most families fold it and put it in the purse and never open it again. That is the mistake I want to talk about today. Because that paper is not the bill. That paper is a menu. And nine of the lines on that menu are lines you are allowed to cross out with a pen.

My name is Ernie Navarro. I have been a funeral director for more than forty years. Twenty-five of those years I spent on the West Side of San Antonio, on Guadalupe Street, on Zarzamora, on Commerce, and I have buried close to seven thousand people. I have handed that paper across a desk thousands of times. I have also sat on the other side of that desk, in the chair where the family sits, and watched a widow agree to eleven thousand dollars for a funeral that should have cost five. The paper that would have saved her was already in her handbag. [emphatically] The paper was never the problem. Nobody reads the paper.

So today we read it together. Nine lines. What each one really costs. What to say at the counter. And around the fourth minute I am going to give you a ninety-second test you can do on the telephone, before you ever walk into that building.

But first let me tell you where the paper comes from, because it explains why some of those lines are printed the way they are.

My father, Refugio Navarro, opened a funeral home in Analco, in Guadalajara, in 1958. I was thirteen years old and my first job was sweeping the chapel. In those years there was no price list. My father wrote the price on the back of a calendar that hung by the door, in pencil, and if you asked him what a funeral cost he would sit down with you first and ask how many brothers and sisters the person had. The price came out of that conversation. Sometimes that was generous and sometimes it was not, and it depended entirely on who was sitting in front of him.

In 1985 I went to work full time beside him. Fifteen years of wakes in the front room of the house, coffee de olla at two in the morning, the Panteón de Mezquitán at six.

Then in 2000 I crossed to San Antonio and I learned the other way. And I will tell you something that maybe a funeral director is not supposed to say out loud: the paper is better. The paper is a good idea. A family should know what a casket costs before they are standing in a selection room at the worst hour of their life. The problem was never the paper. The problem is that the paper is written by the person who is selling to you, handed to you at the worst moment of your life, full of words nobody has ever explained.

So let me explain the words.

The Funeral Rule requires a price for sixteen specific things, itemized, one by one: the basic services of the funeral director and staff, the transfer of the body, embalming, other preparation of the body, the use of the facilities and staff for a viewing, for a funeral ceremony, for a memorial service, the equipment and staff for a graveside service, the hearse, the limousine, the casket, the outer burial container, forwarding remains to another funeral home, receiving remains from another funeral home, direct cremation, and immediate burial.

Sixteen lines. And two sentences in that law matter more than the other fourteen lines combined.

The first: they cannot refuse to sell you any of those items separately.

The second: if you ask for a price on the telephone, they have to give it to you.

Now, before we start crossing things out, I have to be straight with you about one line you cannot cross out. It is usually the first line on the page, and it says something like Basic Services of Funeral Director and Staff. That is what we call the non-declinable fee.

And I want to tell you why it exists, because you are going to hear a lot of people online tell you it is a scam, and that is not true. That fee pays for the building, the insurance, the license, the person who answers the phone at three in the morning, the paperwork with the county, the death certificates, the permits, the months of records that have to be kept after everybody has gone home. It is real work and it has a real cost. If a funeral home tells you that line is optional, be careful with that funeral home, because they are going to make it up somewhere else and you will not see where.

What you can do is check it. That fee should not include anything that is already priced on its own line further down. If the basic services fee covers the arrangement conference, and then there is a separate charge for the arrangement conference, that is double billing and you can point at it. In 2011 I saw a home in this city charging three hundred dollars for what they called a consultation fee on top of a basic services fee that already said, in its own description, that it included the arrangement conference. That is the sort of thing the Federal Trade Commission wants you to write to them about.

One line you cannot cross. Nine that you can. Let's go.

But before the nine, the test. Because a lot of you are listening to this and thinking, Ernie, I don't have a price list, nobody died, why do I need this today. Here is why. You can do this this week, on the telephone, in about ninety seconds, and you will know more about that funeral home than most families learn in three days.

Pick up the phone and call. Ask three questions, in this order.

One. "Can you read me the price for direct cremation, and the price for immediate burial, over the phone?" Under the Funeral Rule they have to give you those numbers. Not a range, not a package, the number.

Two. "If I buy a casket somewhere else, what is your handling fee, and where is it printed on your General Price List?"

Three. "Can you email me the General Price List before I come in?"

And then listen to how they answer. Not what they answer. How.

If the person on the phone says, "I'd rather you come in so we can explain it to you," that is not a crime, but it is a choice they are making, and you now know what they are choosing. If they say they don't have a price list, that is against the law, and you should hang up and call somebody else, because everything after that phone call will be worse.

Ninety seconds. Do it this week, before you need it. Write the three answers on the back of an envelope and put the envelope with your important papers.

Now the nine lines.

Number one. Embalming.

On the price list it is a line with a number next to it, usually somewhere between five hundred and twelve hundred dollars.

Here is what the law says. A funeral home may not tell you that embalming is required by law unless the law in that state, in that specific situation, actually requires it. In most states, for a direct cremation or an immediate burial, it is not required at all. What a funeral home can do is adopt its own policy: if you are having a public viewing with an open casket, many homes will require embalming as their condition of holding that viewing. That is allowed. But it is a policy, not a law, and they have to tell you it is a policy.

In 2011 a family on Zarzamora came to me after the fact. The mother had been cremated, no viewing, no service at the funeral home, and they had been told at the counter, "the law requires embalming." They paid eight hundred and ninety dollars for a preparation that nobody ever saw, on a body that went into a crematory four hours later.

What you say is not clever. What you say is: "We are not having a public viewing. Please take the embalming off."

Tonight's check: take the list, find the word embalming, and if there is no viewing planned, write NO in the margin next to it in pen.

Number two. The casket.

This one has a number that makes people gasp, and then it has a smaller number hidden somewhere that almost nobody reads: the casket handling fee.

You are allowed to buy a casket from somebody else. A casket company, a warehouse, an online seller, a woodworker in your own family. The funeral home cannot refuse to accept it. What they can do is charge you a fee for handling a casket they did not sell, and that fee has to be printed on the General Price List. In my experience it runs between one hundred and three hundred dollars.

In 2003 the Reyes family on Guadalupe Street bought the mother's casket in Laredo, the same model, the same gauge of steel, for eleven hundred dollars. The funeral home in San Antonio had quoted thirty-four hundred for it. The handling fee was one hundred and fifty. They saved two thousand one hundred and fifty dollars and the casket was the same casket.

Now, some funeral directors will tell you the outside casket is inferior, that it will not hold, that it will collapse in the vault. Ask them for that in writing. Ask them what the gauge is. Ask them what the warranty is. In forty years I have seen a casket come apart in a grave exactly once, and it was one we sold ourselves.

Tonight's check: before you shop anywhere, call and ask one question. "What is your casket handling fee, and where is it on your price list?" Write the number down.

Number three. The viewing you are not having.

On the list you will see a line for the use of facilities and staff for a viewing, and another for the use of facilities and staff for a funeral ceremony. Usually three hundred to six hundred dollars each.

These are rooms. If the rosary is at the church, and the funeral is at the church, and the reception is at your aunt's house, you are paying for three rooms you will never open the door to.

There is a version of this that is worse, and it is called the package. The package has one price, and the one price is always lower than if you add the lines up one at a time, because they want you to take the package. What the package does is hide which room you are paying for. Ask them to break it back down into the individual lines. If they can't, that tells you something.

In 2008 a family here held the rosary in their own front room, the way we do it back home, with the casket in the corner and the chairs borrowed from the parish hall. They were still charged for the viewing room. Four hundred and twenty-five dollars for a room that stayed dark and locked for three days.

Tonight's check: take a piece of paper and write down every room you will actually use. Then compare.

Number four. The limousine.

The list says something like, limousine, three hundred and fifty dollars for the first hour, one hundred and fifty for each additional hour.

That is not a bad price for a limousine. The problem is that nobody stops at one. I have watched families take three, because the cousins are here, because the godmother is here, because it looks right.

In 2016 a family in this city paid one thousand and fifty dollars for three vehicles for four hours. A local car service, the kind you would use for an airport, did the same job with two clean black SUVs for three hundred and eighty dollars.

If you do that, two things. Call the cemetery first and ask whether outside vehicles are allowed past the gate, because some are not. And make sure the driver is insured, in writing.

Tonight's check: count the people who truly have to ride together. Usually it is the immediate family and that is one car.

Number five. The stationery.

This line is often called the memorial package, or the stationery package, and it is two hundred to four hundred dollars. For that money you get one hundred prayer cards, a register book, a guest book, and twenty-five thank-you cards.

These are printed paper. That is all they are. The prayer card is a card with a picture on the front and a psalm on the back.

In 2019 a granddaughter in this family took the photograph to a copy shop on Bandera Road the night before the rosary. One hundred and fifty prayer cards, printed and cut, forty-two dollars. They were beautiful. Her grandmother's picture was on the front and she had chosen the psalm herself, in Spanish, the one her grandmother used to say at night.

There is a tenderness in doing that yourself that the package cannot sell you. And it costs a tenth.

Tonight's check: ask for the price of the package, and then ask for the price of each piece inside it, separately. If they will not break it down, buy your own.

Number six. The urn.

Now I am going to tell you about Mrs. Delgado, because she is the reason I still do this work.

2007, San Antonio. Mrs. Delgado was seventy-nine and her husband had died on a Thursday. She came in with a quote from a large funeral home on the other side of town. Eleven thousand and forty dollars. Cremation with a viewing, a rental casket, an urn, the prayer cards, the register book, and something called a memorial service package.

She had the money. That was not the issue. Her hands were shaking when she put the paper on my desk, and she said, "I don't understand what half of this is."

We went through it line by line. Three things came off.

First, the embalming and the rental casket. She did not want an open casket. Her husband had been sick for a long time and she wanted to remember him the way he was in the kitchen, not the way he was in the last month.

Second, the urn. Three hundred and ninety-five dollars for a bronze-colored urn from a catalogue. She had a wooden box at home, a plain one, that her husband had made for his tools in 1968, and she wanted him in that. I have never in forty years seen a family more sure of anything. The crematory has to accept a container you provide, and the funeral home cannot make you buy theirs. The only rule is that the container has to be rigid and it has to fit.

Third, the memorial service package. She held the service in her own church, with her own people, and the food was made by the women of the parish.

The total came to five thousand four hundred and twenty dollars. She paid less than half, and she buried her husband the way she wanted to, and the only thing she gave up was a bronze-colored urn she never wanted.

I am not telling you that story to make myself look good. I am telling you because Mrs. Delgado had the paper in her hand for four days before she walked into my office, and nobody in that first funeral home ever suggested she read it.

Tonight's check: look at the urn price, and then look around your own house.

Number seven. The after-hours charge.

On the list, usually under the transfer of remains, you will see the standard price, and then a higher price for nights, weekends and holidays. Somewhere between one hundred and fifty and three hundred dollars more.

This one I want to be careful with, because it is not dishonest. Somebody has to get out of bed at two in the morning, and that person deserves to be paid.

But it is negotiable in a way that most people never discover, because nobody ever asks. If the death happens at six in the evening on a Sunday and the family is willing to wait until seven the next morning, the transfer can happen at the standard rate. That is a conversation you are allowed to have. Nobody dies on a schedule, I know that. But if the person has died in a facility, and the facility has a cold room, and the family is exhausted, waiting until morning is not a sin.

In 2012 a family called me at twenty to seven on a Sunday night. They paid the higher rate, two hundred dollars more, and they found out two weeks later that the family in the next room had asked and had it waived.

Tonight's check: ask the question out loud on the phone. "If it happens at night, is that fee ever waived, and would you put that in writing?"

Number eight. The grave liner.

This is the line that makes people angry when they find out, and it is the line where I want you to be very careful with me, because I am going to tell you something that will save you money and I do not want you to misuse it.

The price list will say outer burial container, from twelve hundred and ninety-five dollars.

An outer burial container is a concrete box that goes in the ground and the casket goes inside it. There are two kinds. A grave liner has an open bottom. A vault is sealed. The cemetery usually requires one or the other, because the ground has to hold the weight of the equipment that drives over it.

Here is the part nobody tells you. That requirement comes from the cemetery, not from the law, and not from the funeral home. There is no state in this country where a vault is required by statute. And not every cemetery requires one. In 2017 I buried a man in Von Ormy where the cemetery asked for nothing at all, and the family saved thirteen hundred dollars they did not have.

So the question is not for the funeral director. The question is for the cemetery: "Do you require an outer burial container, and if so, what is the minimum you accept?" Ask for that in writing before you buy anything. Some cemeteries will let you buy the liner from them, direct, at a lower price than the funeral home charges for the same box.

Tonight's check: call the cemetery. Not the funeral home. The cemetery.

Number nine. The cash advance items.

This is the last line and it is the sneakiest, because it is not one line. It is a little section at the bottom of the price list with a heading that says cash advance items, or advances, or sometimes nothing at all.

Cash advance means money the funeral home pays to somebody else on your behalf. The flowers. The obituary in the newspaper. The honorarium for the priest or the minister. The death certificates from the county. The hairdresser. The musician.

The Funeral Rule says the funeral home has to tell you, in writing, what the price is and whether they are adding anything on top of it. That addition has a name, it is usually called a service fee or a handling charge, and it is often a percentage.

In 2018 a family here paid six hundred dollars for an obituary in the local paper. The paper's own rate, if you place it yourself, was three hundred and forty. The difference was not the newspaper's price. It was the funeral home's fee for making a phone call.

What you say is: "Please show me the cash advance section, and show me what your fee is on each item."

And then, for the flowers and the obituary, you call the florist and the newspaper yourself. You are allowed. Nobody can stop you, and most of the time it is half.

Tonight's check: turn the page over and find the words cash advance. If they are not there, ask why.

So there they are. Nine lines.

Embalming, when there is no viewing. The casket, and the handling fee that is a tenth of the difference. The rooms you will not use. The limousines you do not need. The stationery you can print yourself for forty-two dollars. The urn, which can be a box your husband made in 1968. The after-hours fee that is sometimes waived if you ask. The vault, which the cemetery decides and the law does not. And the cash advance fee that nobody ever points at.

Nine lines out of sixteen. And the other seven, the ones you keep, are the ones that actually do the work: the people who come at three in the morning, the paperwork, the permits, the room where your family can sit down and cry in private.

I am not against the funeral home. I have been the funeral home for forty years. I am against the family walking out of that office with a number in their head and no idea what is inside it.

So here is what I want you to do tonight, and it takes five minutes.

Find the paper. If you do not have one, call a funeral home tomorrow and ask for it, and they have to give it to you. Take a pen. Put it on the kitchen table under a good light. Read the sixteen lines out loud, one at a time. And every time you do not understand a word, circle it. Circle it and call and ask. That is it. That is the whole system. A pen and a telephone.

My father never had a price list. He had a calendar with a pencil number on the back of it, and he had a chair where he sat with people until they understood. That is the part that the law cannot print on a piece of paper. The law can make them hand it to you. It cannot make them sit down with you.
