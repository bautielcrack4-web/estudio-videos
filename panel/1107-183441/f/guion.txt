There is a piece of paper that sits on the desk of every funeral home in this country, and it is the most useful piece of paper you will ever hold in the worst week of your life.

It is one page. Sometimes two. It has every price in that building printed on it, line by line, in small type. And under federal law they have to put it in your hands before you talk about anything — before you pick a casket, before you pick a date, before you sign a single thing — and you get to take it home with you.

I have watched families sit two feet away from that page for an hour and walk out without it. Not because they were foolish. Because nobody told them it was theirs. Because the man across the desk was kind. And because it was Tuesday, and their mother had died on Sunday, and they had not slept.

My name is Ernie Navarro. I am a funeral director. I started at thirteen, sweeping the chapel of my father's funeral home in Analco, in Guadalajara. I spent fifteen years there with him, and twenty-five more in San Antonio, Texas — the West Side, Guadalupe Street, Zarzamora — with Mexican families who had the same questions and got the same silence. I have buried close to seven thousand people. And I have sat on both sides of that desk.

Today I want to give you seven things that funeral home is not going to tell you before you sign. Not because they are criminals. Because the whole arrangement is built so that you will not ask, and nobody in that room is paid to volunteer anything.

And the seventh one — the last one — is the one that costs the most money, and it is not even on the same contract.

Let me show you how that room works, because everything I am going to tell you starts there.

You walk in, and somebody brings you a glass of water. There is a soft lamp, not the ceiling light. There is a folder on the desk with your mother's name written on the tab in blue ink, before you said one word. And there is a man or a woman who is very, very good at this — because being good at this is part of the job, and I know, because I was good at it too.

In that room, the person across the desk has two jobs. The first one is to take care of your mother. The second one is to sell a funeral. Both are real. Both are happening at the same time, and the whole room is designed so that the second one feels like the first one.

I am not telling you this to make you angry at them. I am telling you so that you walk in knowing where the door is.

So before we start, I want you to do something with me. This is a small test and it takes four minutes.

Pause this video. Go find the last funeral bill your family paid. Your father, your grandmother, an uncle, anybody. Maybe it is in a drawer in the kitchen. Maybe it is a photo on your phone.

Now look at it. Do not read the total. Count the lines that have a price but no quantity. No days, no copies, no hours, no miles. Just a price, sitting there by itself.

Most bills have three or four of those lines. Mine did, the first one I ever wrote by hand, in my father's office, in 1985.

If you do not have a bill, then sit down for a minute and write three things on a piece of paper. The three things you would want if it were your mother. The visit — the viewing, the wake, whatever your family calls it. The service. And the ground, or the fire, whichever your family does.

Just those three. Do not write anything else. Not flowers, not cars, not a folder, not a video tribute. Three things. Keep that paper. We are going to use it in a few minutes, and I promise you it is worth more than anything I could sell you.

[emphatically] Number one. The price list is yours, and they are hoping you leave it on the desk.

Under the Funeral Rule — the federal rule that has been in place since 1984 — a funeral home has to give you an itemized General Price List when you walk in and ask about prices, before they show you anything. Every service, every item, every charge, with the price next to it. It also has to give you a separate casket price list. And you are allowed to take both of them home.

I want you to hear the part that nobody says out loud. The list is not a courtesy. It is not a favor they do for nice families. It is the price of doing business in this country, and it belongs to you the moment you sit down.

So here is your check for tonight. It takes one phone call, and you do not have to be planning a funeral to make it.

Call a funeral home in your town, any one, and say these words: "Could you email me your General Price List and your casket price list?" Not "your prices". Not "a brochure". Those two names. Then save the email, and put it in the same folder where you keep your will or your insurance, because that is what it is.

If they say they cannot send it, that is information too, and it is free.

Number two. You can buy the casket somewhere else, and they have to take it.

This one makes people look at me like I am telling them to steal something. I am not. I am telling you what the rule says, and I am telling you what I watched with my own eyes for twenty-five years.

The Funeral Rule says a funeral home cannot refuse a casket that you bought from somebody else. It cannot charge you a fee for handling it. It cannot require you to be there to receive it. It cannot tell you it is against their policy, because their policy is not the law.

And the reason they never tell you this is simple. The casket is the most profitable thing in that building. Not the service, not the cars, not the chapel. The box.

A casket that costs the funeral home around a thousand dollars sells for twenty-five hundred, three thousand, thirty-five hundred. That is not a secret in the industry. It is the economics of the industry. And it is why, in some homes, the person who shows you the casket room is the best salesperson on staff, and why the room is lit like a jewelry store and why they always start you at the top of the room and walk you down.

I had a family in 2019, on the West Side, the Sosas. Mr. Sosa had worked forty years at a bakery, and his three daughters had already picked out a casket at a store across town for eleven hundred dollars. Same wood, same lining, same handles. They brought me the receipt and asked me if it was all right.

I said, it is not only all right. It is the law. Say it exactly like that, because the director who tells you no is not doing you a favor by breaking a rule — he is doing you harm by hiding one.

Now, I am going to be fair to my own business. There are good reasons to buy from the home. You can see it, you can touch it, and if something is wrong with it, somebody in that building is responsible. That is worth something. Not two thousand dollars. But something.

So your check for tonight is this. If you are ever in that room, ask one question: "What is your price for this casket, and is there a charge if I bring my own?"

Write the answer down. The answer is a sentence you will be able to repeat to your brother, and that sentence will save you more money than anything else in this video.

Number three. Embalming is almost never required by law. The words "required by law" are a sales script.

This is the one that gets me. Forty years, and I still hear it.

Embalming is the process of replacing the fluids in a body with preserving fluid. It is a real thing, it is old, and in some situations it is necessary — if a body is going to travel, if there is a public viewing with an open casket in some states, if there is a communicable disease and the state says so. In those cases it is required, and it is right.

But in the ordinary case — a death at home or in a hospital, a funeral within a few days, a closed casket, a cremation — the law in most states does not require it. Not Texas. Not California. Not Illinois. Most states do not.

And yet, on the arrangement form, next to the line that says embalming, you will see three words in small print: required by law. Sometimes it is a box that has already been checked when the folder is opened.

It costs between seven hundred and nine hundred dollars, and it takes about two hours.

The reason funeral homes do this is not evil. It is habit and it is fear. Habit, because for a hundred years a funeral without embalming was unusual. Fear, because a family who is not expecting it carries that memory for the rest of their life, and the director does not want to be the one who gave it to them.

[whispering] Both are real. Neither is the law.

Here is what I want you to understand, and this is the human part. If you want embalming for your mother — if you want to see her, if you want the family to see her, if that is what your family does — then pay for it, and do not feel bad for one second. That is not being taken. That is choosing. The problem is never the service. The problem is the sentence "required by law" when it is not true.

Your check for tonight: ask the question in those exact words. "Is embalming required in this case, and can you show me where it says so?"

The answer will be either a law or a house policy. Both are fine answers. Only one of them is a law, and you deserve to know which one you are paying for.

Number four. The things they pay for on your behalf have to be the real price, and you can ask for the receipts.

On that price list there is a section with a name that sounds like nothing: cash advance items. Or sometimes, outside charges, or third party.

Those are the things the funeral home pays for somebody else, with your money. The death certificates from the county. The notice in the newspaper. The priest, the minister, the mariachi, the honorarium for the church. The flowers. The limousine company. The crematory, if they do not own one.

The Funeral Rule is specific about this. Those items have to be listed, and when the bill comes, they have to be the actual cost. If the funeral home adds anything on top, it has to tell you in writing that it is adding it, and how much.

Most families never see that. They see one line that says outside expenses, three hundred and forty dollars, and they pay it, because who argues about three hundred dollars the week their father died.

And most of the time, that number is exactly right. I say it clearly, because I do not want you walking in suspicious of people who are doing their job. Most itemized bills I have seen in forty years were correct to the dollar.

But not all of them. In 2012 I saw a bill where the newspaper notice had been marked up by sixty dollars, and nobody had told the family. It was a small thing. It was also the family's money, and the family was a widow on a fixed income who had asked me, in the parking lot, whether she could pay in three months.

So your check is not a confrontation. It is one sentence, said kindly, at the desk, before you sign: "When the bill comes, could you include the receipts for the outside charges?"

A funeral director who is doing his job will say yes before you finish the sentence. And if he hesitates, you have learned something for free.

Number five. The three packages are designed so that the middle one looks reasonable.

You have seen it. Good, better, best. Or bronze, silver, gold. Three columns on one page, with the middle column circled in pencil, because the person across the desk circled it before you arrived.

Here is what is happening, and it is not a funeral home trick. It is a trick that every store in America uses, and it works on all of us.

Eight thousand feels enormous by itself. Eight thousand next to eleven thousand feels like a decision you made wisely. The middle package exists to be bought. The top one exists so the middle one looks like restraint. And the bottom one exists so nobody can say the funeral home offered nothing affordable — even though it usually leaves out the one thing the family wants, so that you have to move up.

I learned this from my own son. Rafa runs the funeral home now, and one day he showed me a new menu he had made. Three columns. I asked him, why is the middle one called the traditional service, and the top one the complete service? He said, because nobody buys the complete one, Pá. They buy the middle one, and they feel good about it.

He was right. He was also selling families a folder of paper they did not need, and we had a long conversation in the back office that I will tell you about another day.

Your check is this. Take the package apart. Say: "I do not want the package. I want the items. Could you show me what the middle package has that the bottom one does not, line by line, with the price of each line?"

That is a legal request. When a family does that, something changes in the room, because now you are talking about a casket and a service and a viewing, and not about bronze, silver and gold.

Number six. The cremation price is not the cremation bill.

Direct cremation is the simplest thing a funeral home sells. No viewing, no service, no cars, no chapel. Pick up the body, cremate it, give the family the ashes. In most of the country that is between twelve hundred and two thousand five hundred dollars, and it is the plain version of the cheapest thing in the building.

But when the bill arrives, it is not twelve hundred dollars. It is three thousand, or four, and here is where the difference comes from.

The container. The law says the funeral home has to offer you an alternative container — a simple, combustible box — on the price list. What it does not have to do is tell you that the one it shows you first, the one that looks like a real casket, costs eight hundred dollars more than the one that does the same job.

The identification viewing. Twenty minutes, in a room, before the cremation, so the family can say goodbye. Two hundred and fifty dollars.

The witness cremation, if you want to be there when it goes in. Three hundred.

The rental casket. If you want a viewing and then a cremation, you do not have to buy a casket. You rent one, with an insert inside. Families do not know this exists, and it is on the price list.

Storage, if the crematory is backed up and your mother waits four days. Some homes charge by the day. Ask.

And the after-hours fee, if your father dies at nine at night and you call at nine at night. Ask.

None of these are wrong. Each one is a real service that costs real money. But a family that is told one number and then receives another has been lied to by omission, and that is the one thing I have never forgiven in my own business.

Number seven is the one that costs the most money, and it is not on the contract you are signing.

The funeral home sells you the funeral. The cemetery sells you the ground. Those are two different companies, two different contracts, and usually two different rooms, and the person who sells you the second one is often not in the building when you sign the first one.

So here is what happens. You sign at the funeral home on Tuesday. On Wednesday, somebody from the cemetery calls, or the funeral director hands you a card and says, my friend will take care of the plot.

The plot. The opening and closing of the grave — that is the digging, and it is not included in the plot, and it is between eight hundred and two thousand dollars.

The vault, or the grave liner. A concrete box that goes into the ground so the ground does not sink. Many cemeteries require it.
