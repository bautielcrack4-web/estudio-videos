Carol called me last Sunday from Sacramento. She was wearing the same jeans she has had since 1998, and she asked me one question. Michiko, she said, what do I wear on the plane to Kyoto?

Carol. Japanese women over sixty wear jeans too. We just wear them differently.

Seven differences. Not one of them is about being thinner, or richer. I am five feet tall. I am a size fourteen. I am sixty-eight. It is the waistband. It is the length. It is where the back pocket sits.

The last one, almost no woman in America knows it. A tailor here charges twelve dollars for it. By the end of this video you will know exactly what to change tonight, with the jeans you already have in your closet.

My name is Michiko Arai. I live in the wooden house my family has kept in Nishijin, the weavers' quarter of Kyoto. My mother was Fumiko. For forty years she was a kitsuke-shi: she dressed brides and geiko in kimono in Gion. She could look at a woman standing in a doorway and tell you, before the woman said one word, exactly where the garment was pulling.

I also lived nineteen years in California. Sunnyvale, from 1983 to 2002. My husband Hiroshi was an engineer there. I wore jeans every day of the nineties. I did it the American way, and in every photograph from those years I look tired.

Every week I see videos that tell you: no jeans after sixty. Only black. Cover the hips, cover the arms, hide. My mother would have said the opposite, and she dressed three thousand women.

The problem was never the jeans. American jeans are cut for a body that is nineteen, and five feet nine. That is all. You are not that body. Neither am I. So we change three small things, and we keep the jeans.

Number one. The waistband.

The mistake is pulling your jeans up, over the belly, as high as they will go, because it feels safe. Then at noon there is a red line across the stomach, and the body looks like it is being held up by a rope.

In 1975 my mother dressed a bride in Gion. The bride's mother was a round woman, and she stood at the door apologizing for her waist before she even stepped inside. My mother said: the obi does not go where the fat is. It goes where the body bends. She put two fingers above the hip, on the side, and she tied the obi there. That woman stood up straight for the first time all morning.

After sixty the waist widens, and the body bends near the hip bone, not above the belly. The waistband has to sit on that bend. High-rise jeans on a woman of sixty-eight do not make her look slim. They make her look like she is holding her breath.

What to change. Take the pair you already own with the mid-rise, the one that sits on the hip bone, and put it on. If the back of the waistband opens away from you when you sit, that is the only thing to fix. Take that pair to a tailor and ask one thing: take in the back of the waistband, one or two centimeters, so it stays against your back. Fifteen dollars. That is the whole fix.

Tonight, stand in front of the mirror with your hand flat on your stomach. Slide two fingers under the front of the waistband. If the whole back of the jeans lifts away from your body, they are too big in the waist. If your fingers cannot go in, they are too small. A waistband should touch, not press.

Number two. The fabric.

You know the jeans that kneel. By eleven in the morning they have two bags at the knees, and a shine at the seat, and by evening they are a different pair of trousers than the ones you put on.

That is the stretch. Many women's jeans sold in America today have polyester in them, and three to five percent elastane. Past two percent, the denim stops holding its own shape, and it takes the shape of wherever you sat.

My mother had one test for fabric, and she used it on kimono and on everything else. She would pick up a corner and let it fall. If it fell straight, it was yours. If it floated, it belonged to the shop.

After sixty the skin on the thigh is thinner and softer, and we sit more than we did at forty. A fabric that sags shows every hour of your day.

What to change. Turn the jeans inside out and read the label. You want ninety-eight percent cotton, two percent elastane. Nothing more. In Kyoto I buy a pair like that for about forty dollars. That same pair exists in America at the same price. You just have to read the tag before you look in the mirror.

And a heavier denim, eleven or twelve ounces, costs the same and lasts nine years. Hiroshi has work trousers from 1991. He has three shirts and one pair of trousers, and he is very happy.

Now, before number three, do something with me. It takes one minute.

Go and sit on a kitchen chair. A hard one, not the sofa. Sit the way you sit when you are waiting for the kettle. Now cross your legs. Now stay there, and breathe out.

If the waistband cuts into your stomach when you sit, the jeans are one size too small in the waist, no matter what the label says and no matter what the mirror said while you were standing. Standing lies. Sitting tells the truth.

My mother used this on every bride, on the morning of the wedding, sitting on the tatami. Cross the legs, breathe out, and watch the face. If the face changes, the garment is wrong. It never failed. Try it tonight with three pairs. Keep the one that lets you breathe, and put the others in a bag for the tailor.

Number three. The leg.

For twenty years America told us the jean should be tight from the hip to the ankle. Skinny. I wore them in Sunnyvale in 1994, with a big sweater, and I looked like a triangle standing on two pencils.

Look at a kimono from the side. My mother called it the one line: from the shoulder, straight down, to the floor. Nothing grabs. Nothing flares. The woman inside is a column, not a shape.

After sixty the calf softens and the knee loses its line. A tight jean draws a ring around the calf and another around the knee, and the eye stops there. The eye should go all the way down to your shoe.

What to change. A straight leg. Not a wide leg, because a wide leg on a small woman looks like a skirt that got lost. Straight: the width at the ankle is the same as the width at the knee, maybe two centimeters more. Go to your closet and find the pair that is already the straightest, the one that falls from the hip without grabbing the calf. That is your pair for the daytime. The skinny ones stay, for tucking into boots in winter. Nothing to buy, and nothing to rebuild.

Tonight, stand sideways in the mirror, in your socks, and look at the outside line from your hip to your heel. If the line is broken at the knee, the jean is wrong. Not your leg.

Number four. The length.

I am five feet tall, and for thirty years I walked on the hems of my jeans. The back of the hem goes black, it drinks the rain, and it makes the whole body look shorter, because the eye follows the fabric down to the floor and stops there.

My neighbor, Mrs. Kinoshita, is eighty-one. Every morning she walks the Philosopher's Path. Ten thousand steps, in the same flat leather shoes she has had for fifteen years. She wears her trousers to the ankle bone, never longer. She says: if you cannot see the shoe, you cannot see the woman.

After sixty the ankle gets thinner. That is not a problem, that is a gift. Two centimeters of ankle between the hem and the shoe makes the leg look longer, and it makes the shoe look like a choice instead of an accident.

What to change. Hem your jeans to the ankle bone, and then take two centimeters more. The tailor under the arcade on Sanjō charges about twelve dollars. In America, ten. Do it once, in one pair, and you will never wear the long ones again.

Tonight, put on the jeans and your winter shoes, stand straight, and look down. If you cannot see the top of your shoe, or the bone of your ankle, they are too long.

Number five. The color at the top.

This one is not really about the jeans. It is about what sits on top of them, next to your face.

In America the uniform is dark jeans, black turtleneck, black jacket. It is easy, it is safe, and it takes the light out of the face. My mother never put black next to a woman's face after sixty. Not because black is wrong, but because black is heavy, and the face has to carry it.

After sixty the skin loses some of its pink. A black collar next to a face without pink makes the face look grey. And then the woman thinks she looks tired. She is right. But it is the collar, not her.

What to change. Keep the jeans, change the top. A blue that is close to your eyes. A soft white. A mustard. My friend Carol tried on a mustard scarf in a shop in Sacramento, and when she sent me the photograph, her face looked ten years lighter in that one photo and grey in all the others. Same woman, same day. A scarf.

Tonight, take the top you wore today and hold it under your chin in front of the window. Then take a white towel and do the same. Look at your mouth, not your eyes. Whichever one makes your mouth look pinker is your color. That is the whole test.

Number six. The top half.

There is a reason French women tuck in the front and leave the back out, and there is a reason it works on us too. A top that hangs to the middle of the thigh cuts the body in two, and the eye reads the shortest part. A top that ends at the hip bone, tucked in the front only, gives you a waist without a belt.

My mother used to say a woman is not one piece. She is three: the face, the middle, the feet. The mistake is to make the middle disappear. Then you have a face and a pair of shoes, floating.

After sixty, most of us buy tops one size bigger to hide the stomach, and that one size bigger is exactly what makes the stomach look bigger.

What to change. A top that ends at the hip bone. A knit that is not tight and not a tent. And a French tuck, the front only, two fingers of fabric. And a belt only if it is thin. A wide belt on a short waist is a second waistband, and you already have one.

Tonight, put on the jeans and take three tops from the closet. Look at where each one ends. If it ends at the widest part of your hip, it goes to the back of the closet. If it ends at the bone, it stays.

[emphatically] Number seven. The back pocket.

Go and look at the back of your jeans. Where does the pocket sit? Most jeans for women in America have a pocket that is too big and too low, and the point of it sits below the curve of the seat. That one detail makes the seat look long and flat, whatever is underneath it. And that pocket was not sewn for you. It was sewn for a factory three thousand miles away from your body.

In Kyoto the tailor on Sanjō does this in ten minutes. He takes the pocket off, moves it up two centimeters, and takes in the seat a little at the center seam. Twelve dollars. When Yui brought her jeans there last spring, the tailor looked at her and said: you are small, the pocket is for a tall girl, we fix it. She came out standing two centimeters taller.

Why does this work after sixty? Because the seat drops a little, and the pocket drops with it, and the eye follows the pocket down. Raise the pocket, and the eye goes up.

Tonight, stand with your back to the mirror and hold a hand mirror so you can see. Find the lowest point of the pocket. If it is below the widest part of you, take the jeans to the tailor and say one sentence: move the pocket up. That is all.

[whispering] Now, the last thing. And it is the most important. Do not buy anything.

Open the closet. Put every pair of jeans you own on the bed, side by side. Look at three things: the waistband, the length at the ankle, the pocket. One of them is already right. That pair is your pair. The others go to the tailor, and you spend thirty dollars, not three hundred.

My mother said: if it does not make you stand straight, it does not belong to you. She said it about kimono, in 1979, to a bride who was crying in the back room of a house in Gion. But it is the same for jeans. Stand straight, and the clothes are yours.
