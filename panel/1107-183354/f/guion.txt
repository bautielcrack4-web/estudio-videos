Four hundred bedsides. That is the number I can stand behind, and I will tell you why in a minute. Of those four hundred families, about a hundred and sixty had somebody with dementia. I am not a physician and I am not on the medical team. I was the chaplain. For thirty-four years, with Alamo Home Hospice here in San Antonio. I sat in the chair next to the bed and I stayed long enough for the talking to start.

And here is what I need you to hear before anything else. Near the end, people with dementia say things. Real sentences. Whole sentences, out of a mouth that had not finished a sentence in months. The families hear it, and they go white, and they look at me like I am supposed to explain the universe. Most of the time I do not explain it. I say: write it down, mija. Because it is not going to come again.

I kept a notebook. A spiral notebook, seventy-nine cents at the H-E-B on Bandera Road, green cover, and I wrote down what the people in those beds said in the last weeks. I wrote it down because the families asked me to remember, and because I have a bad memory and a good pen.

Seven things. Seven kinds of sentences I heard over and over, in different houses, in different years, from people who did not know each other. I am going to give you all seven, and for each one I am going to tell you what it usually means, what I did, and what you can do tonight for almost no money. Because that is the part nobody tells you. Everybody tells you to wait. Nobody tells you what to say back.

[whispering] And I am going to start with the one that breaks families the most. The one that sounds like a complaint and is not.

One. "I want to go home."

I heard this from Inés Garza. Señora Inés. She lived off Bandera Road, out past the H-E-B, in a little house with a porch and a screen door that did not latch right, and her daughter Elena had moved her into the front bedroom with the hospital bed because the back bedroom was too far from the bathroom. This was 2019. Elena did everything right. She quit her job at the credit union. She put a baby monitor on the nightstand and slept four hours a night for eleven months.

And every evening around six, her mother would start. "I want to go home. Take me home. I don't belong here."

Elena would say: "Mamá, you are home. This is your house. You have lived here thirty-one years." And her mother would look at her like Elena was a stranger with bad manners, and say it again. "I want to go home."

Elena called me in tears one Thursday. She said, "She doesn't recognize her own house. She thinks I kidnapped her."

I want you to understand something about this sentence, and it took me years to understand it, and I learned it from Lola Benavides, the old chaplain who trained me in 1991. Lola used to say: listen to the feeling, not the address.

"Home" is almost never the house. When somebody in the last months of dementia says "I want to go home," they are usually not talking about a building. They are talking about a condition. Home is where they are not confused. Home is where their mother is. Home is where the furniture is the furniture they grew up with, the smell of the kitchen, the dog that died in 1978. Home is before.

You cannot drive them there. There is no road.

And when you argue with the address — "you ARE home, look at the picture of Daddy on the wall" — you are asking a brain that cannot hold a fact to hold a fact. That is a fight you cannot win, and you will lose it every single night, and then you will both cry.

So what did I tell Elena?

I told her: stop correcting. When your mother says "I want to go home," you say, "Tell me about home, Mamá." That is it. That is the whole trick. And then you listen, and you write down what she says, because in the middle of that answer she will tell you where home is. For Señora Inés, it was not Bandera Road. It was a house in Laredo with a fig tree in the yard and a kitchen with a blue door. She described that fig tree for twenty minutes. She described picking the figs for her grandmother. Elena had never heard that story in her whole life. Fifty-one years old, and she had never heard it.

And here is the strange, merciful part. After she talked about the fig tree, she stopped asking to go home for the rest of the evening. Not every night. Most nights. The asking was not a request for a car. It was a request for somebody to sit with her inside the place she was homesick for.

Cost? A notebook. The one you already have, or the one at the grocery store for a dollar. Tonight, when your mother or your father says "I want to go home," do not correct them. Say "tell me about home." Write down three things they say. That is your homework, and it is worth more than the medication I am not allowed to talk about.

Two. They call for their mother. Or their father. Or somebody who has been dead for fifty years.

This one is the one that scares people. I want to tell you what I saw, and I want to tell you what I did not see.

I am thinking of a lady in her nineties, in a duplex off Culebra, 2014, whose son had hired round-the-clock help and still slept on the couch in the living room. She had not said a full sentence in about a year. And one night, around two in the morning, she said, clear as a bell: "Mamá, ya vengo." Mama, I'm coming.

Her son heard it from the couch. He came in and she was looking at the corner of the room, near the dresser, and she was smiling. Not frightened. Smiling, like a girl.

He asked me the next day: "Is she seeing things? Is this the disease? Is she hallucinating? Should I have called somebody?"

And this is where I have to be careful, because I am not on the medical team, and I am not going to tell you what is happening in a brain. Call your hospice team. That is always the answer to the medical question, and it is a real answer, not a brush-off. They have seen this a thousand times and can tell you what is in the chart and what is in the body.

But I can tell you what I did in the room, and what I have done in about a hundred rooms since.

I did not tell her there was nobody there. I did not tell her mother was dead. I did not flip the light on and say "look, nothing, see?" That is what we do when we are frightened, honey, we turn on the light. But she was not frightened. I was. The son was. She was having a reunion.

So I sat down next to her where she could see my face and I said, "Is she here?" And she nodded. And I said, "That's good. That's good, mija. Tell her hello for me."

Do you know what that costs? Nothing. Do you know what it does? It keeps her out of a fight she cannot win with a face you cannot see.

Now, some of you are going to say: but chaplain, is that lying? I have been asked that in church parking lots and at funerals and in a Whataburger on Zarzamora.

It is not lying to a person living in a different room. It is walking into their room. If your mother is in 1939 with her mamá, and you stand in the doorway shouting the year 2026 at her, you are not telling the truth — you are being cruel with the truth. Truth without tenderness is just noise. The truth you owe her is this: that you are here, that she is not alone, that whatever she is seeing, you are not going to grab her by the shoulders and drag her out of it.

So tonight: if you hear them talking to somebody who has died, do not argue. Ask a question. "Is she here?" "What is she wearing?" "Does she look good?" And if they ask you where that person is, you can say, "I don't know, honey, but she's close, I can tell." That is not a lie. You can tell. You can tell by the face.

Three. "Where is my husband?" — or wife. And the husband is gone.

This one has a special kind of cruelty in it, and it is called, in my notebook, the widow question. I heard it maybe thirty times.

There was a lady in Leon Valley, 2017. Married fifty-four years. Her husband had died in 2011 — six years before. And her dementia had eaten the six years. So every afternoon she would ask, "Where's Rudy? Is Rudy coming?" And every afternoon her daughter would say, "Mamá, Daddy died. Remember? We buried him at Fort Sam." And every afternoon, her mother would sit down and cry like a widow at a fresh grave. Every single afternoon. For a year and a half.

Do you see what that family did? They buried Rudy about five hundred times.

The daughter told me, "I can't lie to her. It feels wrong." And I said, honey, what feels right about burying your father every day at four o'clock?

Here is what we did. When her mother asked, "Where's Rudy?" We said, "He's at work, mija. He'll be home later." Because in 1974, when her memory was living, Rudy worked at the post office and he came home at six and he kissed her at the door and he smelled like the outside. That was true. That is where Rudy was in her life. And she would say, "Oh, good," and go back to folding the towel in her lap.

Was that a lie? It was a kindness that did not cost a single person anything. And I want to be clear: it did not work every day, and it will not work every day for you. But it worked most days. And most days is the whole game at this stage. You are not looking for perfect. You are looking for a soft landing.

[excited] And I want you to know this is not only sad. I have sat in a kitchen in Prospect Hill and heard a ninety-year-old woman describe her wedding in 1951 in detail — the dress, the priest's name, the tamales — while her daughter wrote it all down crying and laughing at the same time. That is not a tragedy. That is an archive. These sentences are the last tapes in the house. Make copies.

Four. "I'm sorry." And then a thing from fifty years ago.

This one is quieter. People do not call me about it. The families carry it alone.

I had a man in his eighties, 2013, off Guadalupe Street, a few blocks from the church. His daughter was with him. And in his last three weeks he kept saying, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, mija. Forgive me." And his daughter did not know what he was talking about. She thought maybe he was apologizing for being sick, for the mess, for the money. And then one night he said it with a name attached. A name she did not recognize.

She called me and said, "Chaplain, who is this? Who is he apologizing to?"

I do not know. I still do not know. It might have been God, or his mother. He never got to the story.

But here is what I have learned in thirty-four years, and I will say it as plainly as I can. The dying, very often, are not asking for an answer. They are asking for a witness. "I'm sorry" is not a request for information. It is a request for somebody to sit down next to it.

So what do you say? You do not say, "You have nothing to be sorry for," because that is a fight, and because maybe he does have something, and you are not the judge of his heart. And you do not say, "Who? Who are you sorry to?" That is a cross-examination. He is not on the stand.

You say: "I'm here. It's all right. Whatever it is, it's all right now."

You say: "Thank you for telling me."

And if you are a person of faith, and only if it is your faith too, you can say, "God is not holding it against you, honey. Neither am I."

And if the person is gone, if the family is a mess, if it is too late, then you do what the daughter off Guadalupe Street did. She sat down and she said, "Papi, I don't know who it is. But if you want to tell me, I'll listen. And if you don't, that's okay too. I'm not going anywhere." And he cried for a while, and then he slept, and in the morning he did not mention it again, and she had done her job perfectly.

Cost: an hour of your evening. Tonight: if your parent says "I'm sorry," do not fix it. Sit with it. Put your hand on their arm and say, "I'm here. It's all right."

Now, before I give you the last three, I want to do something with you. I want to see if you and I can do this together, because you are watching this in a kitchen or a hallway or a hospital parking lot, and I cannot see your face, but I know why you are here.

Here is my test. Three sentences. I want you to pick the one that is hardest for you to hear. Do not overthink it. The one that made your stomach move.

One: "I want to go home."

Two: "Mamá, ya vengo." Mama, I'm coming.

Three: "Don't leave me."

Pick one. Say it out loud, right now, in your real voice. I'll wait. I am a chaplain. I am very good at waiting. Lola used to say the dying are never in a hurry — we are. So say it, honey. Say it like your own parent says it.

Did you feel that? That little pull under your ribs? That is not weakness. That is the thing that has kept you getting up at two in the morning for a year. That is the love, and it is not a soft thing, it is a muscle, and it is tired.

Now here is the second part, and this is the part that matters. Say back to yourself what you would say to them. Out loud. Right now. "Tell me about home." Or "I'm here." Or "It's all right, honey."

Did it feel like a lie? Good. It is not supposed to feel natural. Nothing about this is natural. You are being asked to enter a room that does not make sense, and you are being asked to do it with love and no map. Every single person who has done this learned it by doing it badly first. You are not behind. There is no schedule. You are exactly as far along as you are.

Five. "Don't leave me." And then, the night shift.

This one is not sweet. I am not going to make it sweet. This one is a logistics problem, and it is the one that breaks the caregivers, not the person in the bed.

I want to tell you about the Hollis family. Southside, 2016. Three sisters. Their mother had vascular dementia and the last six weeks were mostly nights. One of them — the middle one, Linda — had not slept in her own bed since September. She slept on a recliner in the living room with one eye open. She was fifty-eight years old and she looked seventy.

And every night her mother would say, "Don't leave. Don't leave me alone." And Linda would say, "I'm not going anywhere, Mama." And she meant it. And she did not go anywhere. For forty-one nights.

By the last week, Linda was so tired she was crying in the pantry. And I want to tell you what I told her, and I want you to hear it as a chaplain and not as a medical person, because this is not an instruction about a body. This is about the soul of the caregiver.

I said: Linda, your mother is not asking you to die with her. She is asking you not to disappear. Those are two different things. And you are going to disappear if you do not sleep.

Here is what we did, and it cost them almost nothing.

We made a night card. A white index card, taped to the refrigerator, and on it in Linda's handwriting: the hospice team's number, the on-call number, the aide's name and the days she comes, and then, at the bottom, in big letters: "SHE IS NOT ALONE. I AM ASLEEP. THAT IS ALLOWED."

And we made a rotation. The three sisters drew a calendar on the back of a church bulletin. Whoever had the night had the night, and the other two were off. Not "on call." Off. And the person on the night put their chair where their mother could see them when she opened her eyes, and they held her hand, and if she said, "Don't leave," they said, "I'm right here. I'm going to be right here. I'm going to close my eyes for a little while, but my hand stays."

The hand stays. You can sleep with your hand in a hand. That is not abandoning anybody. That is the hand doing its job while the rest of you rests.

Linda slept. She got up at two the first two nights to check, which is normal, and then she didn't. And her mother had eleven more nights, and on about the fourth night she stopped saying "don't leave me." I think because the hand was always there, and her body learned that the hand was part of the furniture.

And I want to say one more thing about the night shift, and then I will move on. If you are the only one, and there is no sister, and no cousin, and no money for an aide, then you call the hospice. You call them and you say the words: "I am not sleeping." Not "my mother is doing this" — "I am not sleeping." There is a benefit in this country, and it is part of the hospice benefit, it is called respite, and it can put your parent in a facility for up to five days so that you can sleep, and it is there for exactly this. It is not a failure. It is not giving up. It is the thing that lets you finish.

Six. "Is it okay?" "Can I go now?" "Is everybody ready?"

This is the one I want you to remember when you turn this off. If you remember one thing, honey, remember this one.

I have heard this sentence in maybe forty rooms. It comes near the very end. Not always in words. Sometimes it is "Is it okay?" Sometimes it is "I need to go." Sometimes it is a person who cannot speak at all, lifting their eyebrows and looking at the door, and then at you, and then at the door. Sometimes it is "Is everybody here?"

And here is what I believe, after thirty-four years, and I will tell you it is a belief and not a fact, because I am a chaplain and I am not going to sell you certainty. I believe they are asking permission. They are asking: can I go? Will you be all right? Is it allowed?

And here is the thing that undoes me every time. Families do not give it. They do not give it because they do not understand the question. They think the person is confused, or they are so full of their own grief that the only thing they can say is "don't go."

I sat with a family in Alamo Heights in 2021, and this is going to be hard to hear. The father had been in the last days for four days. Four days of that breathing, the kind families call the rattle. His wife of sixty years sat by the bed and every time he got restless she said, "Stay with me, Bob. Don't you go. Stay with me." And he stayed. Four days. I do not know what that cost him. I know what it cost her — she was a ghost by the end.

The daughter pulled me aside and said, "Chaplain, is he waiting for something?" And I said, "Honey, I think he is waiting for your mother to say yes." And she looked at me like I had hit her.

That night she sat down on the bed and she took her father's hand and she said, "Daddy, we're going to be okay. Mama's going to be okay. I'm going to take care of her. You did good. You can go whenever you're ready. We'll be all right."

He died the next morning at six-forty. I am not going to tell you that the sentence did it. But I will tell you what I have seen, over and over, in forty rooms. When the family says yes, something in the room lets go. And when the family cannot say it, the person waits. And waiting is hard work. It is the hardest work there is.

So I am going to give you the sentence. Write it on an index card and put it in your wallet, so that when the moment comes and your mouth is dry and your heart is going like a bird, you have something to read.

"Daddy, I love you. You did good. We're going to be okay. You can go when you're ready."

That is it. You do not have to say it perfectly. You do not have to say it once. You can say it every night for a week. The person does not need a speech. They need a yes.

And if you cannot say it yet, that is not a sin, honey. That is a human being. Say the first half. "I love you. You did good." Leave the rest for when you can.

Seven. And then they stop talking. And something else happens.

Nobody tells you about this part. The last one is not a sentence. The last one is the absence of sentences, and it has its own language, and if you do not know the language you will think the person has left already. They have not.

I want to tell you about the hands.

In my notebook I have a page near the back, and it is not organized, it is just a list, and the heading is "hands." Things I saw people do with their hands in the last days. One woman, in a house off Fredericksburg Road, spent two days folding and unfolding a corner of the bedsheet, over and over, neat, like she was making a bed. Her son thought she was agitated. I do not know what she was doing. I know that when he put his hand under hers and let her fold the sheet around both their hands, she stopped being agitated and her face went calm, and he sat there for an hour like that.

Another man, a farmer from out near New Braunfels, picked at the air above his chest for two days, like he was untangling a string. His granddaughter, who was nineteen and had no fear in her at all, lay down next to him and put her hand in his and said, "Here, Grandpa, I'll hold it." And he held her hand instead. And that was the end of the picking.

I am not going to tell you what the picking means. Call your hospice team; they will tell you what the chart says. But I will tell you what to do: give the hands something to hold. A hand. A rolled washcloth. The corner of a blanket. A rosary, if that is your family. A wooden spoon, if that is your family. The hands are looking for a job. Give them one.

And then there is the last thing. The last thing in my notebook is not a phrase. It is a date. March 2015.

My mother. Doña Refugio. Cuca, we called her. She died in her own house with hospice, and I was on the other side of the bed for the first time in my life, the daughter and not the chaplain. And I knew everything. Thirty-four years, and I knew everything, and I still almost got it wrong.

She had stopped talking about three days before. And I sat there and I talked to her, because that is what I do, and I told her about the yard and the limonero and the children and the weather, and I did not ask her anything, because I did not want to make her work. And on the last afternoon I was quiet for a while. And she moved her fingers. Just her fingers, on the sheet. Like she was patting it.

And I did not know what that meant. Thirty-four years, four hundred bedsides, and my own mother patted the sheet and I did not know what it meant.

And then I remembered. When I was a little girl, in Analco, when I was sick, she would sit on the edge of my bed and pat the sheet next to my hand, like this, and she would say, "Ya, mija. Ya." Enough, mija. Enough.

She was telling me enough. She was telling me she was all right.

[whispering] I put my hand in hers and I said, "Ya, Mamá. Ya." And she stopped. And she went that night, around three in the morning, and Ernie was there, and Marisol was there, and the window was open and the limonero was moving, and it was quiet.

I am telling you that story because you are going to look for a sentence, and you may not get one. And I do not want you to think that means nothing happened. My mother did not say "I love you" on the last day. She patted the sheet. That was the sentence. It took me a week to hear it, and I had the notebook in my hand the whole time.

So here is what I want you to do, and then I will let you go.

Write it down. All of it. The phrase your parent repeats. The name they call. The corner of the room they look at. The thing their hands do. And when something comes out of your parent's mouth that does not fit the disease, write it down with the date and the time, because it will not come again, and because someday, maybe twenty years from now, somebody in your family is going to open that notebook and hear them.

That is the whole thing, honey. I have been in four hundred houses, and I have never once seen a family regret writing it down.
