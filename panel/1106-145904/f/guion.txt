Nine rules. That is all my mother used, and she dressed brides in Gion for forty years. She never once let a woman leave her chair with the top of her head flat.

I am sixty-eight. My hair is silver. My face has gone softer at the jaw, the way faces do. And people still stop me at the market to ask who cuts my hair. What they think the answer is, is never the answer.

Hair can take ten years off your face or put ten years on, and it is almost never the length or the price. It is the top of your head. It is the light in the three inches beside your face. That is the whole job. [emphatically]

The last of these nine is one I never heard a hairdresser say in nineteen years in California. By the end you will know what to change tonight, with what is already in your bathroom.

My mother was Fumiko Arai, a kitsuke-shi. She dressed brides and geiko in Gion, in the traditional way, for forty years. She died in 2009. Her comb is still in the tansu in my hallway, and I use it every night.

I grew up in Nishijin, the weaving neighborhood. Then I lived nineteen years in Sunnyvale, California. So I know both bathrooms: the big plastic bottle with the blue label, and my mother's small brown one. I am giving you the small one.

On the internet they tell you: go shorter, add layers, put in volume with a round brush and a lot of spray. My mother would have said the opposite, and she had fifty women in that chair every week. Keep the weight. Lift only the top. Never fight the hair you were given.

**One. Wash the scalp, not the hair.**

You wash your hair every morning with a good shampoo, and every year it looks thinner and duller, so you buy a better bottle. That is the trap.

In 1975 my mother dressed a bride in Gion. She set that hair on Wednesday and nobody washed it for three days. On the third morning the bride came in flat from sleep, and my mother did not wet it. She took a thin damp cloth and cleaned only the scalp, along the part, and put it up in eleven minutes. I was twelve. I remember that cloth.

After sixty the scalp makes less oil. Soap on the ends every day takes out what little there is, the hair lifts, it frizzes, and frizz on a woman our age reads as tired.

So: two washes a week, three in summer. Shampoo on the scalp only, and let the water carry it down. Warm, not hot, about ninety-five, like a bath for a baby. Conditioner from the ears down. Mine costs four dollars.

Tonight, look at the last two inches under the bathroom light. If it looks like a broom, you have been washing the ends.

**Two. Never let the top go flat. One inch, four minutes.**

This is the rule my mother would not bend, not for a bride, not for anyone. The top of the head is the roof. If the roof sags, nobody looks at the walls.

I learned it about myself. In 2016 my daughter Naomi had a birthday in Osaka, and somebody took a photo of me at the table. I looked exhausted. I was not. But my hair was flat on top and wide at the bottom, and my whole face was sliding downhill. My mother had a name for that shape. She called it the umbrella.

At thirty it stood up by itself. At sixty-five it lies down the second you take the towel off, and whatever shape it dries in, it keeps all day.

So here are the four minutes. Hair wet, a claw clip, three dollars at Daiso, and you clip the crown straight up. Then do your face. Then take the clip out and dry the part first, twenty seconds with your head upside down, then the rest. No spray, no round brush. It keeps the lift until you wash again.

Tonight, stand sideways at the mirror. Look at the line from your forehead to the back. If it is straight, you have the umbrella, and tomorrow you have four minutes of work.

**Before the third rule, do something with me. Ten seconds, nothing to buy.**

Put three fingers at the top of your head, right at the part, and push the hair straight up. Look at your face, not the hair. Then let it fall.

Same face. Different woman. The first one looks awake, the second one looks like she has been awake since five.

**Three. Never let the ends stop at the widest part of your jaw.**

You go to the salon and say, shoulder length, please, because it is safe and you have had it since 1998. The hairdresser agrees, because it is safe for her too. The ends stop exactly at the corner of your jaw, so now there is a horizontal line across the widest part of your face. Twice.

Mrs. Kinoshita lives across the street. She is eighty-one, she walks the Philosopher's Path every morning, ten thousand steps, in the same flat leather shoes of fifteen years. She has had the same haircut for twenty years and it still works. I asked her why. She said, because it is right. Her ends stop two fingers below the jaw.

The jaw softens after sixty, and the hair line sits on it like a ruler.

So when you sit in that chair, say this sentence: the ends at my chin, or two fingers below my jaw, never at the corner. It is the same twenty-dollar cut with one more sentence in it. If she argues, say it again, kindly.

Tonight, put one finger at the corner of your jaw and look where your ends stop. If they are on the same line, that is your answer.

**Four. The color stops half an inch from the scalp.**

I colored my hair for years, from Sunnyvale until I was sixty-one. A solid brown, to the scalp, every five weeks, because that is what we did. Then in 2019 I stopped, let it grow for eight months, and had it cut. My daughter saw me at New Year and said, Mama, you look like my mother again. Not younger. Like myself.

Silver is not for everyone and I am not telling you to stop. But look at the hairline. My mother left a soft millimeter of the woman's own hair showing and blended it. She said a hard edge at the skin is a line, and a line is a thing people look at instead of your eyes.

A solid block of dark at the scalp is the darkest thing in the frame, right above your face. It pulls the color out of your skin and hardens every line. It is not the color that is wrong. It is the wall of it.

So. If you color, ask for it to stop half an inch from the scalp and be blended at the root, so the light gets in. Or go one shade lighter than your natural, never darker. If you are ready to stop, let it grow two inches, then have it cut into a shape. A ten-dollar box is fine.

Tonight, take one photo with the flash on. Look at the top of your head. If it is one flat sheet of color, it is too solid.

**Five. Leave air in the bangs, and never cover the brows.**

Last spring my granddaughter Yui asked me to cut her bangs in the kitchen. Twenty-two, a student at Doshisha, very sure about everything. I cut them pointing up, in little points, half an inch above the eyebrows. She looked in the mirror and said, Obaachan, my eyes look bigger. Yes. That is the whole job.

Blunt bangs do the opposite. They make a helmet, they sit on the brows, and they put the eyes in shade. After sixty the brows get thinner on their own, so you are covering the two things that still open a face.

So ask for the bangs cut with the scissors vertical, point by point, so the edge is soft instead of a line. At home, cut them dry, in small snips, scissors pointing at the ceiling, and stop early. You can take more. You cannot put it back.

If you do not have bangs, do not start now. Pin the front back with one clip tonight and look. If your whole face lifts, the front was too low and too heavy.

**Six. One ear. Never both, never neither.**

This sounds like nothing. It is the one people notice without knowing they noticed.

All the hair behind both ears is a helmet. Hair down on both sides is a curtain, and a curtain makes a face wider and hides the one thing that still looks good on every woman I know: the line of the neck.

My mother always left one side clear, always the side where the light came in through the window in Gion. She said, one ear for the earring, one side for the face. She pinned the other side back under the hair, and the woman would look and say, oh, that is better. Every time. Forty years of every time.

Choose your side. Not the side you like, the side with the better jaw, and you will see it in two seconds. Then one small pin, two dollars, under the hair on that side, and the other side stays down.

Tonight, look at the last photo anybody took of you. Is there the same amount of hair on both sides of your face? If yes, put one side back and take the photo again.

**Seven. Dry it with a thin cloth. Press, do not rub.**

I have a stack of tenugui, thin cotton, three dollars at the market. Mr. Okada at Nishiki keeps them for me, since 2002, at the price he charged me that year.

A thin cloth takes the water out in ninety seconds, if you press it. You wrap it, you press with the flat of your hand, you move down. What most of us do is take a thick towel and rub the head like a dog, then a hot dryer for six minutes.

The rubbing is the problem, not the towel. Rub a strand between two fingers and feel it. Now imagine that every day for twenty years. The surface gets rough, rough hair does not catch light, and hair that does not catch light looks gray and flat even when the color is fine.

So. Thin cloth, press from the top down, ninety seconds. Then let it air-dry while you put on your face and your clothes, ten minutes, and it is seventy percent dry when you pick up the dryer. Three minutes on medium, head down.

Tonight, run the flat of your hand from the top of your head down. If it feels like a brush, you have been rubbing.

**Eight. One drop of oil. The last inch. At night.**

My mother kept a small brown bottle of camellia oil. Nine dollars, from a shop near the Kamogawa. She used it on the hair of the geiko in Gion, the last inch, at night. I have that bottle now. Six years on my own hair and I am still on the second one.

Here is what most people do. They put the oil near the scalp, where they think the dryness is. Then the top goes flat and dark, and they decide oil is not for them.

After sixty, the ends have been washed and dried and combed more than any other inch of your head. They get light and dry, they float, and floating ends on a still face is the whole picture of tired.

So. One drop, not three. In the palm, rub the hands together until it is warm and almost gone, then take the last inch between the palms and press. Only the last inch. Only at night, on dry hair.

Tonight, pinch the last inch between two fingers and pull gently. If it crackles, it is dry, and one drop tonight will fix it. If it does not crackle, leave it alone.

If this is useful, subscribe. Yui tells me the button is below. It helps me, and it costs you nothing.

**Nine. And this is the one nobody in America ever told me. Comb it before you wash it, never after.**

[whispering] I never heard this in nineteen years in California. Not from a hairdresser, not from a magazine, not from a friend.

Out of the shower with wet hair, we take a brush and pull it from the top down. And we wonder why it breaks and never gets any longer.

Wet hair stretches more than dry hair, and when it stretches it breaks, and where it breaks you get a short frizzy hair standing up. Multiply that by every wash, for twenty years, and you have the halo. She has not given up. She has been brushing wet hair.

My mother had a boxwood comb. Tsuge wood, warm in the hand, the teeth cut by hand. Mr. Okada found me one at Nishiki for about eight dollars. Before the shower, on dry hair, I comb from the ends up, a little at a time. Then I wash, my fingers do the rest. Once a week, twenty strokes from the nape up.

Tonight, before you get in the shower, comb it dry. Then notice, in there, how little you have to pull.

Nine rules. Two washes a week. Lift the top. Do not end at the jaw. Stop the color half an inch from the scalp. Leave air in the bangs. One ear. Press, do not rub. One drop at night. Comb it dry.

Not one of them costs more than a comb, and eight you can do tonight with what is already in your bathroom. My mother said you do not need to buy anything to look like yourself.

She used to say the face is the last thing you dress. I thought she meant lipstick. She meant this. You dress the face with the line of the hair around it, and then you go out.

So tonight, take the photo. Chest height, window light, straight on, no flash, and look at the top of your head. Not at your face. Then tell me in the comments, is there light up there, or is it flat? Yui reads them to me with my tea.

And next week, Carol lands in Kansai. April, the cherry trees on the Philosopher's Path, and a suitcase she packed alone for the first time in her life, at seventy. I will show you what she wore on the plane, and the six things I swapped before she left the airport. She does not know yet. Do not tell her.

Thank you for sitting with me. Go and comb your hair.
