My grandmother walked every day until she was ninety-eight. Every single day. Rain, snow, and those grey mornings when nobody wants to leave the house. And every one of those mornings started the same way: a wooden kitchen chair, a small silver radio, and a five-minute radio taiso warm-up we did together.

Her name was Fumiko. She lived in a small town north of Sendai, in a wooden house with a narrow garden and a screen door that stuck in the summer. She lived to ninety-eight, and she walked every one of those days. When she was ninety, she could still walk to the fish shop and back without stopping. People in the town knew her walk — small steps, one hand on her hip, one hand with the shopping bag. She never ran. She never hurried. She just went.

I taught physical education for thirty-two years. Boys and girls, middle school, on a school yard with more dust than grass. I taught them volleyball, running, jump rope, and I taught them to stretch before the bell. And after I retired, I taught the neighbor women in their seventies how to do these same movements while sitting on a chair. They came to my living room on Tuesdays. Some of them in their slippers.

[emphatically] So. This morning, I want to give you the five movements. Exactly five of them. Done on a chair. Five minutes. And at the end, I'll tell you what to look for after two weeks.

You don't need a gym. You don't need a mat. A kitchen chair is perfect. Strong chair, no wheels, sit on the front half of the seat. Feet flat, as wide as your hips. Back straight, like a string pulls the top of your head. Good.

Five movements. Number one: arms up. Number two: shoulder rolls. Number three: seated marching. Number four: ankle circles. Number five: deep breathing with a slow arm lift. That is the whole routine my grandmother did for sixty years. Nothing fancy. Nothing expensive. One chair, one radio, five minutes.

Let's begin with the first one. Arms up.

On my whiteboard: one, arms up. Ten times. Both arms rise slowly, up to the ceiling, and at the same time the heels lift from the floor. Only the heels. The toes stay down. Then arms down, heels down. Ten times. Slow. Like the string on your head is pulling up, and your hands just follow it.

Why does arms up matter? Because of the balance. When you reach up, the body finds where it is. The shoulders open and the chest opens with it. And the heels? The heels teach the ankles they still have a job. Standing balance, in a chair.

My grandmother did this one first, always first. Hands on her knees, look at the window, and up. She used to count with me in Japanese. Ichi. Ni. San. She never once missed ten.

Ten times. Count out loud with me. One. Two. Three. Slowly. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Good. Again tomorrow.

Movement number two. Shoulder rolls.

Sitting on the same chair. Hands rest on your thighs. Now lift the shoulders toward the ears, then roll them back, back, back, down. Ten times back. Then forward, ten times forward. Slowly, in big circles, like you are drawing a circle on the wall behind you with your elbow.

The shoulders are where I keep my mornings. When I was teaching, I carried a bag of exams in one hand every day for years, and my right shoulder sat up by my ear by Friday. I would rub it in the staff room, and the history teacher would say, "Kurosawa-sensei, again?" This is the movement that put it back down.

Why it matters: the neck, the tops of the shoulders, the inside of the shoulder blades. That is where the stiffness lives after a night in bed. Ten circles, and they open a little. The first day, maybe one centimeter. The two months, maybe more.

Ten back. Count with me. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Now forward, ten more. Slowly. Slowly is strong.

Movement number three. Seated marching.

Back straight, hands on the seat of the chair, or on your knees if that is easier. Now pick up one foot, set it down, pick up the other, set it down. Like a marching soldier, but sitting. Twenty steps in total. Ten on each side. Lift the knee a little. Not high. Little is fine.

Why marching in a chair? Because it wakes the hip. The hip is a big joint, and it likes to be used. When you lift the knee, the front of the hip opens, and the thigh does the work. And the circulation. The blood moves down to the feet and back up again.

When we were girls, my grandmother and I did this while waiting for the rice to finish cooking. She said, "March while the rice cooks, and the rice will not wait for you." She marched twenty steps in the kitchen, beside the pot.

Twenty steps. Count out loud. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty. Good.

Movement number four. Ankle circles.

Sit up straight. Lift one foot, just a little, off the floor. Now draw a circle with the toes. Like you are drawing a circle on a table with a pencil, and the pencil is your big toe. Ten circles one way, ten circles the other way. Then the other foot. Same. Gentle. Never fast.

Why the ankle? Because the ankle is the door to the ground. Every step you take starts at the ankle. If it is stiff, the walk is short and careful. Little circles open that door again.

My grandmother did this one last, always last, because of her ankles. Nobody had told her to. She just felt they were tight. She did them every morning for twenty years, and her ankles let her walk all the way to ninety-eight. On the bad days, I did them with her, one foot each, sitting side by side on that chair.

Then the other foot. Ten circles. Breathe slowly while you draw. And when you finish, rest the foot on the floor and shake it gently, like shaking water off your hand.

Movement number five. Deep breathing with a slow arm lift.

Sitting tall. Hands on your thighs. Now breathe in through the nose, slowly, and lift the arms out to the sides and up to the ceiling. At the top, hold for one slow breath. Then lower the arms as you breathe out. Five times. Only five. And each one slower than the last.

Why breathing? Because the body is not only the legs. When we breathe slowly, the whole body slows down with us. And a slow breath before you stand up makes the standing easier. It is like a small pause before the music.

My grandmother finished this way every morning for sixty years. Arms up, breathe in, arms down, breathe out. Then she would say, "Now the day can start." And she stood up, and she went out.

Five times. Count with me. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Then sit, and breathe. Good.

So. That is the routine. Five movements, five minutes, one chair.

Now, what to notice after two weeks. Not the first day. Not the second. Two weeks. The first thing most people notice is the getting up. You stand up from the chair, and it feels lighter. The second thing is the stairs. Going down is easier, because the ankles know what to do. The third thing, and this is my favorite, you stop thinking about it. You just do it in the morning, like brushing your teeth.

Write it down somewhere. A small notebook. A calendar on the wall. Mark the days with a red circle when you finish. My students' mothers did this in their notebooks, and they would show me, almost shy, like a school report.

If something hurts sharply, or if you feel dizzy, stop. That is not the routine. Ask your doctor before you begin, especially if you have had an operation or you are not sure about your balance. These movements are gentle, but they are yours, and it is fine to be careful.

One more thing before I finish. If you are enjoying these mornings with me, please press the like button, and subscribe, so we can keep doing them together. It helps this small class grow.

Let me show you the list one more time. One, arms up. Two, shoulder rolls. Three, seated marching. Four, ankle circles. Five, breathing. All five with a red circle next to them. That is the whole class.

A kitchen chair. Five minutes. Before your tea gets cold.
